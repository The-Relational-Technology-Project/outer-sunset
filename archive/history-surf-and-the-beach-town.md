This note covers Ocean Beach surf culture and the "beach town" Outer Sunset, from the first surfers of the 1940s to the shops and cafes on outer Judah, Irving and Noriega today. It also explains the fog season, the bonfire rules, and the 2012 Ocean Beach Master Plan.

## The first surfers at Kelly's Cove, Ocean Beach, in the 1940s

Kelly's Cove is the stretch of Ocean Beach just south of the Cliff House, at the north end of the beach, and it is where San Francisco surfing took hold. The National Park Service says young surfers began meeting there in the 1940s and that the sport grew after World War II. KALW interviewed Al Peace, who started surfing the cove in the early 1940s on a board that weighed 111 pounds. Accounts differ on the name. Some say it honors "Old Man Kelly," a hermit said to swim out to Seal Rock every day in the 1940s. Others say it came from a Kelly Tire billboard on the cliff east of the Great Highway. Surfing here was not new to the city, though: the Park Service notes a Hawaiian surfer, James Apu, gave a demonstration at the 1894 Midwinter Fair. Since 2003 the early Kelly's Cove crowd has held a reunion at the cove every September.

Sources: [National Park Service, Vestiges of Ocean Beach](https://home.nps.gov/goga/learn/historyculture/vestiges-ocean-beach.htm), [KALW Crosscurrents, The first surfers to brave Ocean Beach](https://www.kalw.org/show/crosscurrents/2019-09-30/the-first-surfers-to-brave-san-franciscos-ocean-beach).

## How Ocean Beach surfers stayed warm before wetsuits: tire fires, wool and short sessions

Before wetsuits, Ocean Beach surfers of the 1940s through the 1960s kept warm with wool sweaters, brandy and big fires on the sand. The water at Kelly's Cove averages in the low 50s Fahrenheit. Arne Jin An Wong, who began surfing the cove as a teenager in the 1960s, told KALW that a surfer could last about 45 minutes to an hour before freezing, and that the fire on the beach was what saved you afterward. The fires were often old tires, which burn hot and long. Tire fires were banned in 1972, the same year Playland closed next door. This is why fire and surfing are tied together in Ocean Beach memory, and why the September reunion at Kelly's Cove still ends at a fire, where people burn slips of paper bearing the names of surfers who have died.

Sources: [KALW Crosscurrents, The first surfers to brave Ocean Beach](https://www.kalw.org/show/crosscurrents/2019-09-30/the-first-surfers-to-brave-san-franciscos-ocean-beach), [Lloyd Kahn, Jack O'Neill, 1923-2017](https://www.lloydkahn.com/?p=381).

## Jack O'Neill's Surf Shop on the Great Highway, 1952, and the wetsuit

Jack O'Neill opened one of California's first surf shops on the Great Highway at Ocean Beach in 1952, and the O'Neill wetsuit company grew from it. He had been bodysurfing at Kelly's Cove and wanted to stay in the cold water longer. Accounts differ on the building: some call it a garage, and Lloyd Kahn, who knew the scene, describes a rented shack on a vacant lot. The exact address was not confirmed for this note. O'Neill is often called the inventor of the wetsuit, but that is doubtful. Published accounts credit Hugh Bradner, a UC Berkeley physicist, with inventing the neoprene wetsuit in 1952. Kahn writes that O'Neill bought an early suit at a San Francisco store, measured it to make a pattern, and returned it the next day. What O'Neill did was make neoprene suits for surfers and build a business on them. He moved to Santa Cruz in 1959 and died there on June 2, 2017, at 94.

Sources: [Wikipedia, Jack O'Neill (businessman)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jack_O%27Neill_(businessman)), [Lloyd Kahn, Jack O'Neill, 1923-2017](https://www.lloydkahn.com/?p=381), [Visit Santa Cruz, Remembering surfing pioneer Jack O'Neill](https://www.santacruz.org/blog/remembering-surfing-pioneer-jack-oneill/).

## Fleishhacker Pool by the zoo, its Hawaiian lifeguards, and why it closed in 1971

Fleishhacker Pool was a giant outdoor saltwater pool at the south end of Ocean Beach, next to the zoo. It opened April 22, 1925, measured 1,000 by 150 feet, and held 6.5 million gallons of seawater pumped from the ocean and warmed. It was so big that lifeguards patrolled in rowboats. Two of its head lifeguards, Eddie Ukini and Clifford Kamaka, were Hawaiian, and Jack O'Neill later said they were among the first people surfing Ocean Beach. The pool closed in 1971. After years of thin budgets and poor upkeep, a storm that January damaged its drain pipe, and the city could not afford the repair. A switch to fresh water went badly and the pool shut by the end of the year. It was later filled with rock and gravel and is now a zoo parking lot. The pool house burned on December 1, 2012. A fragment with three ornate entrances remains.

Sources: [Wikipedia, Fleishhacker Pool](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fleishhacker_Pool), [Stab Magazine, Ghosts of the Great Highway](https://stabmag.com/style/ghosts-of-the-great-highway/).

## Playland at the Beach closes on Labor Day 1972

Playland at the Beach was the amusement park along the Great Highway at Ocean Beach, about 10 acres covering several blocks. It grew out of Chutes at the Beach, which the Whitney brothers bought and renamed in 1926. By the late 1960s it was run down. Local News Matters lists the reasons given: neglected upkeep, falling attendance, bad publicity, television, and cleaner new parks like Disneyland. Columnist Herb Caen thought it lost its heart when the Big Dipper roller coaster came down in 1955. A developer, Jeremy Ets-Hokin, proposed housing and shops on the site. The Board of Supervisors approved his plan in July 1972, and Playland closed for good on September 4, 1972, Labor Day. Its rides and signs were auctioned and the park was razed. Residential buildings stand there now. A Laffing Sal, the cackling figure from the Fun House, can still be seen at the Musée Mécanique at Fisherman's Wharf.

Sources: [Local News Matters, The rise and fall of Playland at the Beach](https://localnewsmatters.org/2018/09/07/the-rise-and-fall-of-playland-at-the-beach/), [National Park Service, Vestiges of Ocean Beach](https://home.nps.gov/goga/learn/historyculture/vestiges-ocean-beach.htm).

## Wise Surfboards, the Ocean Beach surf shop from 1968 to 2019

Wise Surfboards was an Ocean Beach surf shop for half a century, and one of the first in the city. Bob Wise opened it in 1968 in a small shop on Wawona Street, where he shaped and glassed custom boards himself. It grew into a three-story store at 800 Great Highway, facing Ocean Beach, selling boards and wetsuits from dozens of makers. Before surf forecasts moved online, surfers could phone the shop to hear Wise's recorded daily report on Ocean Beach, which usually began with the words "For what it's worth." The shop closed in late 2019. An employee said business had been down, and Wise had told Surfer Magazine back in 2011 that the internet was wiping shops like his off the map. Bob Wise died in late 2022 at age 74.

Sources: [SFist, Longtime Ocean Beach surf shop owner Bob Wise dies](https://sfist.com/2023/01/05/longtime-ocean-beach-surf-shop-owner-bob-wise-dies-at-age).

## The Doggie Diner head on Sloat Boulevard near 45th Avenue

The Doggie Diner head is the seven-foot fiberglass dachshund in a chef's hat and polka-dot tie that stands on a pole in the median of Sloat Boulevard at 45th Avenue. Graphic artist Harold Bachman designed it in 1966 as the mascot of Doggie Diner, a local fast-food chain that no longer exists. For decades this head stood outside the last Doggie Diner, later the Carousel restaurant, at 46th and Sloat. When the restaurant closed, neighbors and preservationists campaigned to save the sign, and in 2000 the Board of Supervisors had Public Works take it over. In April 2001 a 45 mile-per-hour windstorm knocked it down and broke its nose. It was repaired, moved to the median just east of its old spot, and welcomed with a party on Valentine's Day 2005. In 2006 the city made it San Francisco Landmark No. 254. Public Works took it down for cleaning and paint in January 2014.

Sources: [SF Public Works, City's iconic Doggie Diner head getting much-needed TLC](https://sfpublicworks.org/node/34596).

## Other Avenues, the worker-owned food co-op on Judah since 1974

Other Avenues, at 3930 Judah Street, is San Francisco's oldest grocery cooperative. It grew out of the Food Conspiracy, a late-1960s movement in which neighbors pooled money to buy food wholesale and split it up. The store was established in 1974 and opened its doors at 4035 Judah on January 1, 1975, staffed at first by volunteers. It moved to its present address in 1982. In the late 1990s it nearly went under, and Outer Sunset residents raised money to save it. In 1999 it became a worker cooperative with no bosses, and its 18 full-time worker-owners each have an equal say. In 2008 the collective bought the building with help from neighbors, which gave it stability, and in the following decade it added rooftop solar panels to cut its power bills. Other Avenues is a city Legacy Business.

Sources: [SF Examiner, Other Avenues remains Outer Sunset staple after 50 years](https://www.sfexaminer.com/news/community/other-avenues-remains-outer-sunset-staple-after-50-years/article_658f194f-9680-47a8-a54e-9e6bc79d65d4.html).

## Trouble Coffee on outer Judah, 2007, and the cinnamon toast that started a craze

Trouble Coffee opened in 2007 at 4033 Judah Street, about three blocks from the ocean, in what had been a dog grooming shop on a bleak stretch of the street. Owner Giulietta Carrelli started it with $1,000 borrowed from friends. Its menu was famously short: coffee, young coconuts served with a straw and spoon, grapefruit juice, and thick slices of white toast with butter, cinnamon and sugar. Carrelli told Pacific Standard magazine in 2014 that toast was her childhood comfort food and that nobody can be mad at toast. The magazine traced San Francisco's much-mocked "$4 toast" trend of 2013 back to her counter, with other cafes copying the idea. A 2018 neighborhood guide called Trouble one of the first businesses to set up on that part of Judah. This note could not open a source confirming what has happened to the cafe since, so check before you walk over.

Sources: [Pacific Standard, A Toast Story (January 2014)](https://psmag.com/social-justice/toast-story-latest-artisanal-food-craze-72676/), [Tinybeans, Neighborhood guide: the Outer Sunset (April 2018)](https://tinybeans.com/san-francisco/neighborhood-guide-the-outer-sunset/).

## The new shops, cafes and makers of outer Judah, Irving and Noriega in the 2000s and 2010s

From the mid-2000s a cluster of small, hand-built businesses changed the blocks nearest the beach. By 2018 a neighborhood guide could list them: Mollusk Surf Shop at 4500 Irving, which it called the surf shop that made the Outer Sunset cool; Outerlands restaurant at 4001 Judah; General Store at 4035 Judah (the address where Other Avenues first opened in 1975); Andytown Coffee Roasters at 3655 Lawton and two shops on Taraval; and Devil's Teeth Baking Company at 3876 Noriega. Artists and makers set up alongside them: 3 Fish Studios, a print shop at 4541 Irving; Case for Making, an art supply store at 4037 Judah; and Woodshop at 3725 Noriega, a workshop started by three surfing fathers. Pacific Standard magazine described this part of Judah as a bleak commercial stretch when Trouble Coffee arrived in 2007, so these shops filled quiet storefronts. Opening years often quoted for these shops could not be confirmed in sources opened for this note, and some addresses may have changed since 2018.

Sources: [Tinybeans, Neighborhood guide: the Outer Sunset (April 2018)](https://tinybeans.com/san-francisco/neighborhood-guide-the-outer-sunset/), [SF Examiner, Other Avenues remains Outer Sunset staple after 50 years](https://www.sfexaminer.com/news/community/other-avenues-remains-outer-sunset-staple-after-50-years/article_658f194f-9680-47a8-a54e-9e6bc79d65d4.html), [Pacific Standard, A Toast Story (January 2014)](https://psmag.com/social-justice/toast-story-latest-artisanal-food-craze-72676/).

## Where the name "Outside Lands" comes from

"Outside Lands" is the 19th century name for the west side of San Francisco, the land that is now the Richmond, the Sunset and Golden Gate Park. It was mostly sand dunes, and people in the young city thought of it as out of reach and unfit to live on. The Outside Lands music festival, first held in Golden Gate Park on August 22 to 24, 2008, took its name from that history. Its producers, Another Planet Entertainment, Superfly and Starr Hill Presents, spent more than three years getting city permits, and before the first festival they sent notices to 28,000 nearby households. Attendance was about 130,000 the first year and has been 200,000 or more in most years since 2012. In 2019 the Board of Supervisors voted unanimously to renew its permit for ten years. The Western Neighborhoods Project, the west side's history group, also uses the name for its magazine and website.

Sources: [Wikipedia, Outside Lands (festival)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Outside_Lands_(festival)), [Wikipedia, Outside Lands](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Outside_Lands).

## Why the Outer Sunset is so foggy, and when fog season is

Fog season in the Outer Sunset is summer, and it peaks in August, which is why people say "Fogust" (after May Gray, June Gloom and No-Sky July). Bay Nature explains the cause. In summer the strongest north winds of the year blow along the coast and drive upwelling, which brings the coldest water of the year to the surface. Moist air over that cold water forms a cool, wet layer called the marine layer, and where it meets the year's warmest air, it condenses into fog. When the layer is 500 feet deep or less, fog sits right along the coastline, where the Outer Sunset is. At 1,000 to 2,000 feet it covers the bay. It tends to form overnight and last to late morning. The fog is useful: redwoods get 30 to 40 percent of their yearly water from it. The summer of 2025 was unusually foggy and ran a few degrees cooler than average in parts of the Bay Area.

Sources: [Bay Nature, Ode to summer fog (Summer 2026)](https://baynature.org/magazine/summer2026/ode-to-summer-fog/).

## Ocean Beach fire pits and the bonfire rules

Bonfires are legal on Ocean Beach only in the 16 metal fire rings the National Park Service sets out between Stairwells 15 and 20, and only from March 1 through October 31. From November 1 to the end of February no fires are allowed, matching the Bay Area's winter Spare the Air season. Fires must be out by 9:30 p.m. and must be put out with water, not buried in sand, which does not put a fire out. Groups of 25 or more need a permit. The Park Service also bans fires on summer Spare the Air days. Do not build your own ring from rocks or logs. Fines run up to $5,000. Beach fires here are older than the park: they were a tradition before Ocean Beach joined the Golden Gate National Recreation Area in 1975. After trial programs in 2007 and 2014, the current rules took effect April 4, 2016. They raised the number of rings from 12 to 16.

Sources: [National Park Service, Ocean Beach Fire Program](https://www.nps.gov/articles/ocean-beach-fire-program.htm), [National Park Service, Ocean Beach](https://www.nps.gov/goga/planyourvisit/oceanbeach.htm).

## The Ocean Beach Master Plan (SPUR, 2012) and what it set in motion

The Ocean Beach Master Plan is a long-range plan for the whole 3.5 miles of beach, published in May 2012 by SPUR, a planning nonprofit, after a process it began in 2010 with city, state and federal agencies and neighbors. The trigger was erosion at the south end, where winter storms were eating the bluff below the Great Highway and threatening the Lake Merced Tunnel, a large sewer pipe. In 2011 the Coastal Commission had refused to approve more of the city's boulder walls. The plan made six recommendations: reroute the Great Highway behind the zoo by way of Sloat and Skyline; build a new system of coastal protection, restoration and access at the south end; narrow the Great Highway in the middle stretch; restore the dunes there; connect Golden Gate Park to the beach better; and improve walking and biking north of Balboa (done in 2014). SPUR later described Proposition K, the 2024 measure that closed the Upper Great Highway to cars, as growing out of this plan.

Sources: [SPUR, Ocean Beach Master Plan (May 2012)](https://spur.org/publications/spur-report/2012-05-21/ocean-beach-master-plan), [SPUR, California's largest pedestrian project expands the vision of a SPUR-led climate adaptation plan](https://www.spur.org/news/2024-12-09/californias-largest-pedestrian-project-expands-vision-spur-led-climate-adaptation), [National Park Service, Ocean Beach](https://www.nps.gov/goga/planyourvisit/oceanbeach.htm).
