This note covers the birds an Outer Sunset neighbor sees and hears from the sidewalk, the yard, the west end of Golden Gate Park, Ocean Beach and Lake Merced. It explains why ravens and crows have multiplied, what happened to the quail and the local sparrow songs, when migrants come through, and where to go birding or join a count.

## Ravens and crows in San Francisco: the numbers from the Christmas Bird Count

Common ravens (Corvus corax) and American crows (Corvus brachyrhynchos) are now everyday birds on Sunset rooftops and wires, but both are newcomers in these numbers. The 1930 Handbook of Birds of Golden Gate Park said no raven had ever been reported in the park and called crows extremely rare.

The San Francisco Christmas Bird Count, run by Golden Gate Bird Alliance, tracks the change. Counters found 5 American crows in 1984. They found 1,532 in December 2024 and 3,260 on December 29, 2025, an all-time high. Ravens set their own record in the 2024 count at 1,538, slightly more than the crows that year.

Telling them apart: the crow is the smaller of the two. The raven nests on cliffs or in tall trees starting in mid-February, according to the National Park Service. Counts swing from year to year with weather and the number of volunteers, so the long trend matters more than any single jump.

Sources: [Golden Gate Bird Alliance, SF Christmas Bird Count 2026 post](https://goldengatebirdalliance.org/blog-posts/sf-christmas-bird-count-2026/), [Golden Gate Bird Alliance, 2024 SF Christmas Bird Count Recap](https://goldengatebirdalliance.org/blog-posts/2024-sf-christmas-bird-count-recap/), [SF Environment, The changing face of avian diversity](https://www.sfenvironment.org/blog/changing-face-avian-diversity), [National Park Service, Common Raven](https://www.nps.gov/prsf/learn/nature/common-raven.htm).

## Why there are so many crows and ravens now, and what that means for other birds

Crows and ravens have increased in San Francisco because the city feeds and shelters them. KQED's reporting in February 2026 gives the reasons biologists offer: garbage, fast-food scraps, roadkill and lawns supply food all year; crows are hunted in farm country but not in town; they have few predators; and they learn fast and teach each other. In winter, mostly December through February, crows gather at dusk in big shared roosts, then split into pairs to nest in spring.

What this does to other birds is less settled. Crows and ravens do eat eggs and nestlings. Glenn Phillips of Golden Gate Bird Alliance told KQED there is no evidence so far that crows are driving other species down, and crow researcher Kaeli Swift said studies do not show crows wiping out songbirds. The exception both name is rare ground-nesting and beach birds. Golden Gate Bird Alliance's summer 2026 newsletter says growing crow numbers put new pressure on the snowy plovers at Ocean Beach.

Sources: [KQED, Is it crow-maggedon?](https://www.kqed.org/news/12072433/is-it-crow-maggedon-why-crows-are-flocking-to-bay-area-cities-each-winter), [Golden Gate Bird Alliance, The Gull, Summer 2026](https://goldengatebirdalliance.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/07/The_Gull_Summer_2026_FINAL-1.pdf).

## Red-tailed, red-shouldered and Cooper's hawks over the Sunset

The hawks circling over the avenues are mostly red-tailed hawks (Buteo jamaicensis). The other common large hawk is the red-shouldered hawk (Buteo lineatus), a bird of wooded, wet places. Both were nearly impossible to find in San Francisco in 1930, because a hunter employed by Golden Gate Park shot birds then considered pests. Both are common now. The National Park Service lists each as a year-round breeder in the Presidio: red-shouldered hawks nest from about February through July and hunt from perches near wet ground, and red-tailed hawks nest March through July and hunt open grass early and late in the day.

Near the Sunset, Golden Gate Bird Alliance notes red-shouldered hawks nesting in the eucalyptus at Sunset Circle on Lake Merced. Cooper's hawk (Accipiter cooperii) was once rare in the city. SF Environment reported in 2011 that it was nesting in San Francisco again, since it is no longer shot on sight.

Sources: [SF Environment, The changing face of avian diversity](https://www.sfenvironment.org/blog/changing-face-avian-diversity), [National Park Service, Red-shouldered Hawk](https://www.nps.gov/prsf/learn/nature/red-shouldered-hawk.htm), [National Park Service, Red-tailed Hawk](https://www.nps.gov/prsf/learn/nature/red-tailed-hawk.htm), [Golden Gate Bird Alliance, Lake Merced: San Francisco's Birding Jewel](https://goldengatebirdalliance.org/blog-posts/lake-merced-san-franciscos-birding-jewel/).

## Great horned owls, barn owls and peregrine falcons

Great horned owls (Bubo virginianus) nest at the west end of Golden Gate Park. A 2015 account described a group that had nested for several years in the pines near the bison paddock. They start early, in the middle of winter, and the National Park Service gives February through July as the breeding season. SF Environment's David Assmann wrote in 2011 that he saw six great horned owls in one afternoon on Strawberry Hill, more than all the San Francisco Christmas Bird Counts of the first half of the 1900s recorded together.

Barn owls were also returning by 2011, when three nests with at least 11 owls were found at the Exploratorium. The sources opened for this note did not give a barn owl nest site in Golden Gate Park.

Peregrine falcons came back after the DDT era. The first Bay Area pair appeared on the Bay Bridge in 1983, and a pair first used a nest tray on the PG&E building at 77 Beale Street in 2003.

Sources: [From the Thicket, Getting to know the great horned owls of Golden Gate Park](https://fromthethicket.com/2015/11/24/getting-to-know-the-great-horned-owls-of-golden-gate-park-hoodline/), [National Park Service, Great Horned Owl](https://www.nps.gov/prsf/learn/nature/great-horned-owl.htm), [SF Environment, The changing face of avian diversity](https://www.sfenvironment.org/blog/changing-face-avian-diversity), [Golden Gate Bird Alliance, Falcon drama in downtown San Francisco](https://goldengatebirdalliance.org/blog-posts/falcon-drama-in-downtown-san-francisco/).

## The white-crowned sparrow and its San Francisco song dialects

The white-crowned sparrow (Zonotrichia leucophrys) is the small sparrow with the black and white striped crown that feeds on open ground near shrubs. Young birds learn their song early in life, and the songs come in local dialects.

San Francisco's dialects are among the best studied anywhere. David Luther and Luis Baptista compared recordings made in the city in 1969 and 1970, 1990 and 1998. There had been three dialects: one around the Presidio, one in the scrub at Lake Merced, and a "San Francisco" dialect in the gardens, yards and parks in between, which includes the Sunset. By 1998 the Presidio dialect was gone, the Lake Merced dialect had shrunk, and the San Francisco dialect had spread and blended with the others. Over those thirty years the lowest notes of the surviving songs rose in pitch. The authors' explanation is traffic noise, which drowns out low notes.

In the Christmas Bird Count, which also includes wintering migrants, 2,552 white-crowned sparrows were counted in 2025, down from 4,583 in 2020.

Sources: [JSTOR Daily, A noisy city affects birdsong](https://daily.jstor.org/a-noisy-city-affects-birdsong/), [Golden Gate Bird Alliance, SF Christmas Bird Count 2026 post](https://goldengatebirdalliance.org/blog-posts/sf-christmas-bird-count-2026/).

## What the 2020 shutdown did to white-crowned sparrow song

During the spring 2020 shelter-in-place order, San Francisco's white-crowned sparrows changed their song. Elizabeth Derryberry and colleagues had recorded the birds in spring 2015 at sites in the city and in rural Marin County. They went back to the same sites in April and May 2020 and published the comparison in the journal Science in September 2020.

With traffic gone, Derryberry said, city noise fell to roughly 1950s levels. City sparrows sang more quietly and dropped the low end of their songs back down while keeping the middle and high notes. Because there was less noise to cut through, the songs carried about twice as far. Her earlier work had shown that San Francisco sparrows had raised their pitch over the decades to be heard over the city.

One caution from an outside scientist, Jeffrey Podos: adult sparrows mostly keep the song they learned when young, so the change may reflect which birds held territories, not individual birds retuning. What the change meant for the sparrow population is not known.

Sources: [Smithsonian Magazine, September 2020](https://www.smithsonianmag.com/science-nature/pandemic-shutdown-san-francisco-had-sparrows-singing-sexier-tunes-180975913/).

## Anna's and Allen's hummingbirds in Sunset yards

Two hummingbirds use Sunset yards. Anna's hummingbird (Calypte anna) lives in San Francisco all year and is the one a neighbor is most likely to see. It is about four inches long, green and gray, and the male's throat and crown flash red in the right light. In the December 2024 Christmas Bird Count, 1,343 were counted and it was the most widespread species, on 245 of 353 checklists. In December 2025 it was on 242 checklists.

Allen's hummingbird (Selasphorus sasin) is smaller, green and orange, and only here for its breeding season. SF Environment's 2017 account says it arrives around mid-February, among the first spring migrants, and is among the first to leave. At El Polin Spring in the Presidio one June, 14 were counted in mid-month and none a week later. Allen's is the more aggressive of the two and chases Anna's off good flowers.

Sources: [SF Environment blog, January 2017](https://www.sfenvironment.org/node/2915), [Golden Gate Bird Alliance, 2024 SF Christmas Bird Count Recap](https://goldengatebirdalliance.org/blog-posts/2024-sf-christmas-bird-count-recap/), [Golden Gate Bird Alliance, SF Christmas Bird Count 2026 post](https://goldengatebirdalliance.org/blog-posts/sf-christmas-bird-count-2026/).

## California quail, the city's official bird, and how it vanished from Golden Gate Park

The California quail (Callipepla californica) is San Francisco's official bird and is gone from the city. Around 1930 there were more than 1,200 in Golden Gate Park alone. Bay Nature traces the losses place by place: Ocean Beach in the 1970s, Bernal Heights in the 1980s, Lake Merced and Lands End in the 1990s, the Presidio around 2006 to 2008. The last holdout was the San Francisco Botanical Garden in Golden Gate Park. Accounts differ on the end date. Golden Gate Bird Alliance says the last male was heard calling in 2017, and Bay Nature says the last wild quail vanished by 2018.

The causes named are outdoor cats, loss and isolation of shrubby habitat, cars, and inbreeding once numbers were small. The Presidio Trust and the San Francisco Estuary Institute plan a comeback. As of summer 2026, a pilot release of 50 wild quail from Point Reyes at two Presidio sites was scheduled for fall 2026. Researchers think returning coyotes, which thin out cats and raccoons, improve the odds.

Sources: [Bay Nature, The Comeback Quail](https://baynature.org/?p=221258), [SF Environment, The changing face of avian diversity](https://www.sfenvironment.org/blog/changing-face-avian-diversity), [Golden Gate Bird Alliance, The Gull, Summer 2026](https://goldengatebirdalliance.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/07/The_Gull_Summer_2026_FINAL-1.pdf), [Presidio Trust press release](https://presidio.gov/about/press/can-san-franciscos-official-bird-be-brought-back-to-the-city).

## Great blue herons nesting in Golden Gate Park and at Lake Merced

Great blue herons (Ardea herodias) have nested at Stow Lake in Golden Gate Park since at least 1993. Nancy DeStefanis of San Francisco Nature Education found the first nesting pair that June. Most years since have had two or more nests, and in several years seven or eight. The birds have used two islands, one by the boathouse and one by the waterfall. The group's count of chicks fledged stood at 259 in one tally and 270 by spring 2023.

That is a recovery. In 1930 herons and egrets were almost impossible to find in Golden Gate Park, after decades of hunting for hat plumes.

San Francisco Nature Education runs Heron Watch on spring Saturdays, with spotting scopes and volunteers at the lake. In 2023 it ran April 15 through May 20, 10 a.m. to 1 p.m.

Closer to the Sunset, Golden Gate Bird Alliance reports great blue herons and double-crested cormorants nesting in the eucalyptus near Harding Park at Lake Merced. Herons also walk lawns hunting gophers.

Sources: [San Francisco Nature Education, History of the Stow Lake heron colony](https://www.sfnature.org/history-of-the-stow-lake-heron-colony.html), [Richmond Review and Sunset Beacon, March 2023](https://richmondsunsetnews.com/2023/03/22/great-blue-herons-return-to-nests-at-stow-lake/), [SF Environment, The changing face of avian diversity](https://www.sfenvironment.org/blog/changing-face-avian-diversity), [Golden Gate Bird Alliance, Lake Merced](https://goldengatebirdalliance.org/blog-posts/lake-merced-san-franciscos-birding-jewel/), [Bay Nature, The Gopher Underground](https://baynature.org/?p=70174).

## The small yard birds: phoebes, towhees, juncos, chickadees, nuthatches, finches and blackbirds

Seven small birds make up most of what a Sunset neighbor sees in the yard. The details here come from National Park Service pages for the Presidio, the nearest place with published status for each.

The black phoebe is an insect-catcher that perches on poles and wires and builds a mud nest on buildings, cliffs and bridges, March through August. The California towhee is the plain brown, long-tailed bird scratching under shrubs. Almost none were recorded in San Francisco in the early 1900s, and more than 300 are now counted each Christmas, a gain SF Environment credits to planted yards and parks. The dark-eyed junco nests on the ground near cover, April through August. The chestnut-backed chickadee nests in tree cavities from mid-March. The pygmy nuthatch lives in older pines and cypress and nests in holes in dead wood. A University of San Francisco class counted 15 in Golden Gate Park in one morning in 2023. House finches and Brewer's blackbirds are both listed as abundant year-round.

Sources: [National Park Service, Black Phoebe](https://www.nps.gov/prsf/learn/nature/black-phoebe.htm), [National Park Service, California Towhee](https://www.nps.gov/prsf/learn/nature/california-towhee.htm), [National Park Service, Dark-eyed Junco](https://www.nps.gov/prsf/learn/nature/dark-eyed-junco.htm), [National Park Service, Chestnut-backed Chickadee](https://www.nps.gov/prsf/learn/nature/chestnut-backed-chickadee.htm), [National Park Service, Pygmy Nuthatch](https://www.nps.gov/prsf/learn/nature/pygmy-nuthatch.htm), [National Park Service, House Finch](https://www.nps.gov/prsf/learn/nature/house-finch.htm), [National Park Service, Brewer's Blackbird](https://www.nps.gov/prsf/learn/nature/brewers-blackbird.htm), [SF Environment, The changing face of avian diversity](https://www.sfenvironment.org/blog/changing-face-avian-diversity), [USF Ornithology blog, Golden Gate Park](https://usfblogs.usfca.edu/0rnith0l0gy/?p=25).

## The wild parrots of San Francisco, and whether they reach the Sunset

San Francisco's wild parrots are cherry-headed conures, also called red-masked parakeets, with some mitred conure hybrids mixed in. They come from Ecuador and Peru. The flock descends from pet-trade birds that escaped or were let go, and the United States banned the wild bird trade in 1992. SF Environment put the flock at more than 200 birds in 2011. The parrot rescue group Mickaboo later estimated more than 300 conures in the Bay Area.

The published accounts place them on the east and north sides of the city and down the Peninsula: Telegraph Hill, the Embarcadero and Ferry Building, Mission Dolores, and south to Brisbane and Sunnyvale. None of the sources opened for this note records a parrot flock in the Outer Sunset. A neighbor who does see parrots west of 19th Avenue has something worth logging on eBird or iNaturalist.

Sources: [KQED, Where did the wild parrots of San Francisco come from?](https://www.kqed.org/news/11185731/where-did-the-wild-parrots-of-san-francisco-come-from), [SF Environment, The changing face of avian diversity](https://www.sfenvironment.org/blog/changing-face-avian-diversity).

## Migration seasons, and why odd birds turn up at the west end of Golden Gate Park

Fall migration in San Francisco peaks in September and October. SF Environment explains the pattern: birds from Alaska, British Columbia and the Northwest head for Mexico and beyond, and songbirds that fly at night drop into the nearest good cover once they have crossed water. Townsend's and yellow-rumped warblers winter here. Orange-crowned, Nashville, black-throated gray, hermit and MacGillivray's warblers pass through.

The west end of Golden Gate Park, with North, Middle and South lakes (the Chain of Lakes) and thick trees a few blocks from the ocean, offers that cover. North Lake has long been described as a popular birding spot, with herons, ducks, kingfishers and egrets. A University of San Francisco class there in October 2022 logged Townsend's warbler, pygmy nuthatch, red-shouldered hawk and belted kingfisher. Christmas counts turn up strays too: a rock wren at the Polo Fields in 2024, and in 2025 a Grace's warbler back for a second winter and a rufous-crowned sparrow, last recorded on the count in 1987.

Sources: [SF Environment, Fall migration](https://www.sfenvironment.org/blog/fall-migration), [USF Ornithology blog, Golden Gate Park Chain of Lakes](https://usfblogs.usfca.edu/ornithology/?p=120), [Greenbelt Alliance, West end of Golden Gate Park](https://www.greenbelt.org/blog/urban-outings-west-end-of-golden-gate-park/), [Golden Gate Bird Alliance, 2024 recap](https://goldengatebirdalliance.org/blog-posts/2024-sf-christmas-bird-count-recap/), [Golden Gate Bird Alliance, 2026 post](https://goldengatebirdalliance.org/blog-posts/sf-christmas-bird-count-2026/).

## Window strikes, night lights and migrating birds

Glass and lights kill migrating birds. The San Francisco Planning Department cites estimates of up to a billion birds a year killed in window collisions in North America. In 2011 San Francisco adopted Standards for Bird-Safe Buildings (Ordinance 199-11), which set requirements for the most hazardous situations in new construction, along with a checklist and voluntary measures on glass treatment and lighting.

More than 250 species migrate through the Bay region. Golden Gate Bird Alliance's Lights Out for Birds asks people to turn off or shade non-essential lights from dusk to dawn in two seasons: February 15 to May 30 and August 15 to November 30. The request is aimed at tall buildings and the people who work in them. Neighbors can do the same at home: close blinds and switch off decorative outdoor lights on those nights. The group cites a Field Museum study in Chicago in which turning off one high-rise's lights cut bird deaths there by 80 percent.

Sources: [San Francisco Planning, Standards for Bird-Safe Buildings](https://sfplanning.org/standards-bird-safe-buildings), [Golden Gate Bird Alliance, Lights Out San Francisco](https://goldengatebirdalliance.org/conservation/make-the-city-safe-for-wildlife/learn-about-lights-out-san-francisco/).

## Where to go birding near the Outer Sunset, and counts neighbors can join

Lake Merced is the best birding spot near the Outer Sunset. With 267 species on eBird as of 2020, it ranked second among San Francisco hotspots outside the Farallones. Try the concrete bridge, the wooden bridge and pier at Sunset Circle, and the boathouse side at Harding Park. Western and Clark's grebes breed on the lake, and soras and Virginia rails are among the wintering birds.

Ocean Beach has sanderlings, willets and, from about July to May, the threatened western snowy plover on the dry sand between Stairwell 21 and Sloat Boulevard. Walk on the wet sand there. In Golden Gate Park, start at North Lake and work south along the Chain of Lakes.

To take part: the San Francisco Christmas Bird Count is next on Monday, December 28, 2026. In 2025 it drew 214 birders, who found 183 species and 94,315 birds. Golden Gate Bird Alliance also leads free field trips. eBird, run by the Cornell Lab of Ornithology and Audubon, takes sightings any day.

Sources: [Golden Gate Bird Alliance, Lake Merced](https://goldengatebirdalliance.org/blog-posts/lake-merced-san-franciscos-birding-jewel/), [National Park Service, Protecting the Snowy Plover brochure](https://www.nps.gov/goga/planyourvisit/upload/sb-snowy_plover_web.pdf), [Golden Gate Bird Alliance, The Gull, Summer 2026](https://goldengatebirdalliance.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/07/The_Gull_Summer_2026_FINAL-1.pdf), [Golden Gate Bird Alliance, SF Christmas Bird Count 2026 post](https://goldengatebirdalliance.org/blog-posts/sf-christmas-bird-count-2026/), [Golden Gate Bird Alliance, Bird with us](https://goldengatebirdalliance.org/birding-resources/birding-information/).
