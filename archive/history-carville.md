Carville was a settlement of retired horsecars and cable cars turned into clubhouses, cafes and homes in the dunes just south of Golden Gate Park, from 1895 into the 1920s. This note covers how it started, who lived there, what the cars cost, how it ended, and what is left to see today. Where the sources disagree, the note says so.

## What Carville-by-the-Sea was and where it stood in today's Outer Sunset

Carville, also called Carville-by-the-Sea or Cartown, was a beach community built from cast-off horsecars and cable cars. It began in 1895 on one block owned by Adolph Sutro, the block bounded today by Lincoln Way, Irving Street, 48th Avenue and La Playa (then 49th Avenue). That was the end of the Park and Ocean rail line, which stopped at about 47th Avenue and Lincoln Way, so day trippers walked right past it. Over the next fifteen years the car-houses spread south along the Great Highway, La Playa, 48th, 47th and 46th Avenues as far as Moraga Street. A well-known 1905 photograph shows car-houses at 48th Avenue and Judah Street, where the N Judah turns around now. By about 1903 newer residents were calling the same blocks Oceanside. One National Park Service page places Carville in the Outer Richmond, but every block it names is in the Outer Sunset.

## How Carville started in 1895: the Market Street Railway sells its old horsecars for $10 and $20

Carville exists because San Francisco's transit companies had more old cars than they could use. An 1893 merger put most of the city's horsecar lines under the Market Street Railway, which then switched them to electric streetcars. In 1895 the company advertised the leftover horsecar bodies: $20 with seats, $10 without. Historian Woody LaBounty dates one such ad to February 12, 1895. The ad suggested uses like newsstands, lunch stands, playhouses, tool sheds and chicken houses, and buyers did all of that. In September 1895 the Examiner found cars working as a real estate office, a shoemaker's shop, backyard playhouses and a "coffee saloon" at the beach. One early builder was Charles Stahl, a gripman on the Ellis Street line. In 1895 he joined three North Beach and Mission horsecars, bought for $45, on stilts above the sand at today's 20th Avenue between Judah and Kirkham, and moved his family in.

## Colonel Charles Dailey, Adolph Sutro and the first Carville coffee saloon, the Annex

Colonel Charles Dailey is called the founder of Carville, though he never lived in a car. Sources describe him as a Civil War veteran and former government agent in Arizona Territory, in poor health, and a friend of Adolph Sutro. (Columnist Gary Kamiya notes his military service is unclear.) In 1893 Sutro let him settle at the corner of Lincoln Way and the Great Highway. By one report he moved into an abandoned real estate shed, by another he built with driftwood. He and his wife fenced the place with washed-up coconut shells and furnished it from the beach. Around 1895 Dailey rented horsecars from Sutro at $5 a month (Western Neighborhoods Project says three cars, Kamiya says one) and opened one as "the Annex," selling coffee, doughnuts, candy, soft drinks and cigarettes to beachgoers and bicyclists. A Bulletin reporter in July 1896 called the cluster "Beachside." Dailey lived there the last ten years of his life. After he died, musicians took over the Annex car.

## Jacob Heyman, Sol Getz and how many cars Carville had by 1900

Carville grew because landowners could not sell sand. Adolph Sutro, mayor at the time, hoped wealthy buyers would build estates on his beach blocks, but he had put in no water, gas or sewers, so renting old cars at $5 a month brought in a little income. A block south, real estate man Jacob Heyman copied him. He advertised ocean lots at $7.50 a month with two horsecars thrown in for $35, assembled cars into a permanent house for a client in 1898, and sank a well into the aquifer, which made year-round living possible. His cars waited for buyers near 47th Avenue and Kirkham Street. From 1902 Sol Getz and Sons graded blocks near 47th and Lincoln and encouraged car-houses, as Jules Getz said, just to get someone out there. Car counts differ: an estimated 70 in July 1899 (Gary Kamiya), more than 100 by 1900 (Natalie Jahraus Cowan), or some 200 by 1900 (Woody LaBounty). By 1900 a lot with two cars cost $600.

Sources: [Carville-by-the-Sea in 10 Slides (Woody LaBounty, San Francisco Story)](https://www.sanfranciscostory.com/carville-by-the-sea-in-10-slides/), [From S.F.'s past: a crazy car colony in the dunes (Gary Kamiya, SF Chronicle)](https://www.sfgate.com/bayarea/article/From-S-F-s-past-a-crazy-car-colony-in-the-dunes-4676168.php), [CARVILLE: Suburban Bohemia in Fin de Siecle San Francisco (Natalie Jahraus Cowan, FoundSF)](https://foundsf.org/CARVILLE:_Suburban_Bohemia_in_Fin_de_Siecle_San_Francisco), [Historic Context Statement of the Oceanside (SPEAK, SF Planning)](https://default.sfplanning.org/Preservation/oceanside_survey/2010_Oceanside_Historic_Context.pdf).

## The Falcons, the women's bicycling club with a Carville clubhouse

The Falcons were a women's bicycle club that rented one of the first cars at the beach, on Sutro's block near Lincoln Way and 48th Avenue. Bicycling was the craze of the 1890s, and the seven married women of the club rode out through Golden Gate Park and used the car to rest afterward, napping on the long upholstered seats. As the club grew they added a second car and a lean-to for parking bicycles. They built a table that could seat 28 and held card games and large dinner parties. A Chronicle reporter wrote that they waded in the ocean when the beach was empty. Natalie Jahraus Cowan's history lists Ambrose Bierce, Adolph Sutro and Collis Huntington among their guests. One founder, Ida Fitzgerald, later lived with her husband Robert in a car-house on the 1400 block of 47th Avenue. The old Falcons car was the one burned in public on July 4, 1913.

Sources: [CARVILLE: Suburban Bohemia in Fin de Siecle San Francisco (Natalie Jahraus Cowan, FoundSF)](https://foundsf.org/CARVILLE:_Suburban_Bohemia_in_Fin_de_Siecle_San_Francisco), [Streetwise: Carville (Western Neighborhoods Project)](https://www.outsidelands.org/sw18.php), [Historic Context Statement of the Oceanside (SPEAK, SF Planning)](https://default.sfplanning.org/Preservation/oceanside_survey/2010_Oceanside_Historic_Context.pdf).

## Who lived in Carville: musicians, bohemians, a judge, writers and families

Carville drew people who wanted cheap rooms and distance from the city. Musicians who worked downtown theaters and cafes named Colonel Dailey's old Annex car "La Boheme" and gathered there from midnight to seven in the morning, with night swims between rounds. In 1908 they hosted players from New York's Metropolitan Opera, who called it the best stop of their tour. The "Fuzzy Bunch" picnic car drew Jack London, George Sterling, Maynard Dixon, Ina Coolbrith, Gelett Burgess and Anna Strunsky. A Dr. Cross kept a clubhouse for writers and artists and gave them an open tab at a nearby grocery. Painter Xavier Martinez rented a car as a studio. The Barefoot Club, professional men who liked beach walks, had a car at 46th Avenue and Lawton Street. A bachelor judge rented one of the first few cars. Most cars were weekend places at first. By 1901 about fifty families lived there full time, and after 1906 Bulletin editor Fremont Older stayed for a while.

## How Carville's car-houses were built, and its restaurants and nicknames

Carville builders treated streetcar bodies like blocks. They set cars side by side and opened the walls between them, placed them end to end in L, T, U and even W shapes, and stacked them two and three stories high. Some houses used as many as ten cars. Owners added porches and fences to block blowing sand, and many covered the cars in shingles, which hid their origin and stood up to salt air. A house in a hollow could have a garden (the Overland Monthly noticed nasturtiums and pelargoniums). A house on a dune had the ocean view, but sand buried any garden. Tea rooms and restaurants opened in old cable cars, and a circa 1905 photograph taken from La Playa shows "Mrs. Gunn's Home Cooking." Residents named a big sand hill "Mt. Diablo" and called their homes "Castle Chillon" and "Fortress Quebec." Scientific American wrote up the building methods in June 1901. By 1908 some complained that telephone wires and concrete sidewalks were spoiling the place.

## St. Andrew's by-the-Sea, the Carville church made of streetcars

St. Andrew's by-the-Sea was an Episcopal church in Carville built from streetcars. The Oceanside historic context statement says it was made of three cars and stood on 47th Avenue near Irving Street. A 1908 Overland Monthly article, quoted by Western Neighborhoods Project, says its second floor was made of North Beach and Mission line cars, so it was a two-story building. The sources we read do not give its founding date, its clergy, or what became of it. The first permanent church in the neighborhood was probably St. Paul's Presbyterian, a brown shingled building on Kirkham Street between 46th and 47th Avenues, dedicated September 9, 1906. It soon added a gymnasium called the Oceanside Boys Club. In 1923 the congregation built a new church and gym at 43rd Avenue and Judah Street, designed by Thomas A. Cuthbertson, and that building was still standing when the context statement was updated in 2010.

Sources: [Historic Context Statement of the Oceanside (SPEAK, SF Planning)](https://default.sfplanning.org/Preservation/oceanside_survey/2010_Oceanside_Historic_Context.pdf), [Streetwise: Carville (Western Neighborhoods Project)](https://www.outsidelands.org/sw18.php).

## What the 1906 earthquake did to Carville, and the peak of 2,000 people

The 1906 earthquake and fire made Carville bigger and more permanent. People who had lost homes moved into what had been clubhouses and weekend cottages. The disaster also produced more cars: transit companies rebuilt damaged cable lines as electric streetcar lines, old cable cars became surplus, and some burned-out families hauled them to the beach. That is why later Carville houses include cable cars as well as horsecars. Bulletin editor Fremont Older was among those who stayed a while. The peak figure most often repeated, 2,000 residents by 1908, comes from a November 1908 Overland Monthly article by Gibbs Adams. The Oceanside historic context statement applies that 2,000 to the whole beach neighborhood, which already had over 200 ordinary houses by the end of 1905, so it likely counts everyone near the beach, not only people in cars. By about 1910 car-houses ran from Lincoln Way to Moraga Street. About 30 cars were still standing into the 1930s.

## The Oceanside neighborhood name and the Oceanside Improvement Club

Oceanside was the name people with regular houses preferred to Carville. It covered the Sunset west of about 37th Avenue, from Lincoln Way to Sloat Boulevard, and was in use from about 1903 to about 1930. It likely came from the Ocean Side House, an 1866 roadhouse on the Great Highway between today's Ulloa and Vicente Streets. Adolph Sutro had also pushed the name. The first school, in 1903, was the Oceanside Primary School, and an October 1903 newspaper said the area once called Carville now had a "more dignified title." A neighborhood improvement club formed in 1903, and city directories list an Oceanside Improvement Club from 1909. It met at Oceanside Hall on 48th Avenue between Irving and Judah (now a house) and lobbied for sewers, streetlights, paving, a firehouse and better schools. Its president, Alexander Russell, lived in the old Ocean Side House. Membership was men only, so women founded the Oceanside Women's Club in 1913.

## The July 4, 1913 bonfire: burning the car out of Carville

On the Fourth of July 1913 the Oceanside Improvement Club burned Carville cars in public to show the neighborhood had moved on. Club president Alexander Russell got permission from Emma Sutro Merritt, Adolph Sutro's daughter, to clear the original cluster on her land at Lincoln Way and the Great Highway. Russell threw in $10 of fireworks, and hundreds came to watch. The slogan was to make clean today by burning up the debris of yesterday. The Chronicle headline two days later read "Burn the car out of Carville." Accounts differ on how much burned. Western Neighborhoods Project describes the burning of the old Falcons bicycle club car. Gary Kamiya and Natalie Jahraus Cowan say four cars, including the Falcons clubhouse. The planning department's context statement says the group torched the houses on that block. The fire was a publicity event, not the end. Many car-houses still stood in 1915, and they disappeared slowly through the 1920s and 1930s.

## The last known Carville car-house, on the Great Highway between Lawton and Moraga

One Carville house is known to survive: a shingled two-story home on the 1600 block of the Great Highway, between Lawton and Moraga Streets. Its upper floor is two cable cars set side by side with the inner walls removed, plus an intact horsecar used as a bedroom. Permit research found that a Mrs. Collins (the planning department names her as Minnie Collins, a notary and photo-journalist) had the cars set on a frame in 1908, and they were probably raised to a second story in 1921. Researcher James Heisterkamp found the house in 1994 when it was for sale at $309,000. Filmmaker Scott Anderson bought it and told Western Neighborhoods Project in 2002 that he meant to preserve it. Inside were original benches, a slat ceiling and kerosene lamps. It is a private home, the cars cannot be seen from the sidewalk, and the owner asked then that the address not be published. We found no report of any change to it through 2026.

## Other Carville traces on 47th and 48th Avenues and the blocks that still feel like Carville

Beyond the Great Highway house, no other Carville car-house is confirmed today, though people keep looking. A three-story building on the 1400 block of 47th Avenue, once the home of Falcons founder Ida Fitzgerald and her husband Robert, had streetcars in an upper story, but a remodel removed them. A February 1925 photograph, captioned as the last of Carville, shows a car-house on the 1300 block of 48th Avenue. Historian Lorri Ungaretti wrote that two surviving houses are built around streetcars, while Woody LaBounty and the planning department's historians know of only one. Some cars were simply built into later houses and shingled over, and Western Neighborhoods Project tells of a remodel that turned up wheels under a living room floor. For the feel of the years when Carville became Oceanside, the planning survey points to the 1500 block of 45th Avenue and the 1600 and 1700 blocks of the Great Highway, with their mix of self-built cottages and Craftsman houses.

## Where to see photographs of Carville and read more

Carville was photographed a great deal, and most of the pictures are free to see online. Western Neighborhoods Project runs the OpenSFHistory photo archive, which holds Carville views such as the circa 1905 images of car-houses on La Playa between Irving and Judah and at 48th Avenue and Judah. Its two Streetwise articles on Carville include several of them. FoundSF shows the Falcons at their clubhouse, the "Villa Miramar" wheelmen's rest and Mrs. Gunn's Home Cooking, some from the San Francisco History Center on the sixth floor of the Main Library. The Market Street Railway's Sunset streetcar history reproduces the 48th and Judah photograph and identifies individual cars in it by number and paint color. Woody LaBounty's book "Carville-by-the-Sea: San Francisco's Streetcar Suburb" (2009, with a 2022 digital edition) is the fullest account. The classic article is Natalie Jahraus Cowan's "Carville, San Francisco's Oceanside Bohemia," in California History, Winter 1978/79.

Sources: [Streetwise: Carville (Western Neighborhoods Project)](https://www.outsidelands.org/sw18.php), [CARVILLE: Suburban Bohemia in Fin de Siecle San Francisco (Natalie Jahraus Cowan, FoundSF)](https://foundsf.org/CARVILLE:_Suburban_Bohemia_in_Fin_de_Siecle_San_Francisco), [Streetcars in the Sunset (Market Street Railway)](https://www.streetcar.org/streetcars_in_the_sunset_troll-2/), [Carville-by-the-Sea in 10 Slides (Woody LaBounty, San Francisco Story)](https://www.sanfranciscostory.com/carville-by-the-sea-in-10-slides/), [San Francisco History Center (SF Public Library)](https://sfpl.org/locations/main-library/sf-history-center).

- [SF Planning, Oceanside Historic Resource Survey](https://sfgov.org/sfplanningarchive/oceanside-historic-resource-survey): ask whether an early beach cottage on your block was surveyed.
