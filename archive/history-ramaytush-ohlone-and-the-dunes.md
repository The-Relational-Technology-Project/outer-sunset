This note covers the first people of the San Francisco peninsula, the Ramaytush Ohlone, and the Yelamu, who lived in what is now San Francisco. It says plainly what is and is not documented for the western dunes that became the Outer Sunset, what happened after Mission Dolores was founded in 1776, and what the Ramaytush Ohlone are doing today. It also describes the land itself before the city: the dune field, its plants and animals, its ponds, wind and fog.

## Who the Ramaytush Ohlone are, the original people of the San Francisco Peninsula

The Ramaytush Ohlone (said "rah-my-toosh") are the original peoples of the San Francisco Peninsula, and they are living people today. The Association of Ramaytush Ohlone (ARO) explains that Ramaytush is a Chochenyo word, from the East Bay, meaning "people of the west." Ramaytush was one dialect of the San Francisco Bay Costanoan language, alongside Chochenyo and Tamyen.

Before the Portolá expedition of 1769, about 1,500 to 2,000 people lived on the peninsula, according to the ARO. Jonathan Cordero, writing for the National Park Service, says they were divided into ten independent tribes. Their homeland runs from the Golden Gate south to roughly Pigeon Point on the ocean side and the Palo Alto area on the bay side.

The ARO says only one lineage is known to have survived the mission period. Its descendants form the four branches of the Ramaytush people today and make up the Ramaytush Tribe and the staff of the ARO.

Sources: [Association of Ramaytush Ohlone, Original Peoples](https://www.ramaytush.org/original-peoples.html), [Association of Ramaytush Ohlone, Ramaytush Territory](https://www.ramaytush.org/ramaytush-territory.html), [National Park Service, Impact of Spanish Colonization](https://www.nps.gov/articles/impact-of-spanish-colonization.htm), [Association of Ramaytush Ohlone, home page](https://www.ramaytush.org/).

## The Yelamu, the Ramaytush Ohlone tribe of what is now San Francisco

The Yelamu were the independent Ramaytush Ohlone tribe whose land was what is now San Francisco County. Their southern boundary roughly matched today's San Francisco and San Mateo county line, according to the Association of Ramaytush Ohlone.

They were few. Historian Randall Milliken estimated 160 to 300 Yelamu at the time the Spanish arrived, and a National Park Service exhibit at Crissy Field says about 200. Milliken described three related groups. One moved along Mission Creek between Sitlintac, a winter village on Mission Bay, and Chutchui, near today's Mission Dolores. One moved between Tubsinte, near Candlestick Point, and Amuctac in Visitacion Valley. A third lived seasonally at Petlenuc, near the Presidio shore.

All five named villages are on the bay side or the Golden Gate, not the ocean dunes. The Association of Ramaytush Ohlone says there are no known living Yelamu descendants. The living Ramaytush trace their village of origin to what is now Pacifica.

Sources: [Association of Ramaytush Ohlone, Original Peoples](https://www.ramaytush.org/original-peoples.html), [FoundSF, Searching for the Yelamu in San Francisco, by Gary Kamiya](https://www.foundsf.org/index.php?title=Searching_for_the_Yelamu_in_San_Francisco), [National Park Service, Home of the Yelamu](https://www.nps.gov/places/000/8-home-of-the-yelamu.htm).

## What is and is not documented about Native life on the Sunset dunes

No Yelamu or Ramaytush Ohlone village is documented in what is now the Sunset District. The San Francisco Planning Department's 2013 Sunset history says there were no known Native American settlements in the Sunset, and that its own records do not flag the area as likely to hold archaeological remains. A 2008 city study of the Parkside says Native people probably did not hunt or gather there, though they may have at Lake Merced.

That is a statement about evidence, not proof of absence. Randall Milliken told writer Gary Kamiya that the Yelamu "used the whole peninsula," treating San Francisco like one large campsite. A park service archaeologist told Kamiya that how people moved around the peninsula is largely conjecture. Much knowledge was lost with the people who died at the mission, and some is held by the Ramaytush community, not published.

So a careful answer is: the dunes between Golden Gate Park and Sloat were part of Yelamu territory, and nobody has documented what people did here.

Sources: [SF Planning, Sunset District Residential Builders 1925 to 1950, Historic Context Statement](https://default.sfplanning.org/Preservation/sunset_survey/Adopted_SunsetHCS.pdf), [SF Planning, Parkside District Historic Context Statement, 2008](https://archives.sfplanning.org/documents/4976-parkside-statement%20march%202008.pdf), [FoundSF, Searching for the Yelamu in San Francisco, by Gary Kamiya](https://www.foundsf.org/index.php?title=Searching_for_the_Yelamu_in_San_Francisco).

## Lands End and Lake Merced, the nearest documented Native sites to the Outer Sunset

The closest places to the Outer Sunset with documented Native sites are Lands End, just north of Ocean Beach, and Lake Merced, just south of Sloat.

At Lands End, the National Park Service says the Yelamu lived in seasonal settlements at the ocean's edge, drawn by hillside springs, brush for shelter and easy access to the shore. They fished and hunted otters, sea lions and sea birds. Park archaeologists have found clam shell fragments and a Monterey chert spear point there. Gary Kamiya lists an archaeological site off Merrie Way at Point Lobos.

At Lake Merced, Kamiya notes a couple of sites by the lake and sparse bones found on the San Francisco State campus. The city's Parkside study says deer and elk grazed around Lake Merced and that Ohlone people presumably hunted them.

Published sources give few details for either place. Kamiya notes that information about archaeological sites is kept semi-confidential.

Sources: [National Park Service, Ohlone Life at Lands End](https://www.nps.gov/places/000/9-ohlone-life.htm), [National Park Service, Prehistoric Artifacts](https://home.nps.gov/goga/learn/historyculture/prehistoric-artifacts.htm), [FoundSF, Searching for the Yelamu in San Francisco, by Gary Kamiya](https://www.foundsf.org/index.php?title=Searching_for_the_Yelamu_in_San_Francisco), [SF Planning, Parkside District Historic Context Statement, 2008](https://archives.sfplanning.org/documents/4976-parkside-statement%20march%202008.pdf).

## Foods, shellmounds and tule boats of the Yelamu and other Ohlone people

The Yelamu did not farm. They hunted, fished and gathered, and moved with the seasons between village sites. Gary Kamiya describes short daily trips: down to the tidal flats for mussels, a couple of miles to a grove of coast live oaks for acorns. A FoundSF essay drawn from Pete Holloran's research says Ohlone people used more than 150 plants for food, medicine and other needs, and that the Presidio's tidal marshes supplied at least 29 kinds of fish.

Shellmounds are the largest thing they left. In 1909 Nels Nelson of UC Berkeley mapped 425 shellmounds around the bay, 18 of them in San Francisco. Kamiya writes that most of the city's roughly 50 known Native sites are in the southeast, near Islais Creek, Hunters Point and Visitacion Valley. None of the sources read for this note places a shellmound in the Sunset.

People crossed calm bay water in boats made of tule reeds. Houses were light frames covered with willow and tule.

Sources: [FoundSF, Searching for the Yelamu in San Francisco, by Gary Kamiya](https://www.foundsf.org/index.php?title=Searching_for_the_Yelamu_in_San_Francisco), [FoundSF, The Pre-European Landscape](https://www.foundsf.org/index.php?title=The_Pre-European_Landscape), [SF Planning, Sunset District Residential Builders 1925 to 1950, Historic Context Statement](https://default.sfplanning.org/Preservation/sunset_survey/Adopted_SunsetHCS.pdf).

## Fire and land tending by Ohlone people on the San Francisco peninsula

Ohlone people shaped the land with fire. The National Park Service says periodic burning promoted native grasses for seed gathering and made forage for deer and elk. A FoundSF essay based on Pete Holloran's research adds that fire kept shrubs from taking over grasslands rich in edible bulbs, and that willows were pruned to grow the straight shoots basket weavers wanted.

There are eyewitness records. In late October 1816 a visitor camped near Mission Dolores wrote that fires burned all night because the Native people were in the habit of burning the grass to help it grow. When Father Pedro Font walked the open Presidio mesa in 1776 and found wildflowers and good pasture, he was probably looking at tended land, the essay argues.

These records come from the Presidio and the Mission side of the city. No source read for this note describes burning on the Sunset dunes.

Sources: [National Park Service, Presidio, Indigenous Period](https://home.nps.gov/prsf/learn/historyculture/indigenous-period.htm), [FoundSF, The Pre-European Landscape](https://www.foundsf.org/index.php?title=The_Pre-European_Landscape), [KQED, The Bay 250 Years Ago](https://www.kqed.org/news/12097245/the-bay-250-years-ago).

## Mission Dolores from 1776 and the collapse of the Ramaytush Ohlone population

Mission San Francisco de Asís, known as Mission Dolores, was founded in 1776 by Fray Francisco Palou beside Dolores Creek, near the Yelamu village of Chutchui. The Association of Ramaytush Ohlone (ARO) gives the date as July 29. Baptisms began in 1777. The first was a twenty-year-old Yelamu man named Chamis, renamed Francisco Moraga.

Because the mission stood in Yelamu territory, the Yelamu were drawn in earlier than tribes farther south. By 1801 all the Native people of the peninsula had joined Mission Dolores, and about eighty percent had died, according to the National Park Service, citing Randall Milliken's research. The causes were disease and poor living and working conditions. Average life expectancy after baptism fell to about four years.

By 1834 only 37 descendants of the original peninsula people were living at the mission, and by 1842 about 15, the ARO reports. By 1850 only about five Ramaytush families survived. The ARO describes this as near total genocide, followed by the loss of most of the culture and language.

Sources: [Association of Ramaytush Ohlone, Spanish Arrival](https://www.ramaytush.org/spanish-arrival.html), [National Park Service, Impact of Spanish Colonization](https://www.nps.gov/articles/impact-of-spanish-colonization.htm), [Association of Ramaytush Ohlone, About the ARO](https://www.ramaytush.org/about-the-aro.html).

## Pedro Alcantara and the question of the last Yelamu

Pedro Alcantara is the person most often named as the last of San Francisco's first people, and the sources do not fully agree about him. A National Park Service exhibit at Crissy Field calls him the last known Yelamu, born in 1780. It quotes an interview recorded in 1850 by Indian Agent Adam Johnston, in which he says he is all that is left of his people.

The Association of Ramaytush Ohlone gives more detail. It says Pedro Alcantara was born at the village of Ssalaime on Purisima Creek, south of Half Moon Bay, that his father came from nearby Torose, and that his mother came from the Yelamu village of Chutchui. He was baptized at Mission Dolores on September 25, 1786. By that account he was Ramaytush with Yelamu ancestry through his mother, born outside San Francisco.

Both sources agree on the larger point: no Yelamu descendants are known today, and the Ramaytush people who survive descend from a single lineage.

Sources: [National Park Service, Home of the Yelamu](https://www.nps.gov/places/000/8-home-of-the-yelamu.htm), [Association of Ramaytush Ohlone, Ramaytush Territory](https://www.ramaytush.org/ramaytush-territory.html), [Association of Ramaytush Ohlone, Original Peoples](https://www.ramaytush.org/original-peoples.html).

## The Association of Ramaytush Ohlone today: rematriation and the Ramaytush Land Trust

The Association of Ramaytush Ohlone (ARO) is a nonprofit that represents the Ramaytush Tribe. Jonathan Cordero is its executive director and chair of the tribe. The ARO names two inherited responsibilities: to care for the earth, and to care for the people who live in the Ramaytush homeland, which includes everyone in the Outer Sunset.

Its stated goals include rematriation, meaning the return of land in the homeland to its original stewards, and public education. The ARO writes that very little of the culture and language remains, so it works to revive traditions and to bring its community back after long displacement.

The Ramaytush Land Trust is the tribe's official land trust. It seeks to hold land, gain access to it, or help manage it, for stewardship, service and ceremony. The ARO says legal ownership is not always needed. It has an agreement with the Filoli estate in San Mateo County, and access arrangements with the Peninsula Open Space Trust and Midpeninsula Regional Open Space District.

Sources: [Association of Ramaytush Ohlone, About the ARO](https://www.ramaytush.org/about-the-aro.html), [Association of Ramaytush Ohlone, Land Trust](https://www.ramaytush.org/land-trust.html), [Association of Ramaytush Ohlone, Our Vision](https://www.ramaytush.org/our-vision.html), [Association of Ramaytush Ohlone, home page](https://www.ramaytush.org/).

## Land acknowledgment in San Francisco and what the Ramaytush Ohlone ask of it

Many San Francisco public meetings open with a Ramaytush Ohlone land acknowledgment. The San Francisco Human Rights Commission adopted one on November 12, 2020. The Recreation and Park Commission passed its own in 2021, written with Gregg Castro and Jonathan Cordero. The Elections Commission followed on October 19, 2022. A Building Inspection Commission resolution says the Board of Supervisors, the Human Rights Commission and the Office of Racial Equity urged every city board and commission to open meetings this way.

The city wording was written with and approved by the Association of Ramaytush Ohlone (ARO). It calls San Francisco the "unceded ancestral homeland" of the Ramaytush Ohlone and says they have never given up their responsibilities as caretakers of this place.

The ARO's advice to anyone writing one: write it yourself, name the specific people whose land you are on, ask the ARO to review a draft, and then act on it. The ARO calls this "Land Acknowledgement Plus." Its site has a template to start from.

Sources: [Association of Ramaytush Ohlone, Land Acknowledgement](https://www.ramaytush.org/land-acknowledgement.html), [San Francisco Elections Commission, Resolution on Ramaytush Ohlone Land Acknowledgment](https://www.sf.gov/sites/default/files/2023-01/Elections_Comm_Land_Acknowledgment_Res_Oct_2022.pdf), [Building Inspection Commission, Ramaytush Ohlone Land Acknowledgement resolution](https://www.sf.gov/sites/default/files/2022-09/BIC%20Ohlone%20Land%20Resolution.pdf), [Glen Park Association, Ramaytush Ohlone Land Acknowledgment Resolution](https://glenparkassociation.org/ramaytush-ohlone-land-acknowledgment-resolution).

## Ramaytush Ohlone work in the Sunset, Golden Gate Park and the Presidio

The Association of Ramaytush Ohlone (ARO) is a partner in the Sunset Natural Resilience Project, begun in 2022 with the San Francisco Estuary Institute. A grant of up to $555,000 pays for designs and guidance for six projects in western San Francisco: urban greening, a wildlife corridor and dune restoration.

One of the six is a garden for Ramaytush Ohlone, American Indian and other Indigenous people in Golden Gate Park. The ARO describes the site as a wood chip storage yard at the lower end of the park. Its pages give the size as five acres in one place and 3.5 acres in another. The partners are Friendship House Association of American Indians and the Recreation and Parks Department. A May 2024 ARO presentation to the city reported a signed agreement and the start of design.

In the Presidio, the ARO is working with the Presidio Trust to design a park near Outpost Meadow. Since 2025 it has taught Youth Naturalist classes with Sutro Stewards.

Sources: [Association of Ramaytush Ohlone, Ecological Restoration](https://www.ramaytush.org/ecological-restoration.html), [Association of Ramaytush Ohlone, Land Trust](https://www.ramaytush.org/land-trust.html), [Association of Ramaytush Ohlone, Community Service](https://www.ramaytush.org/community-service.html), [Association of Ramaytush Ohlone, presentation to the city, May 2024](https://sf.gov/sites/default/files/2024-05/ARO%20Presentation%20May%202024%20%28002%29.pdf).

## Where the Sunset's sand dunes came from

The Outer Sunset sits on part of a dune field that once lay under much of San Francisco. The National Park Service explains that the sand began on a broad coastal plain that reached about 30 miles offshore when sea level was low in the last Ice Age. As glaciers melted, between roughly 18,000 and 5,000 years ago, the rising sea pushed the sand landward, and wind blew it up and over the hills from Ocean Beach and Baker Beach.

Bay Nature traces the grains farther back: quartz, magnetite and other minerals from the Sierra Nevada, carried down rivers, out through the bay, and pushed back onto the coast by waves. That is why Ocean Beach sand shows beige, black and clear grains.

The dunes are as deep as 100 feet in places, the city's Parkside history says. Bay Nature reports that sand once covered nearly a third of the city. They stayed active until the city covered them. Fort Funston holds the largest dune field left on the peninsula.

Sources: [National Park Service, Dune Habitat](https://www.nps.gov/places/000/dune-habitat-a-once-common-now-rare-commodity.htm), [Bay Nature, Ocean Beach Gets a Dunescaping, 2025](https://baynature.org/2025/03/13/ocean-beach-gets-a-dunescaping/), [SF Planning, Parkside District Historic Context Statement, 2008](https://archives.sfplanning.org/documents/4976-parkside-statement%20march%202008.pdf).

## Plants and animals of the Sunset dunes before houses

The Sunset dunes were not bare. City histories describe two kinds: drifting dunes with no plants, pushed west to east by the wind, and stabilized dunes held by deep-rooted plants.

On the looser sand grew dune grass, also called beach wildrye (Leymus mollis), yellow sand verbena (Abronia latifolia), beach strawberry (Fragaria chiloensis), yellow bush lupine (Lupinus arboreus) and Chamisso's lupine (Lupinus chamissonis). Stabilized dunes carried monkey flower, lizard tail, dune tansy, seaside daisy and San Francisco wallflower. In sheltered gullies stood coast live oak, arroyo willow, wax myrtle and California buckeye. There were no tall trees on open sand because roots could not hold.

Bay Nature describes brush rabbits eating coast dandelions and Xerces blue butterflies (Glaucopsyche xerces), now extinct, among the lupine. The San Francisco Estuary Institute's Hidden Nature project lists the silver digger bee (Habropoda miserabilis) as a dune specialist and says California quail were once common in the city. A photo from about 1910 shows a rabbit hunting party in the Parkside dunes.

Sources: [SF Planning, Parkside District Historic Context Statement, 2008](https://archives.sfplanning.org/documents/4976-parkside-statement%20march%202008.pdf), [SF Planning, Oceanside Historic Context Statement, 2010](https://default.sfplanning.org/Preservation/oceanside_survey/2010_Oceanside_Historic_Context.pdf), [Bay Nature, Ocean Beach Gets a Dunescaping, 2025](https://baynature.org/2025/03/13/ocean-beach-gets-a-dunescaping/), [San Francisco Estuary Institute, Hidden Nature SF, Wildlife](https://sites.google.com/sfei.org/hidden-nature-sf/learn/wildlife).

## Ponds, lakes, wind and fog in the Sunset before the city

Water collected among the Sunset dunes. Creeks running west from Golden Gate Heights were blocked by sand, forming tidal and freshwater ponds and lakes. The largest were Lake Merced and Laguna Puerca, now Pine Lake in Stern Grove, but many smaller seasonal ponds were scattered through the district and supported plants and wildlife. The San Francisco Estuary Institute shows a photograph from about 1910, probably taken in the Sunset, of a pond among vegetated dunes.

Wind did the rest. An early geologist, William P. Blake, saw that sand on the Pacific side moved steadily inland in wave-like hills that buried shrubs and trees. He also noticed that scrub-covered dunes held firm until someone cut the scrub for firewood. Harold Gilliam wrote that the same sea winds brought the city its two ocean-made elements, drifting sand and flowing fog.

Early maps called the area the "Great Sand Waste." Naturalists Greg Gaar and Ryder Miller argue it was a varied habitat, not a waste.

Sources: [SF Planning, Parkside District Historic Context Statement, 2008](https://archives.sfplanning.org/documents/4976-parkside-statement%20march%202008.pdf), [FoundSF, Sand Dunes of Early San Francisco](https://www.foundsf.org/index.php?title=The_San_Francisco_Sand_Dunes), [San Francisco Estuary Institute, Hidden Nature SF, Learn](https://sites.google.com/sfei.org/hidden-nature-sf/learn).
