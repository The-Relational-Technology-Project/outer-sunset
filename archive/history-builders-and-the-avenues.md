This note covers how the Sunset District, and the Outer Sunset in particular, went from sand dunes to rows of stucco houses between about 1925 and 1960. It names the builders, explains the house types and styles, and covers the boulevard, parks, shopping streets and zoning that came with them. Who was allowed to buy these houses is in a separate note, "Who could buy a house in the Sunset."

## Sunset sand dunes: why the houses came late and how the dunes were leveled

The Sunset District sand dunes kept builders away until the mid-1920s. Old maps called the area the "Great Sand Waste." The street grid was drawn on paper in 1868, but some streets were not graded or paved until the 1940s. A 1937 aerial survey still showed open dunes beside Sunset Boulevard, and a November 1943 photo from 33rd Avenue and Pacheco shows bare sand to the southwest.

The big builders bought whole blocks of dune, graded them and paid for streets. In 1926 the Rousseau brothers' Marian Realty graded lots and paid for street work on ten blocks between Noriega and Lawton. Sand had uses: in 1910 the United Railroads ran a conveyor streetcar at 21st Avenue and Ortega to collect dune sand for streetcar traction. The last major dune went when St. Ignatius College Preparatory was built at 2001 37th Avenue. (FoundSF says the 1970s. The school moved there in fall 1969.) The sources read for this note do not say where builders put the leveled sand.

## Streetcars, tunnels, cars and the Sunset shopping streets: Irving, Judah, Noriega, Taraval

Streetcar tunnels and cars opened the Sunset, and the shopping streets grew along the car lines. The Twin Peaks Tunnel opened in 1917 or 1918 (sources differ) and carried the L Taraval, which reached the beach in 1923. The Sunset Tunnel opened in 1928 with the N Judah. Car ownership in San Francisco tripled from 47,969 in 1920 to 146,182 in 1930. Even so, a 1926 map showed only one north-south road, 34th Avenue, crossing the central dunes.

Irving Street owners organized in 1925 to get paving and ornamental street lights, and argued that shopping zones on Irving, Taraval and 48th Avenue were enough, fighting a business zone on Judah. In 1926 Noriega Street owners gave up strips of land so the street could be widened to 70 feet for future streetcar tracks. On Taraval, stores sprang up along the L line in the 1920s. The 1954 city directory listed 224 businesses on Taraval between 12th and 35th Avenues.

## Henry Doelger and "Doelger City": how many houses, how fast, and where

Henry Doelger (born 1896, raised above his parents' store at 7th Avenue and Hugo) built more Sunset houses than anyone. His first house, from 1927, is at 1427 39th Avenue, and his first tract is the 1400 block of 39th Avenue between Judah and Kirkham. His office and sales room at 320 Judah Street, an Art Deco building designed by Charles Clausen and built in 1932, became a city landmark in March 2013.

City planners say that by 1934 Doelger was finishing a house a day, and that from 1934 to 1941 he was the country's largest home builder, averaging two houses a day with about 500 workers. The often quoted total of 11,000 San Francisco buildings cannot be checked: Doelger destroyed his records. By 1940 he had built more than 2,500 homes. He advertised "Doelger City" as 26th to 36th Avenues between Noriega and Quintara. Historians put his main territory at 27th to 39th Avenues, Kirkham to Quintara. Specialized crews did foundations, framing and roofing, like an assembly line.

Sources: [SF Planning, Sunset District Residential Builders 1925-1950](https://default.sfplanning.org/Preservation/sunset_survey/Adopted_SunsetHCS.pdf), [SF Planning, Doelger Building landmark report](https://default.sfplanning.org/Preservation/landmarks_designation/Approved_Doelger_Final_LM_Report.pdf), [Western Neighborhoods Project, Doelger City](https://www.outsidelands.org/sw2.php).

## The Gellert brothers, Standard Building Company and "Sunstream Homes"

Carl and Fred Gellert ran the Standard Building Company, Doelger's main rival in the Sunset. They began as house painters and small contractors. (The Western Neighborhoods Project dates the company to 1922. City planners note it first appears in city directories in 1932.) Their first full subdivision was 24 houses on Ardenwood Way off Sloat Boulevard in 1932.

In 1939 they launched the "Sunstream Homes" brand for Sunset row houses priced under $6,000. The first Sunstream block is 31st Avenue between Quintara and Rivera. They put up more than 100 billboards, ran a Sunday radio show called "I Want a Home," and sent a free repair truck to new owners. In 1940 the company built and sold 357 houses. In 1939 and 1940 it built over 240 houses in Pine Lake Park beside Stern Grove. Its office was at 1500 Judah Street from 1935 to 1946, then at 2222 19th Avenue. By 1949 a Sunstream house cost about $12,000. Standard built roughly 25,000 homes around the Bay Area.

Sources: [Western Neighborhoods Project, The Gellert Brothers and Lakeshore Park](https://www.outsidelands.org/lakeshore.php), [SF Planning, Sunset District Residential Builders 1925-1950](https://default.sfplanning.org/Preservation/sunset_survey/Adopted_SunsetHCS.pdf).

## Oliver Rousseau and the Rousseau blocks, 34th to 36th Avenue

The Rousseau houses on 34th, 35th and 36th Avenues between Kirkham and Lawton are the fanciest tract houses in the Sunset. Brothers Arthur and Oliver Rousseau were trained architects whose Marian Realty Company built hotels and apartments in the 1920s. When the Depression killed big projects, they turned to small houses. Their first Sunset tract, 24 houses on 33rd Avenue between Kirkham and Lawton, was finished in May 1931.

Their last and largest tract, 93 houses beside the new Sunset Boulevard, went up in 1932 and 1933, priced from $6,995. Oliver designed turrets, sunken living rooms and the "patio plan," an open courtyard in the middle of the upper floor. The "Surprise House" at 1548 35th Avenue drew more than 6,000 visitors when it opened. Both brothers lived on 36th Avenue. The company went bankrupt at the end of 1933, after fewer than 200 Sunset houses. A city survey later found 253 such picturesque houses in 13 Sunset tracts eligible for the National Register as a historic district.

Sources: [SF Planning, Rousseaus' Boulevard Tract draft report](https://default.sfplanning.org/Preservation/rousseau/rousseau_landmark_district_report_draft.pdf), [SF Planning, Sunset District Residential Builders 1925-1950](https://default.sfplanning.org/Preservation/sunset_survey/Adopted_SunsetHCS.pdf), [SF Planning, Sunset Picturesque Period Revival Tracts](https://default.sfplanning.org/Preservation/sunset_survey/PicturesquePeriodRevivalHD.pdf).

## Ray Galli, Chris McKeon, the Stonesons, Lang Realty and Golden Gate Heights

Other Sunset builders worked beside Doelger and the Gellerts. Ray Galli, a former bookkeeper, built more than 3,000 homes in the Sunset and Parkside, paid union wages, and is credited with the "tunnel entrance." His one-story "Hollywood" and "Holiday" houses stand on the 2100 block of 45th Avenue. Chris McKeon, a former butcher, ran Happy Homes from offices on Taraval Street and built the "Rivera Heights" tract around 29th Avenue and Rivera in 1936. Henry and Ellis Stoneson, sons of Icelandic immigrants, built Sunset tracts in the 1930s, then Lakeside and Stonestown (opened 1952). Lang Realty, with an office at 900 Irving Street, built about 200 bungalows in the Parkside in 1926 and 1927. Permits show at least 60 builders and firms at work in the central Sunset.

Golden Gate Heights, on the hills near 15th Avenue, was a Doelger and Standard Building project of larger detached houses. A 1937 federal appraiser found it only 5 or 6 percent built and selling slowly, and blamed wind, fog and poor transit.

Sources: [SF Planning, Sunset District Residential Builders 1925-1950](https://default.sfplanning.org/Preservation/sunset_survey/Adopted_SunsetHCS.pdf), [Mapping Inequality, HOLC area description data (San Francisco B23)](https://github.com/americanpanorama/HOLC_Area_Description_Data).

## Sunset house types: garage on the ground floor, junior five, patio plan, tunnel entrance

The standard Sunset house is one story of living space over a ground-floor garage, on a lot 25 feet wide. Builders put the garage inside the house because 1920s cars were open-topped and leaked, and because a 25-foot lot left no room for a side driveway. City planners know of no examples outside San Francisco and, later, Daly City. A typical house has five rooms in a little under 1,000 square feet: living and dining rooms in front, kitchen and bath in the middle, two bedrooms over the back yard.

The "junior five" is the plain version, under 900 square feet with a combined kitchen and dining area, built mostly during and just after World War II. The "patio plan," introduced by Oliver Rousseau in 1932, has an open-air courtyard on the upper floor. The "tunnel entrance" is a ground-level passage back to a small court and the stairs. The earliest in the city's survey area dates to 1938. By 1940 it was standard. Most tunnels now have metal gates.

Sources: [SF Planning, Sunset District Residential Builders 1925-1950](https://default.sfplanning.org/Preservation/sunset_survey/Adopted_SunsetHCS.pdf), [Parkside District Historic Context Statement, 2008](https://archives.sfplanning.org/documents/4976-parkside-statement%20march%202008.pdf), [Western Neighborhoods Project, Doelger City](https://www.outsidelands.org/sw2.php).

## Sunset house styles and why the facades differ on one block

Sunset house facades differ on purpose. The floor plans behind them were limited to a few layouts to save money, so builders varied the fronts to avoid monotony and give buyers a choice. In the mid-1920s, whole blocks were built in one style, the barrel-front Mediterranean Revival with red tile trim. After Rousseau's 1931 to 1933 tracts, builders mixed Spanish Colonial, Tudor, French Provincial and Storybook fronts (turrets, dovecotes, miniature castles and chateaus) on one block. San Francisco's Storybook houses date from about 1930 to 1935.

Once federally insured loans brought mass production in the late 1930s, fronts became plainer and cheaper. Streamline Moderne, with glass block, porthole windows and curved balconies, appeared around 1936 and 1937 but made up fewer than 10 percent of facades. Doelger sold a Moderne model called the "Styleocrat," and Galli sold the "Casa Moderna." After 1940, Colonial Revival touches such as shutters became common. Doelger said rows of identical houses lost the character a house should have.

Sources: [SF Planning, Sunset District Residential Builders 1925-1950](https://default.sfplanning.org/Preservation/sunset_survey/Adopted_SunsetHCS.pdf), [SF Planning, Sunset Picturesque Period Revival Tracts](https://default.sfplanning.org/Preservation/sunset_survey/PicturesquePeriodRevivalHD.pdf).

## FHA mortgages, veterans' loans and Sunset house prices by decade

Federal mortgage insurance set the pace of Sunset building. In the 1920s a buyer needed at least 30 percent down on a loan renewed every five to ten years. The Federal Housing Administration, created in 1934, insured loans with under 10 percent down, paid off over 25 to 30 years. Building in the central Sunset peaked in 1940, with 499 houses in the city's survey area that year. The 1944 GI Bill added veterans' loans, and by May 1946 San Francisco had issued more house permits (17,000) than any other U.S. city.

Prices: federal appraisers said a standard six-room Sunset house sold for $7,000 to $7,500 in 1929, $4,500 in 1933 and $6,000 to $6,750 in 1937. Rousseau houses started at $6,995 in 1932. Doelger's 1941 "Rainbow House" was $5,560 with 10 percent down and $37.50 a month. His 1942 "Freedom House" was $4,780. By 1949 a Sunstream house cost about $12,000. In 1940 most buyers' families earned under $2,000 a year.

Sources: [SF Planning, Sunset District Residential Builders 1925-1950](https://default.sfplanning.org/Preservation/sunset_survey/Adopted_SunsetHCS.pdf), [Mapping Inequality, HOLC area description data (San Francisco B15, B17)](https://github.com/americanpanorama/HOLC_Area_Description_Data), [Western Neighborhoods Project, The Gellert Brothers and Lakeshore Park](https://www.outsidelands.org/lakeshore.php).

## Why the Outer Sunset blocks near the beach filled in last, and wartime building

The blocks from Sunset Boulevard west toward the beach were the last part of the Sunset to fill in. In 1937 a federal appraiser found the western end only about half built, a mix of older dwellings up to 30 years old and new stucco rows, with "only fair" transit and no high school nearby. The land beside Sunset Boulevard stayed dune into the late 1930s and 1940s. Doelger was opening model homes on 40th, 41st and 43rd Avenues in 1940 to 1942, and Galli on 44th and 45th in 1941 and 1942.

Then the war stopped nearly everything. A War Production Board order in 1942 halted non-essential building, and the city's central Sunset survey area gained one house in 1944. Galli built small "Holiday Homes" for defense workers on 45th Avenue. After 1945 building resumed, heaviest in the blocks bounded by Noriega, 37th Avenue, Sloat and 46th Avenue. St. Gabriel parish (2550 41st Avenue), founded in 1941, recalls dunes becoming blocks of houses by 1947.

## Sunset Boulevard: built in 1931 and a full block wide

Sunset Boulevard, between 36th and 37th Avenues, opened to the public on October 18, 1931. It is about 300 feet wide, a whole city block, because it was built as a boulevard with planted parkway strips on both sides of the roadway. As early as 1925, developers had called for a Sunset parkway that could act as a firebreak. The city's Bureau of Engineering built it with unemployed workers as a Depression jobs project, across what was then open sand. A bridge carries Lincoln Way over the boulevard at the park.

The boulevard gave the central Sunset its first good north-south road for cars, and the Rousseaus sold their houses on 36th Avenue as "Boulevard Homes" with ocean views across it. The parkway was planted with Monterey pine and Monterey cypress, partly as a windbreak. A local conservation blog reports that those trees are dying of age, that by 2019 the city had removed many hazardous ones and planted about 250 replacements, and that volunteers are adding native plant gardens.

## Sunset Reservoir, parks, schools and Stern Grove, built along with the houses

Parks, schools and a reservoir arrived with the Sunset's houses. McCoppin Square and Parkside Square were set aside as parks in the 1860s deal that created Golden Gate Park. McCoppin Square got improvements in 1935, celebrated with a parade that drew about 10,000 people. Carl Larsen gave the city two blocks at 19th Avenue and Ulloa in 1926 (Larsen Park). Rosalie Stern bought and donated Stern Grove in 1931. It opened June 4, 1932. The city added Pine Lake within five years, and free summer concerts have run since 1938. Lincoln High School on 24th Avenue opened in 1940 after a 1938 bond vote.

After the war the city took many of Ray Galli's unbuilt Sunset and Parkside lots by eminent domain for schools, a library and a community center. St. Gabriel School on 41st Avenue had about 1,200 pupils in the 1950s. Sunset Reservoir, at 24th Avenue and Ortega, is the city's largest, completed in 1960 according to Wikipedia. Its roof has carried 25,000 solar panels since December 2010.

Sources: [Parkside District Historic Context Statement, 2008](https://archives.sfplanning.org/documents/4976-parkside-statement%20march%202008.pdf), [FoundSF, From Dunes to Cityscape](https://foundsf.org/Changing_Physical_Landscape_of_the_Sunset), [SF Planning, Sunset District Residential Builders 1925-1950](https://default.sfplanning.org/Preservation/sunset_survey/Adopted_SunsetHCS.pdf), [St. Gabriel School, Our History](https://stgabrielsf.com/about-us/our-history), [Wikipedia, Sunset Reservoir](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sunset_Reservoir).

## Why Sunset blocks have few street trees, and why the backyards meet mid-block

Most Sunset blocks lack street trees because of sand, weather and layout. The dunes had lupine and scrub but no trees, since roots could not anchor in loose sand. City planners say fog and sandy soil make plantings hard to keep alive, and that street trees are "notably absent" from the builders' tracts. Salty ocean wind is hard on trees too.

The layout matters too. Sunset streets were designed for cars: the roadway is about twelve feet wider than in the Haight, and the sidewalks are much narrower. Every 25-foot lot has a driveway, so little curb is left. Builders gave each house a 10-foot setback with thin strips of lawn beside the driveway. Most of those strips have since been paved.

The houses stand wall to wall along the avenues, on lots about 120 feet deep, so the open space ends up behind them. The small houses left larger than usual backyards, which line up back to back down the middle of each block.

## After 1950: apartments, the 1978 downzoning and the 2025 Family Zoning Plan

Single-family building in the Sunset slowed after 1950. The big builders moved on: Doelger to Westlake in Daly City, the Stonesons to Stonestown, the Gellerts to Lakeshore and later Serramonte. Citywide, more two- to ten-unit buildings went up after the war. That was legal in most of the Sunset. In 1937 federal appraisers noted the district was zoned "second-residential," which allowed flats and small apartments, and they called apartments at the western end a "threat."

On September 18, 1978, the Board of Supervisors adopted a citywide residential rezoning. Its backers wanted to protect single-family neighborhoods. One present-day critic says it made apartments illegal in all but a few places and "stunted" the city.

That changed again recently. Mayor Lurie signed the Family Zoning Plan on December 12, 2025, and it took effect January 12, 2026. The city says it allows more and taller housing mainly along major transit routes and commercial streets on the north and west sides.
