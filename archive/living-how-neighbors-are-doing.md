This note is for questions like "How are my neighbors doing?", "Who could use some extra care this week?" and "What do people here need?" It has two halves. The first is what public, aggregate data says about people in the Sunset: census estimates, city surveys, needs assessments and 311 requests. The second is who in the neighborhood actually knows and checks on people, and how to join them. Most figures cover an area wider than the Outer Sunset alone, and each section says which. None of it is about any one person.

## What Knowy can and cannot tell you about how neighbors are doing

Knowy holds no information about individuals. It does not know who lives on your block, who is sick, who is lonely, or who just had a baby, and it will not guess. What it can show is the aggregate picture: census estimates for the Sunset, what the city's needs assessments found, what neighbors report to 311 by type, the events calendar, the groups listed below, and the topics neighbors have asked Knowy about each week (`log/questions.md`, which keeps topics and counts, never names or messages). In short, the public data says this is a neighborhood with many older adults, many of them living alone, many households that speak Chinese at home, and a stated wish for more places and reasons to meet. How your neighbors are doing this week is something only neighbors know. The real answer comes from knocking, hosting, and showing up. The sections below are meant to help you choose a door.

## How many older neighbors live in the Sunset, and how many live alone

About one in five people here is 65 or older. Census estimates for 2020 to 2024 put about 21,900 people aged 65 and over in ZIP codes 94122 and 94116, out of about 99,600 residents, or 22 percent. Those two ZIP codes cover the Outer Sunset plus the Inner Sunset and Parkside, so the area is wider than the Outer Sunset alone. About 4,100 of those older neighbors live alone, roughly 19 percent, and about 2,550 of them are women. About 10 percent of all residents report a disability. Among people 75 and older it is about 41 percent. These are survey estimates with margins of error, not a head count. They also do not show who is lonely. Living alone is a risk the city tracks, not a verdict: plenty of people who live alone are well connected, and some people in full houses are not.

Sources: [Census Reporter, ZIP 94122 (ACS 2020-2024, tables B01001, B09020, B18101)](https://censusreporter.org/profiles/86000US94122-94122/), [Census Reporter, ZIP 94116](https://censusreporter.org/profiles/86000US94116-94116/).

## Families with kids, and the languages Sunset households speak at home

About 9,200 of the 37,800 households in ZIP codes 94122 and 94116 include someone under 18, about 24 percent (census estimates, 2020 to 2024). Roughly 15,600 residents are under 18. English is the only home language in about 45 percent of households. A Chinese language, Cantonese or Mandarin, is the home language in about 10,900 households, or 29 percent. Spanish is spoken in about 3,200 households, and Russian or another Slavic language in about 1,200. The census also counts "limited English speaking households," where no one 14 or older speaks English very well. There are about 4,700 of these, 12.5 percent of all households. About 3,900 speak Chinese, about 280 speak Russian or another Slavic language, and about 100 speak Spanish. What this means in practice: a flyer, a block party invitation, or a storm warning in English alone will miss roughly one household in eight.

Sources: [Census Reporter, ZIP 94122 (ACS 2020-2024, tables B11005, B16002)](https://censusreporter.org/profiles/86000US94122-94122/), [Census Reporter, ZIP 94116](https://censusreporter.org/profiles/86000US94116-94116/).

## Renters, owners, rent burden, income and cars in the Sunset

Across ZIP codes 94122 and 94116, about 58 percent of households own their home and 42 percent rent (census estimates, 2020 to 2024). Renters are the majority in 94122 and a minority in 94116. Among renters whose costs could be measured, about 40 percent pay 30 percent or more of their income in rent, and about 20 percent pay half or more. Median household income is about $146,000 in 94122 and $159,000 in 94116. A median hides a lot: the same streets hold retired owners on fixed incomes and renters paying half their pay. People stay a long time. About 45 percent of owners moved in before 2000, and about a quarter before 1990. About 35 percent of renters moved in during 2020 or later. About 5,300 households, 14 percent, have no car. So a typical block has neighbors of forty years next to neighbors of two.

Sources: [Census Reporter, ZIP 94122 (ACS 2020-2024, tables B25003, B25070, B19013, B25038, B25044)](https://censusreporter.org/profiles/86000US94122-94122/), [Census Reporter, ZIP 94116](https://censusreporter.org/profiles/86000US94116-94116/).

## What Sunset neighbors said they need: the Sunset Forward needs assessment

The best single record of shared needs is Sunset Forward, run in 2020 and 2021 by SF Planning, then-Supervisor Gordon Mar's office, the SF County Transportation Authority and the District 4 Youth and Families Network. It heard from over 2,000 people, with 757 survey responses, in English and Chinese. Three findings stand out. Housing: 64 percent of respondents said housing in the Sunset is unaffordable, and nearly 40 percent said their future housing needs will not be met in their current home. Services: community and recreation centers and cultural centers were the most wanted, then parks, youth programs and grocery stores. Connection: the report says plainly "there is a lack of community connectedness in the Sunset" and asks for more events where people can meet across ages and cultures. A limit to know: only 26 percent of survey respondents were Asian, in a district that was 53 percent Asian, and it was done during the pandemic.

Sources: [SF Planning, Sunset Forward Community Needs Assessment Findings Report (2021)](https://sfplanning.org/sites/default/files/documents/citywide/sunsetforward/SunsetForward_Community_Needs_Assessment_Findings_Report.pdf), [SF Planning, Sunset Forward project page](https://sfplanning.org/project/sunset-forward).

## What Sunset Forward heard from seniors, families, young people and immigrants

Sunset Forward also reported needs group by group, in people's own words. Seniors "were isolated even before COVID-19" and asked for senior centers, food and health clinics closer to home or reachable by transit, plus internet and tech help. Many senior homeowners described being "house rich and cash poor." Families reported a shortage of affordable childcare, after-school and summer programs, and were "craving a sense of community connection with other families." Young adults aged 16 to 24 said they felt isolated from peers and wanted places to gather, such as libraries and affordable cafes. Immigrants asked for English lessons, translation at schools and nonprofits, and WeChat as a main way to reach households that speak only Chinese. Renters asked for tenant counseling. People across groups named mental health services, and noted a tension between longtime residents and newcomers that community events could ease. These are what people said in 2020 and 2021, not a current measurement.

Sources: [SF Planning, Sunset Forward Community Needs Assessment Findings Report (2021)](https://sfplanning.org/sites/default/files/documents/citywide/sunsetforward/SunsetForward_Community_Needs_Assessment_Findings_Report.pdf).

## Isolation among older adults: what the city's 2026 aging needs assessment found

The Department of Disability and Aging Services (DAS) published its Dignity Fund Community Needs Assessment in April 2026. Citywide, it estimates 192,300 San Franciscans are 60 or older, and that about 27 percent of them live alone and 25 percent have limited English. In its survey, 27 percent of seniors and 37 percent of adults with disabilities reported an unmet need for socializing or taking part in community life. Social connection ranked alongside housing, care and food. For District 4, the Sunset, the report counts 15,755 people 65 and over, about 3,650 of them with low to moderate income. It found the city's services are concentrated in central neighborhoods, which makes travel a barrier for people in outer districts, and that group meal participation was lowest in District 4. It did not publish an isolation rate for the Sunset. A District 4 participant said of a senior center, "I feel healthier and happier because of these activities."

Sources: [SF DAS, Dignity Fund Community Needs Assessment FY 2025-26](https://www.sfhsa.org/sites/default/files/media/document/2026-03/2026_dfcna_report_final_2025.03.25.pdf), [SF DAS, Dignity Fund Community Needs Assessment appendices](https://www.sfhsa.org/sites/default/files/media/document/2026-03/2026_dfcna_report_appendices_final_2025.03.25.pdf).

## What Outer Sunset neighbors report to 311

In the twelve months from November 17, 2025 to November 16, 2026, the city logged 19,419 requests to 311 in the area it labels Outer Sunset. The largest types: parking enforcement (6,920, about 36 percent), street and sidewalk cleaning (5,384, about 28 percent), general requests (1,250), graffiti on public property (1,173) and private property (352), sewer issues (534), tree maintenance (448), noise (390), park requests (353), street defects (303), sidewalk and curb (269), streetlights (233), and encampments (201). Read it for what it is. 311 counts requests, not people, and one neighbor can file many. It records what bothers people about streets and cars, because that is what 311 is for. There is no 311 category for a neighbor who has not been seen in a while, a family short on food, or a person who is lonely. So this list shows what neighbors ask the city to fix, not how neighbors are doing.

Sources: [DataSF, 311 Cases, Outer Sunset request types over the last 12 months (live query)](https://data.sf.gov/resource/vw6y-z8j6.json?$select=service_name,count(*)%20as%20n&$where=neighborhoods_sffind_boundaries='Outer%20Sunset'%20AND%20requested_datetime>='2025-11-17T00:00:00'&$group=service_name&$order=n%20DESC), [SF311](https://sf311.org).

## What you may have missed in the Sunset in 2026 if you stopped paying attention

Four things from 2026. The South Sunset Clubhouse at 2601 40th Avenue reopened on August 27 after a $6 million renovation. Self-Help for the Elderly runs it and serves about 200 neighborhood seniors there with daily hot meals, dance and fitness classes. The city's 2026 City Survey, released September 30, found residents citywide feel safer walking alone in their neighborhood than in 2023 (a B grade, up from C+), and named Sunset/Parkside as one of four neighborhoods where ratings rose across several topics. It also found 70 percent of residents say housing affordability got worse. The Transportation Authority finished a District 4 Community Shuttle Study in February 2026. It found only 4 percent of trips within the district use transit, and proposed a one-year on-demand shuttle pilot, with seniors and people with disabilities among those who would gain most. And the Sunset Wave set its date: gatherings on every block on Saturday, March 20, 2027.

## How to check on a neighbor, and who to call if you are worried

Start with the plain thing: knock, say your name and which house is yours, and leave a note with your phone number if no one answers. If you are worried and it is not an emergency, San Francisco police list wellness checks under their non-emergency line, 415-553-0123, open all day and night. Their guidance is to call 911 when there is danger to life or a medical emergency. The city also says 911 is the number for a mental health or substance use crisis, and that since 2020 it has shifted thousands of those calls from police to teams of paramedics, social workers and health staff. For someone in crisis on the street, the city's Street Team is requested through 311. For an older neighbor who would like a regular call, the Institute on Aging's Friendship Line is free and open 24 hours: 888-670-1360 in English, 888-670-2504 in Cantonese and Mandarin, 888-560-7360 in Russian, 888-571-0411 in Spanish. It makes outbound check-in calls on request. DAS can connect an older or disabled neighbor to services at 415-355-6700.

Sources: [SFPD, When to Call 9-1-1](https://www.sanfranciscopolice.org/node/4321), [SF.gov, San Francisco Street Team](https://www.sf.gov/san-francisco-street-team), [SF.gov, education campaign on helping people in crisis (2023)](https://www.sf.gov/news--san-francisco-launches-education-campaign-inform-public-how-help-people-crisis), [Institute on Aging, Friendship Line](https://www.ioaging.org/services/friendship-line/), [SF DAS, service site guide](https://www.sfhsa.org/sites/default/files/media/document/2026-03/2026_dfcna_report_appendices_final_2025.03.25.pdf).

## What research says about neighbors and wellbeing

Two pieces of evidence are worth knowing. In the July 1995 Chicago heat wave, sociologist Eric Klinenberg found that roughly 739 more people died than in a normal week. Those who died were typically older, living alone, and without nearby social contacts. Two neighboring areas with similar poverty had very different death rates. He traced the gap to the life of the streets: the area with busy sidewalks, shops and public places, where people saw each other daily, lost far fewer people. In May 2023 the U.S. Surgeon General issued an advisory on loneliness. As reported by NPR, it found about half of U.S. adults had experienced measurable loneliness, and that lacking connection raises the risk of early death about as much as smoking 15 cigarettes a day, with higher risks of heart disease (29 percent), stroke (32 percent) and dementia in older adults (50 percent). San Francisco had its own lesson: on September 1, 2017 the city reached 106 degrees, and six people died.

Sources: [University of Chicago Press, interview with Eric Klinenberg on Heat Wave](https://press.uchicago.edu/Misc/Chicago/443213in.html), [NPR via WAMC, America has a loneliness epidemic (May 2, 2023)](https://www.wamc.org/2023-05-02/america-has-a-loneliness-epidemic-here-are-6-steps-to-address-it), [U.S. Climate Resilience Toolkit, San Francisco's Heat and Air Quality Resilience Plan](https://toolkit.climate.gov/case-study/burning-issues-cool-solutions-san-francisco%E2%80%99s-heat-and-air-quality-resilience-plan).
