This note covers the living layer between and around Sunset houses: the dunes that were here first, the sandy soil and fog, street trees and Sunset Boulevard, butterflies and bees, salamanders and lizards, and the plants worth growing or pulling. It also says where neighbors can get native plants and garden help.

## What grew on the Sunset dunes before the houses

Before the houses, the Sunset was sand. The National Park Service says dunes covered about one-third of what is now San Francisco, and the California Native Plant Society's Yerba Buena Chapter puts the dune scrub on the city's west side at as much as 14 square miles. The dunes built up over the last 10,000 years as sea level rose.

It was not bare. The sand held low shrubs, grasses and wildflowers adapted to wet winters, dry summers and salt wind. Plants named in the sources include beach strawberry, pink sand verbena, silver bush lupine, beach bur, other lupines and dune tansy. Dune plants cope with dry, poor sand by having small, waxy, hairy or succulent leaves and deep roots.

A botanical study by Kaufeldt in 1954 recorded this plant life just before the last open dunes in the Sunset District were built over. The trees most neighbors think of as local, Monterey cypress and Monterey pine, are California natives but were not native to this stretch of coast.

Sources: [National Park Service, Sand Francisco](https://www.nps.gov/places/000/11-sand-francisco.htm), [CNPS Yerba Buena Chapter, Coastal dune scrub](https://cnps-yerbabuena.org/coastal-dune-scrub/), [Sutro Stewards, Gardening in the dunes](https://www.sutrostewards.org/amp/2016/08/31/gardening-in-the-dunes), [CNPS Yerba Buena Chapter, Sunset Boulevard Reimagined](https://cnps-yerbabuena.org/sunset-boulevard-reimagined-biodiversity-corridor/).

## Where pieces of the old dunes survive near the Sunset

A few pieces of the original dune landscape survive within reach of the Outer Sunset. The closest true remnant is Hawk Hill, a city natural area at 14th Avenue and Rivera Street in Golden Gate Heights. Recreation and Parks describes it as a remnant hilltop dune where shifting sand still supports dune plants, including the locally rare California croton and the San Francisco wallflower. The houses around it cut off the blowing sand that once renewed it. It is fragile and the city discourages walking on it.

Other dune scrub remnants are at Fort Funston south of Sloat, and in the Presidio at Lobos Creek Valley and above Baker Beach.

Along the ocean, the sand hills beside Sunset Dunes park are a mix of coastal scrub and dune habitat. Friends of Sunset Dunes lists yarrow, coastal sand verbena, coyote brush and California goldenweed growing there, along with invasive iceplant. In winter 2024 to 2025, volunteers planted more than 1,500 native beach wildrye grasses, and more planting has followed.

Sources: [SF Recreation and Parks, Hawk Hill](https://sfrecpark.org/facilities/facility/details/Hawk-Hill-340), [CNPS Yerba Buena Chapter, Coastal dune scrub](https://cnps-yerbabuena.org/coastal-dune-scrub/), [Sutro Stewards, Gardening in the dunes](https://www.sutrostewards.org/amp/2016/08/31/gardening-in-the-dunes), [Friends of Sunset Dunes, Visit](https://sunsetdunes.org/visit).

## Sandy soil in Sunset yards and what it means for gardening

Dig down in most Sunset yards and you reach dune sand. Sutro Stewards, who run a native plant nursery, describe that sand as dry and low in nutrients, and the wind off the ocean carries salt.

The plants that evolved here are built for those conditions. Sutro Stewards recommends these natives for sandy San Francisco gardens: seaside daisy (Erigeron glaucus), a low-water ground cover; coast buckwheat (Eriogonum latifolium), which draws butterflies; yellow bush lupine (Lupinus arboreus), which adds nitrogen to the soil; lizard tail (Eriophyllum staechadifolium), which takes salt and wind; sea thrift (Armeria maritima); dune tansy (Tanacetum bipinnatum); and gum plant (Grindelia hirsutula), good for holding slopes.

The city's free SF Plant Finder has a ready-made Sandy Soil plant list and lets you filter by soil, water needs, habitat value and bloom time, or search by your address.

Sources: [Sutro Stewards, Gardening in the dunes](https://www.sutrostewards.org/amp/2016/08/31/gardening-in-the-dunes), [SF Plant Finder](https://sfplanninggis.org/plantsf/).

## Fog, fog drip and how much water fog delivers

Summer fog brings moisture to the Sunset in the dry season. The U.S. Geological Survey's Pacific Coastal Fog Project explains what it does on land: it lowers summer temperatures, cuts evaporation, and adds moisture to dry ground, which reduces stress on plants.

Fog drip is the water that collects on leaves and needles and falls to the ground. The classic local measurement was made by G.T. Oberlander on Cahill Ridge on the San Francisco Peninsula from July 20 to August 28, 1951. Under three Douglas firs he collected 7 to 17 inches of water, and under one tanoak 59 inches, more than a year's rain on nearby grassland. Those were forest trees on a ridge about 1,000 feet up. No comparable measurement for the Sunset was found.

The fog is thinning. A 2010 study by Johnstone and Dawson found summer fog hours on the California coast fell about 33 percent over the past century.

Sources: [USGS Pacific Coastal Fog Project](https://www.usgs.gov/centers/western-geographic-science-center/science/pacific-coastal-fog-project), [Wikipedia, Fog drip (citing Oberlander, Ecology, 1956)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fog_drip).

## Street trees in the Sunset: why so few, and who plants them

The Sunset has far fewer street trees than most San Francisco neighborhoods. That is the finding of the city's street tree census, EveryTreeSF, carried out from January 2016 to January 2017 by SF Planning, Public Works, Friends of the Urban Forest and the firm ArborPro. It counted 124,795 street trees citywide and about 40,000 empty spots where a tree could go. Public Works says the Sunset and Richmond, along with southeast neighborhoods, have the fewest trees and the most open planting sites. The sources opened for this note report the gap without spelling out its cause. Local gardeners describe the conditions any Sunset tree faces: dry, nutrient-poor sand and salt wind.

Since voters passed Proposition E in 2016, Public Works maintains every street tree. New trees are planted with separate money: 655 trees in a 2021 round and about 408 in fiscal 2024, each watered weekly for three years. Friends of the Urban Forest runs neighborhood plantings where residents request a tree for their sidewalk.

Sources: [SF Planning, street tree census press release](https://sfplanning.org/press-release/first-comprehensive-census-san-franciscos-street-trees-reveal-higher-number-estimated), [SF County Transportation Authority, Tree Planting FY24 project form](https://www.sfcta.org/sites/default/files/2023-09/EP%2020%20Tree%20Planting%20FY24%20PIF%20DRAFT.pdf), [SF County Transportation Authority blog, June 2021](https://www.sfcta.org/blogs/transportation-authority-board-approves-15-million-plant-over-600-new-trees), [Richmond Review and Sunset Beacon, 2017](https://richmondsunsetnews.com/2017/03/14/street-trees-for-sunset-parkside/), [Sutro Stewards, Gardening in the dunes](https://www.sutrostewards.org/amp/2016/08/31/gardening-in-the-dunes).

## Sunset Boulevard's plantings and the plan to add native habitat

Sunset Boulevard runs two miles from Golden Gate Park to Lake Merced, with about 60 acres of greenbelt. From the 1930s on it was planted with hardy, fast-growing non-native trees and a few natives such as Monterey cypress. Many are now near the end of their lives.

In 2019 Public Works released a plan to renew it: 250 new trees between Judah and Ortega and 100 more on blocks farther along (Monterey cypress and coast live oak among them), rain gardens, and irrigation with recycled water. In fall 2020 the nonprofit CAN!, the California Native Plant Society's Yerba Buena Chapter and Public Works planted two test blocks at 36th Avenue and Taraval and at 36th and Santiago, with four seeded wildflower meadows and more than 500 coastal scrub and oak woodland plants.

In 2025 the Metropolitan Transportation Commission adopted the boulevard as a Priority Conservation Area. The district supervisor's office listed 30 rain gardens, $1 million in state money for recycled-water irrigation and $500,000 from the city for gardeners.

Sources: [CNPS Yerba Buena Chapter, Sunset Boulevard Reimagined](https://cnps-yerbabuena.org/sunset-boulevard-reimagined-biodiversity-corridor/), [Richmond Review and Sunset Beacon, June 2019](https://richmondsunsetnews.com/2019/06/30/new-irrigation-trees-and-native-plants-planned-for-sunset-boulevard/), [Supervisor Joel Engardio's blog, August 2025](https://engardio.com/blog/emerald-necklace).

## The Green Hairstreak butterfly corridor in Golden Gate Heights

The Green Hairstreak Corridor is a string of small native gardens along 14th Avenue in Golden Gate Heights, just up the hill from the Sunset flats. It exists to keep one small butterfly, the green hairstreak, from dying out in San Francisco. Recreation and Parks says it lives in only two parts of the city: the Presidio's coastal bluffs and the Golden Gate Heights ridge.

The nonprofit Nature in the City runs the corridor. It stretches about six blocks, from Hawk Hill at 14th Avenue and Rivera north to a street median at Aloha and Lomita, taking in Golden Gate Heights Park, Rocky Outcrop and Grandview Park, plus medians, stairway gardens and home gardens.

The caterpillars eat almost nothing but coast buckwheat and deerweed. The adults, which fly from March to May, drink nectar from seaside daisy, wild cucumber and coast strawberry. Volunteer workdays are the third Saturday of each month, tools provided.

Sources: [Nature in the City, Green Hairstreak Corridor](https://natureinthecity.org/green-hairstreak), [SF Recreation and Parks, Hawk Hill](https://sfrecpark.org/facilities/facility/details/Hawk-Hill-340).

## The Xerces blue, the butterfly the Sunset dunes lost

The Xerces blue (Glaucopsyche xerces) was a blue butterfly of the coastal sand dunes on the upper San Francisco Peninsula, and it is extinct. Its range ran through the dune country from Fort Funston to Baker Beach, which takes in the land that became the Sunset and Richmond districts. It lived on stabilized sand where its low-growing host plant grew. As the dunes were built over, it disappeared. It was last seen in the early 1940s.

It is often described as one of the first American butterflies known to have been wiped out by people, and the Xerces Society for Invertebrate Conservation, founded in 1971, is named for it.

There is a sequel. Scientists at the California Academy of Sciences used museum specimens to work out the Xerces blue's closest living relative, the silvery blue. In April 2024 the Academy and the Presidio Trust released silvery blues into restored Presidio dunes planted with deerweed, to fill the role the Xerces blue once had.

Sources: [Florida Museum of Natural History, Xerces Blue](https://www.floridamuseum.ufl.edu/100years/xerces-blue/), [ABC7 News, April 2024](https://abc7news.com/post/silvery-blue-butterflies-replace-xerces-in-san-franciscos-presidio-after-species-went-extinct-1940s/14670871/).

## Monarch butterflies on the coast: the decline, and why not to plant milkweed here

Western monarchs spend the winter clustered in trees along the California coast, and their numbers have collapsed. The Xerces Society's Western Monarch Count found about 12,260 butterflies at 249 sites in late 2025, the third-lowest total since the count began in 1997. The two lower years were 2020 (1,901) and 2024 (9,119). In the 1980s there were regularly a few million. Xerces blames loss of overwintering groves, pesticides, climate change and extreme weather.

The advice for coastal gardens surprises people: do not plant milkweed. The Monarch Joint Venture says to plant milkweed only where it grew historically and more than a mile from an overwintering site. Milkweed was rare on the immediate coast, and in mild winters it stays green and keeps monarchs breeding when they should be resting, which spreads a parasite and can leave caterpillars to starve. Sunset neighbors help more by planting native flowers that give nectar from early spring through fall and by not using insecticides.

Sources: [Xerces Society, Western monarch numbers remain at historic low](https://xerces.org/press/western-monarch-numbers-remain-at-historic-low), [Monarch Joint Venture, milkweed near overwintering grounds](https://monarchjointventure.org/faq/planting/i-live-near-the-california-overwintering-grounds-should-i-be-planting-milk).

## Native bees: the yellow-faced bumble bee and the bees that nest in sand

The yellow-faced bumble bee (Bombus vosnesenskii) is a native bumble bee of Bay Area gardens, and city gardens seem to suit it. A study led by Sylvana Finn, using monthly surveys on 15 routes around Berkeley in 2022 and 2023 plus iNaturalist photos, found queens active in 11 months of the year and searching for nest sites in 8 of them. Normally nest-searching lasts about a month in spring. The authors credit urban gardens.

Many native bees and wasps do not live in hives at all. They dig single nests in bare, sunny sand. Bay Nature's account of the Presidio's restored dunes describes bees and thread-waisted wasps nesting in open sandy patches.

SF Environment's Share Nature page links to its pollinator protection guidance and a list of bee-friendly Bay Area nurseries.

Sources: [Dryad dataset, Urban gardens foster year-round activity of bumble bee queens](https://datadryad.org/dataset/doi:10.5061/dryad.qjq2bvqww), [Bay Nature, Learning to love the Presidio's ancient dune ecosystem](https://baynature.org/?p=98026), [SF Environment, Share Nature](https://www.sfenvironment.org/share-nature).

## Salamanders, lizards, snakes and frogs in Sunset yards

The California slender salamander (Batrachoseps attenuatus) is the small amphibian San Francisco gardeners are most likely to turn up. It has a slender body, very short legs and four toes on each foot. The National Park Service calls it common in the Presidio, in woodland and coastal scrub, active mostly at night on moist ground with objects on the surface to hide under. It lays its eggs in moist ground and they hatch in spring. Coyotes eat a few: slender salamanders turned up in the UC Davis scat study.

Reptiles are scarcer. The Presidio's list of species reported in recent years includes the western fence lizard, the San Francisco and California alligator lizards, and the Santa Cruz and coast garter snakes, but the Park Service says reptiles are seldom seen there and snake sightings are almost unheard of. Its amphibian list has the arboreal salamander, yellow-eyed salamander (an ensatina), coast range newt, California toad and bullfrog. The Pacific chorus frog is not on that Presidio list.

Sources: [National Park Service, California Slender Salamander](https://nps.gov/prsf/learn/nature/california-slender-salamander.htm), [National Park Service, Presidio reptiles](https://www.nps.gov/prsf/learn/nature/reptiles.htm), [National Park Service, Presidio amphibians](https://www.nps.gov/prsf/learn/nature/amphibians.htm), [Nature in the City field notes on the coyote diet study](https://natureinthecity.org/field-notes/san-franciscos-coyotes-are-going-after-an-unexpected-source-of-prey-new-study-shows).

## Backyards as habitat, and where Sunset neighbors get native plants

Back yards are the biggest piece of green most Sunset neighbors control. The California Native Plant Society's Yerba Buena Chapter points out why they matter: so much of San Francisco's land is back yards, and so many of them touch each other and nearby parks, that planted together they could work as wildlife corridors. Its Back Yard Natives project asks neighbors to add local native plants, ideally several households in a row, and to put out a bird bath. Nobody has to give up a favorite tree or rose.

Where to get plants: the chapter holds a yearly sale of locally collected natives. The Sutro Native Plant Nursery at 476 Johnstone Drive grows more than 180 California native species and sells its surplus. The city's SF Plant Finder suggests species by address.

SF Environment's Share Nature program maps residents' habitat plantings. The city's goal is for 30 to 40 percent of its land to be permeable by 2030, with at least 30 percent of that as biodiverse green space.

Sources: [CNPS Yerba Buena Chapter, Native plant gardening in San Francisco's back yards](https://cnps-yerbabuena.org/native-plant-gardening-in-san-franciscos-back-yards/), [Sutro Stewards, Nursery](https://www.sutrostewards.org/nursery), [SF Plant Finder](https://sfplanninggis.org/plantsf/), [SF Environment, Share Nature](https://www.sfenvironment.org/share-nature).

## Garden for the Environment and the city's community gardens

Garden for the Environment is the teaching garden nearest the Sunset, at Seventh Avenue and Lawton Street in the Inner Sunset. It was founded in 1990 and is open to the public. The San Francisco Public Utilities Commission funds it, and it serves about 2,500 people a year. Classes cover composting, growing food, beekeeping, fruit tree care and, twice a year, rainwater harvesting. Its Get Up! program is a three-month composting course that takes applications. It runs programs six days a week, two of them for school field trips.

For a plot of your own, the Recreation and Park Department runs 42 community gardens citywide. Most assign individual plots from a wait list, one plot per household, San Francisco residents only. You can join more than one list through the department's sign-up form, and questions go to CommunityGardens@sfgov.org. A few gardens are communal, with drop-in workdays and a shared harvest and no wait list.

Sources: [Richmond Review and Sunset Beacon, Garden for the Environment, December 2024](https://richmondsunsetnews.com/2024/12/12/garden-for-the-environment-cultivating-community-in-the-sunset/), [SF Recreation and Parks, Community Gardens](https://sfrecpark.org/1403/Community-Gardens).

## Invasive plants to know: iceplant, oxalis, cape ivy and pampas grass

Four invasive plants are worth knowing in and around Sunset yards. Iceplant, oxalis and jubata grass were all brought to California as ornamentals.

Iceplant (Carpobrotus edulis), from South Africa, grows in dense mats in the dunes beside Sunset Dunes park. The California Invasive Plant Council rates it High. The mats build up organic matter in the sand, which changes the soil and lets other weeds in, and any broken piece of stem can root.

Yellow oxalis or sourgrass (Oxalis pes-caprae), also South African, sets no seed here. It spreads by small bulbs that stay in the soil, which makes it hard to control. It is rated Moderate and invades dunes, scrub and gardens.

Cape ivy (Delairea odorata) is a vine that smothers shrubs and trees. It resprouts from any fragment and is toxic to animals. Rated High.

Jubata grass, the pink pampas grass (Cortaderia jubata), comes from the Andes. A single plume can release up to 100,000 wind-blown seeds, and it takes over coastal dunes and bluffs. Rated High.

Sources: [Cal-IPC, Carpobrotus edulis](https://www.cal-ipc.org/plants/profile/carpobrotus-edulis-profile/), [Cal-IPC, Oxalis pes-caprae](https://www.cal-ipc.org/plants/profile/oxalis-pes-caprae-profile/), [Cal-IPC, Delairea odorata](https://www.cal-ipc.org/plants/profile/delairea-odorata-profile/), [Cal-IPC, Cortaderia jubata](https://www.cal-ipc.org/plants/profile/cortaderia-jubata-profile/), [Friends of Sunset Dunes, Visit](https://sunsetdunes.org/visit).
