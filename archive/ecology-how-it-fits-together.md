This note describes the connections between the Outer Sunset's animals, plants, weather and people, one relationship per section. Each is something a neighbor can watch for, and each is documented in a published source. The last section lists the ways neighbors add to what is known.

## Gophers feed coyotes, hawks, owls and herons

The pocket gopher is the animal that most of the Sunset's larger hunters depend on. Botta's pocket gophers live under lawns and meadows, where watering can support a lot of them, eating roots and pushing up mounds of soil. That digging mixes nutrients into the ground and lets rain soak in.

Above ground, the gopher is food. In the UC Davis study of 707 coyote scats collected across San Francisco from 2019 to 2022, pocket gopher was the most common natural prey, found in about 57 percent of samples, and coyotes in green areas such as Golden Gate Park relied on natural prey more than coyotes in built-up areas did. The Presidio Trust describes coyotes as natural rodent control. Great blue herons, which nest in Golden Gate Park and at Lake Merced, leave the water to stalk gophers on watered lawns. Red-tailed hawks hunt open grass, and great horned owls take small mammals at night.

Sources: [Bay Nature, The Gopher Underground](https://baynature.org/?p=70174), [University of California news, coyote diet study](https://www.universityofcalifornia.edu/news/dining-out-san-franciscos-coyotes-how-city-reshapes-what-native-predator-eats), [Presidio Trust, Coyotes in the Presidio](https://presidio.gov/about/sustainability/coyotes-in-the-presidio), [National Park Service, Red-tailed Hawk](https://www.nps.gov/prsf/learn/nature/red-tailed-hawk.htm), [National Park Service, Great Horned Owl](https://www.nps.gov/prsf/learn/nature/great-horned-owl.htm).

## Rat poison moves up the food chain to hawks, owls and coyotes

Rat poison set out on one Sunset block can end up in an owl in Golden Gate Park. Anticoagulant baits do not kill at once. A poisoned rat or mouse lives for days, may eat several doses, and grows slow and easy to catch. Whatever eats it takes in the poison. Golden Gate Bird Alliance reports that hawks, owls and other animals are sickened or killed this way every year.

The eaters are known. Rats and mice were in the coyote scats UC Davis collected in San Francisco. Hawks and owls live on the same rodents. Statewide testing cited in California's 2020 law found second-generation anticoagulants in more than 90 percent of mountain lions and 88 percent of bobcats tested.

That evidence is why the state banned most uses of second-generation anticoagulants from January 1, 2021 (AB 1788) and extended limits to first-generation ones from January 1, 2025 (AB 2552). Neighbors break the chain by sealing holes, removing food, and using snap traps in place of bait.

Sources: [Golden Gate Bird Alliance, Prevent Raptor Poisoning](https://goldengatebirdalliance.org/conservation/make-the-city-safe-for-wildlife/rodenticides-and-birds/), [UC Agriculture and Natural Resources on AB 1788](https://ucanr.edu/blog/pests-urban-landscape/article/california-places-further-restrictions-rodenticides), [Pest Management Professional on AB 2552](https://www.mypmp.net/california-passes-another-rodenticide-ban-2/), [Nature in the City field notes](https://natureinthecity.org/field-notes/san-franciscos-coyotes-are-going-after-an-unexpected-source-of-prey-new-study-shows).

## Coyotes, cats, raccoons and the ground birds they affect

Coyotes change what happens to birds by changing the numbers of smaller predators. Biologists call it mesopredator release: take away the top predator and mid-sized hunters such as raccoons, skunks and outdoor cats multiply, and ground-nesting birds pay for it.

San Francisco has run the experiment in both directions. With no coyotes for most of the 1900s, California quail fell from more than 1,200 in Golden Gate Park around 1930 to none in the city by about 2017 or 2018, with cats named as a main cause. After coyotes returned in the early 2000s, Bay Nature reports, cat numbers in the Presidio dropped. A 2021 study led by Kelly Iknayan in the Journal of Applied Ecology found quail much more likely to be present in parks that also had coyotes. San Francisco coyotes do eat raccoons, skunks and some cats.

That finding is the basis for the Presidio's plan to bring quail back, with a first release of 50 birds scheduled for fall 2026.

Sources: [Bay Nature, The Comeback Quail](https://baynature.org/?p=221258), [Golden Gate Bird Alliance, The Gull, Summer 2026](https://goldengatebirdalliance.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/07/The_Gull_Summer_2026_FINAL-1.pdf), [SF Environment, The changing face of avian diversity](https://www.sfenvironment.org/blog/changing-face-avian-diversity), [Nature in the City field notes](https://natureinthecity.org/field-notes/san-franciscos-coyotes-are-going-after-an-unexpected-source-of-prey-new-study-shows).

## Crows, ravens, trash and the shorebirds of Ocean Beach

Trash left at Ocean Beach feeds the birds that threaten its shorebirds. Crows and ravens have multiplied in San Francisco on human food: garbage, fast-food scraps and roadkill. The city's Christmas Bird Count found 5 crows in 1984 and 3,260 in 2025, and a record 1,538 ravens in 2024.

Both birds eat eggs and chicks, and they are a known danger to the western snowy plover, the threatened shorebird that winters at Ocean Beach. At Point Reyes, where plovers nest, the National Park Service found ravens responsible for more than 40 percent of losses at unprotected nests. Plovers do not nest at Ocean Beach, but Golden Gate Bird Alliance wrote in summer 2026 that fast-growing crow numbers are putting new pressure on the plovers there.

The Park Service's plover brochure for Ocean Beach makes the link to people directly. It asks visitors to dispose of garbage properly so as not to attract predators. The Friends of Sunset Dunes ask the same: pack out all trash.

Sources: [KQED, Is it crow-maggedon?](https://www.kqed.org/news/12072433/is-it-crow-maggedon-why-crows-are-flocking-to-bay-area-cities-each-winter), [Golden Gate Bird Alliance, SF Christmas Bird Count 2026 post](https://goldengatebirdalliance.org/blog-posts/sf-christmas-bird-count-2026/), [National Park Service, Park Science article on snowy plovers](https://www.nps.gov/articles/000/western-snowy-plovers-could-face-multiple-threats-from-climate-change.htm), [Golden Gate Bird Alliance, The Gull, Summer 2026](https://goldengatebirdalliance.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/07/The_Gull_Summer_2026_FINAL-1.pdf), [National Park Service, Protecting the Snowy Plover brochure](https://www.nps.gov/goga/planyourvisit/upload/sb-snowy_plover_web.pdf), [Friends of Sunset Dunes, Visit](https://sunsetdunes.org/visit).

## Fog, dune plants and the insects that depend on them

Fog, sand, a few tough plants and a few insects form the oldest chain in the Sunset. The U.S. Geological Survey explains the fog's part: it cools the coast in summer, cuts evaporation and adds moisture when no rain falls, which lowers stress on plants. Dune plants are built for the rest, with small, waxy, hairy or succulent leaves and deep roots for dry, poor sand and salt wind.

Insects are tied to particular dune plants. Green hairstreak caterpillars eat almost only coast buckwheat and deerweed, and the adults take nectar from seaside daisy and coast strawberry, which is why the butterfly survives in San Francisco only where those plants do: the Presidio bluffs and the Golden Gate Heights ridge above the Sunset. Bees and wasps nest in bare sand between the plants. When the Sunset and Richmond dunes were built over, the Xerces blue butterfly, which needed a low-growing dune plant, went extinct in the early 1940s.

Sources: [USGS Pacific Coastal Fog Project](https://www.usgs.gov/centers/western-geographic-science-center/science/pacific-coastal-fog-project), [Sutro Stewards, Gardening in the dunes](https://www.sutrostewards.org/amp/2016/08/31/gardening-in-the-dunes), [Nature in the City, Green Hairstreak Corridor](https://natureinthecity.org/green-hairstreak), [SF Recreation and Parks, Hawk Hill](https://sfrecpark.org/facilities/facility/details/Hawk-Hill-340), [Bay Nature, Presidio dune ecosystem](https://baynature.org/?p=98026), [Florida Museum of Natural History, Xerces Blue](https://www.floridamuseum.ufl.edu/100years/xerces-blue/).

## Beachgrass, iceplant, sand and snowy plovers

What grows on a dune decides which animals can use it. The National Park Service lists the spread of European dune grass among the threats to the western snowy plover and its beach habitat. The California Native Plant Society's Yerba Buena Chapter names European beach grass and iceplant as the two invaders that replace native dune plants in San Francisco, and with them the birds, butterflies and beetles that depend on those plants.

Iceplant grows in the dunes beside Sunset Dunes park. The California Invasive Plant Council says its mats build up organic matter in sand and open the way for more weeds. In the Presidio, after dunes were rebuilt in Lobos Valley, the endangered San Francisco lessingia rebounded, and bees and wasps nest in the open sandy patches.

The work now under way beside Sunset Dunes park runs the other direction. In winter 2024 to 2025 more than 1,500 native beach wildrye grasses were planted, in a project led by Recreation and Parks with volunteers.

Sources: [National Park Service, Protecting the Snowy Plover brochure](https://www.nps.gov/goga/planyourvisit/upload/sb-snowy_plover_web.pdf), [CNPS Yerba Buena Chapter, Coastal dune scrub](https://cnps-yerbabuena.org/coastal-dune-scrub/), [Cal-IPC, Carpobrotus edulis](https://www.cal-ipc.org/plants/profile/carpobrotus-edulis-profile/), [Bay Nature, Presidio dune ecosystem](https://baynature.org/?p=98026), [Friends of Sunset Dunes, Visit](https://sunsetdunes.org/visit).

## Dogs, kelp wrack and resting shorebirds at Ocean Beach

The western snowy plover spends up to ten months a year at Ocean Beach, roughly July to May. The six-inch bird was listed as threatened in March 1993. The Park Service says up to 100 of the roughly 2,300 on the Pacific Coast use Golden Gate National Recreation Area.

Plovers do not nest here. They rest in footprint-sized hollows in the dry upper beach and fatten up for breeding by eating small invertebrates in the kelp and debris the tide leaves. That is why the Park Service asks people to leave kelp and driftwood on the sand. A plover sees a dog as a predator and flies. Each flight burns stored fat, and repeated scares can cost it the next breeding season.

So the Snowy Plover Protection Area runs from Stairwell 21, just south of the Beach Chalet, to Sloat Boulevard. Dogs must be leashed there in season. People are asked to walk on the wet sand.

Sources: [National Park Service, Protecting the Snowy Plover brochure](https://www.nps.gov/goga/planyourvisit/upload/sb-snowy_plover_web.pdf), [Golden Gate Bird Alliance, The Gull, Summer 2026](https://goldengatebirdalliance.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/07/The_Gull_Summer_2026_FINAL-1.pdf).

## Backyards and Sunset Boulevard as a corridor between Golden Gate Park and Lake Merced

The Outer Sunset sits between two large habitats, Golden Gate Park on the north and Lake Merced on the south, and the question is what lets wildlife move between them. Two things are documented.

The first is Sunset Boulevard. It runs two miles from the park to the lake with about 60 acres of planted greenbelt. The California Native Plant Society's Yerba Buena Chapter, which helped plant native test blocks at 36th Avenue and Taraval and at Santiago in 2020, states the aim plainly: restore dune and scrub plants so that scrub birds and butterflies can return, and link the west end of Golden Gate Park with Lake Merced. In 2025 the boulevard was named a Priority Conservation Area.

The second is back yards. The same chapter notes that San Francisco's yards touch each other and nearby parks, and that neighbors planting natives side by side could turn them into corridors. Coyote families already hold territories that average about two square miles, according to coyote watcher Janet Kessler.

Sources: [CNPS Yerba Buena Chapter, Sunset Boulevard Reimagined](https://cnps-yerbabuena.org/sunset-boulevard-reimagined-biodiversity-corridor/), [Supervisor Joel Engardio's blog, August 2025](https://engardio.com/blog/emerald-necklace), [CNPS Yerba Buena Chapter, back yards](https://cnps-yerbabuena.org/native-plant-gardening-in-san-franciscos-back-yards/), [Potrero View, August 2024](https://www.potreroview.net/?p=8461).

## Night lighting, glass and migrating birds

Lights left on at night in fall and spring affect birds that are only passing over the Sunset. More than 250 species migrate through the Bay region, and SF Environment notes that many songbirds travel at night and drop into the nearest good cover after crossing water. San Francisco Planning cites estimates of up to a billion birds a year killed by window collisions in North America, and the city adopted bird-safe building standards in 2011.

Golden Gate Bird Alliance's Lights Out for Birds seasons are February 15 to May 30 and August 15 to November 30. The ask is to turn off or shade non-essential lights from dusk to dawn. The group cites a Chicago study in which darkening one high-rise cut bird deaths there by 80 percent.

City noise matters too. White-crowned sparrows in San Francisco raised the pitch of their songs over decades of rising traffic noise, and sang lower and softer when traffic stopped in spring 2020.

Sources: [Golden Gate Bird Alliance, Lights Out San Francisco](https://goldengatebirdalliance.org/conservation/make-the-city-safe-for-wildlife/learn-about-lights-out-san-francisco/), [SF Environment, Fall migration](https://www.sfenvironment.org/blog/fall-migration), [San Francisco Planning, Standards for Bird-Safe Buildings](https://sfplanning.org/standards-bird-safe-buildings), [Smithsonian Magazine, September 2020](https://www.smithsonianmag.com/science-nature/pandemic-shutdown-san-francisco-had-sparrows-singing-sexier-tunes-180975913/).

## Cats and birds in Sunset yards

A cat let outdoors in the Sunset is both a hunter and prey. As a hunter: a study by the Smithsonian Conservation Biology Institute and the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service estimated outdoor cats kill up to 3.7 billion birds a year in the lower 48 states, about 70 percent of them taken by unowned cats. Several of the Sunset's common yard birds live low: the dark-eyed junco nests on the ground, and the California towhee and white-crowned sparrow feed on open ground near shrubs.

As prey: domestic cat was found in 4.5 percent of the San Francisco coyote scats UC Davis analyzed, 32 detections among 707 samples.

The city has not resolved the feral cat question. The San Francisco SPCA runs a trap-neuter-release program, and Golden Gate Bird Alliance has asked that feeding stations and release be kept out of the most important natural areas. Its 2026 newsletter calls for humane, science-based answers. Keeping a pet cat indoors settles both problems for that cat.

Sources: [Golden Gate Bird Alliance, Number-One Killer of Birds is Outdoor Cats](https://goldengatebirdalliance.org/blog-posts/number-one-killer-of-birds-is-outdoor-cats/), [Nature in the City field notes](https://natureinthecity.org/field-notes/san-franciscos-coyotes-are-going-after-an-unexpected-source-of-prey-new-study-shows), [National Park Service, Dark-eyed Junco](https://www.nps.gov/prsf/learn/nature/dark-eyed-junco.htm), [National Park Service, California Towhee](https://www.nps.gov/prsf/learn/nature/california-towhee.htm), [National Park Service, White-crowned Sparrow](https://www.nps.gov/prsf/learn/nature/white-crowned-sparrow.htm), [Golden Gate Bird Alliance, The Gull, Summer 2026](https://goldengatebirdalliance.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/07/The_Gull_Summer_2026_FINAL-1.pdf).

## The west end of Golden Gate Park as a stopover for migrants

The west end of Golden Gate Park, with its lakes and woods near the ocean, works as a resting place for birds on the move. The park lies on the Pacific Flyway and holds about 680 acres of forest, 130 acres of meadow and 33 acres of lakes, and more than 250 bird species have been recorded in it. SF Environment explains the mechanism: night-flying songbirds heading south in September and October come down in the nearest good habitat once they have crossed water, and may stay a day or a whole winter.

North Lake is a long-known birding spot for herons, ducks and kingfishers, and a University of San Francisco class there in October 2022 found wintering Townsend's warblers alongside resident pygmy nuthatches. The sources opened for this note describe this stopover pattern. They do not use the birders' phrase "migrant trap" for the park, and no published count of vagrants for the west end was found.

Sources: [USF Ornithology blog, Golden Gate Park Chain of Lakes](https://usfblogs.usfca.edu/ornithology/?p=120), [SF Environment, Fall migration](https://www.sfenvironment.org/blog/fall-migration), [Greenbelt Alliance, West end of Golden Gate Park](https://www.greenbelt.org/blog/urban-outings-west-end-of-golden-gate-park/).

## Sunset Dunes park and wildlife: what has been reported so far

Sunset Dunes opened in April 2025 on the former Upper Great Highway. Its effect on wildlife has been argued over but, as far as this research found, not yet measured in a published study.

What is on record: Friends of Sunset Dunes counts more than 1.7 million visits in the first year. Golden Gate Bird Alliance wrote in summer 2026 that the park has made Ocean Beach busier and that plover advocates are "working overtime". Its volunteers and the National Park Service's B.A.R.K. program talk with dog owners about leash rules. In a letter to the Sunset Beacon in May 2025, an Outer Sunset neighbor argued that foot traffic and loose dogs in the dunes are harming plovers. The letter cited no data, and other letter writers have argued the opposite.

Two facts help sort it out. Snowy plovers do not nest at Ocean Beach, and they rest on the beach, not in the dunes. And native dune planting has begun beside the old roadway.

Sources: [KQED, One year of San Francisco's beachside park](https://www.kqed.org/news/12079238/one-year-of-san-franciscos-controversial-beachside-park), [Golden Gate Bird Alliance, The Gull, Summer 2026](https://goldengatebirdalliance.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/07/The_Gull_Summer_2026_FINAL-1.pdf), [Sunset Beacon letter, May 28, 2025](https://richmondsunsetnews.com/2025/05/28/letter-to-the-editor-snowy-plover-being-harmed-by-sunset-dunes/), [Friends of Sunset Dunes, Visit](https://sunsetdunes.org/visit), [National Park Service, Protecting the Snowy Plover brochure](https://www.nps.gov/goga/planyourvisit/upload/sb-snowy_plover_web.pdf).

## Climate change effects already measured on this coast

Three climate-linked changes that touch the Outer Sunset have been measured or documented.

Fog: a 2010 study by Johnstone and Dawson, summarized by the U.S. Geological Survey, found summer fog hours on the California coast fell by about 33 percent over the last century. Fog cools the coast and supplies moisture in the dry season, so less fog means warmer, drier summers for plants that count on it.

Erosion: KQED reports that the land under the Great Highway is being lost to the ocean and that the lower portion of the road is already closed because of coastal erosion. The National Park Service names sea level rise as a cause of habitat loss for snowy plovers.

Predators and heat: a Park Service and Point Blue study of plover nests at Point Reyes from 2002 to 2020 found that warmer conditions went with lower nest survival, apparently because ravens were more active, while nests inside protective fencing were unaffected.

Sources: [USGS Pacific Coastal Fog Project](https://www.usgs.gov/centers/western-geographic-science-center/science/pacific-coastal-fog-project), [KQED, One year of San Francisco's beachside park](https://www.kqed.org/news/12079238/one-year-of-san-franciscos-controversial-beachside-park), [National Park Service, Park Science article on snowy plovers](https://www.nps.gov/articles/000/western-snowy-plovers-could-face-multiple-threats-from-climate-change.htm).

## How neighbors add to what is known

Much of what is known about Sunset wildlife comes from neighbors writing it down.

iNaturalist and the City Nature Challenge: the challenge began in 2016 as a contest between the California Academy of Sciences and the Natural History Museum of Los Angeles County. In 2026 it ran April 24 to 27 in 754 cities. In the San Francisco Bay Area, 3,003 observers logged 63,589 observations of 3,911 species. The most photographed was the black-crowned night heron, and one find in San Francisco was the first observation anywhere of a golden gate leech.

Christmas Bird Count: next on Monday, December 28, 2026. In 2025, 214 birders counted 94,315 birds of 183 species in San Francisco.

Coyotes: a UC Berkeley study of human and coyote conflict was built on nearly 4,000 reports residents sent to San Francisco Animal Care and Control from 2012 to 2022. The Presidio Trust also asks for sightings on iNaturalist.

Snowy plovers: the National Park Service takes volunteers to monitor plovers at 415-561-4755.

Sources: [California Academy of Sciences, City Nature Challenge 2026 results](https://www.calacademy.org/press/releases/city-nature-challenge-records-over-3-million-wildlife-observations-from-more-than), [Golden Gate Bird Alliance, SF Christmas Bird Count 2026 post](https://goldengatebirdalliance.org/blog-posts/sf-christmas-bird-count-2026/), [UC Berkeley Research](https://vcresearch.berkeley.edu/node/28451), [Presidio Trust, Coyotes in the Presidio](https://presidio.gov/about/sustainability/coyotes-in-the-presidio), [National Park Service, Protecting the Snowy Plover brochure](https://www.nps.gov/goga/planyourvisit/upload/sb-snowy_plover_web.pdf).
