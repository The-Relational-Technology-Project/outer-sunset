This note covers who was allowed to buy and live in the Sunset District from the 1920s to the 1960s, and how that changed. It separates what is documented for the Sunset itself (deeds, federal maps, census samples, club letters, pickets) from better-known cases elsewhere on the west side and the Peninsula. The builders and their houses are in a separate note, "How the Sunset got built."

## Racial covenants in Sunset District deeds: what is documented, block by block

Racial covenants (deed clauses barring non-white owners or occupants) are documented in the Sunset, though not everywhere. San Francisco planners who sampled deeds found that Sunset builders mostly did not use them until the late 1930s. Henry Doelger's 1932 houses on 31st Avenue between Lawton and Moraga, and Oliver Rousseau's 1932 houses on 36th Avenue between Kirkham and Lawton, carried no such clause. Neither did Chris McKeon's 1936 "Rivera Heights" tract around 29th Avenue and Rivera, or a 1938 tract at 32nd Avenue and Quintara.

By 1939 the clause looks standard in Doelger's tracts. Deeds on the 1800 block of 30th Avenue said the house could not be sold to, rented to or occupied by anyone not of the "White or Caucasian race." An earlier exception is Fernando Nelson's Parkway Terrace (27th to 32nd Avenues, Lincoln to Irving), restricted in the 1910s. The planners list Doelger and Standard Building among west side builders who used covenants or discriminatory sales practices. No full count of Sunset deeds has been published.

Sources: [SF Planning, Sunset District Residential Builders 1925-1950](https://default.sfplanning.org/Preservation/sunset_survey/Adopted_SunsetHCS.pdf), [SF Planning, Doelger Building landmark report](https://default.sfplanning.org/Preservation/landmarks_designation/Approved_Doelger_Final_LM_Report.pdf).

## The Gellerts' whites-only covenant at Lakeshore Park, 1941, and deed restrictions in Golden Gate Heights

The clearest published covenant text from a Sunset builder comes from just outside the Sunset. In 1941 the Gellert brothers' Standard Building Company, which built Sunstream Homes across the Sunset, recorded restrictions on every lot in Lakeshore Park, south of Sloat Boulevard at Sunset Boulevard. The declaration, filed August 1, 1941, allowed only single-family houses, banned businesses, set minimum building values, and said no one other than those "wholly of the Caucasian race" could use or live in the tract. Servants of other races were exempt. The company advertised the tract as a "de luxe subdivision" with the kind of restrictions found in expensive residence parks.

Inside the Sunset, a 1937 federal appraiser wrote that Golden Gate Heights, then being developed by Doelger and Standard Building, was "protected by deed restrictions." The appraiser did not say whether those restrictions were racial, about building type, or both.

Sources: [Western Neighborhoods Project, The Gellert Brothers and Lakeshore Park](https://www.outsidelands.org/lakeshore.php), [Mapping Inequality, HOLC area description data (San Francisco B23)](https://github.com/americanpanorama/HOLC_Area_Description_Data).

## The 1937 HOLC "residential security" map: how the Sunset was graded

The federal Home Owners' Loan Corporation (HOLC) graded San Francisco neighborhoods for lenders in 1937. No part of the Sunset was redlined (graded D, red). Most of the central Sunset and Parkside got B (blue, second grade): area B15 along the park and Sunset Boulevard, called a "high blue"; B16, 84 blocks on former dunes, 70 percent built; B17, 40 blocks with ocean views; and Golden Gate Heights (B23), a provisional blue. The western end of the Sunset near the ocean (C15) and the older Inner Sunset (C16) got C (yellow, third grade).

Race was part of the scoring. Of B16 the appraisers wrote that there were "no racial problems" and little chance of any. Of the western end they wrote that it was "free from undesirable racial conditions." The C grade there came from mixed older houses, "only fair" transit and doubts about construction quality, not from who lived there. Several Sunset entries list fog and wind as drawbacks and note many FHA loans.

Sources: [Mapping Inequality, HOLC area description data (San Francisco B15, B16, B17, B23, C15, C16)](https://github.com/americanpanorama/HOLC_Area_Description_Data), [SF Planning, Sunset District Residential Builders 1925-1950](https://default.sfplanning.org/Preservation/sunset_survey/Adopted_SunsetHCS.pdf).

## FHA loan rules and why Sunset builders had a money reason to exclude

The Sunset was built on FHA-insured mortgages, and FHA rules rewarded all-white tracts. The Federal Housing Administration (1934) insured long, low-down-payment loans, which let policemen, clerks and salesmen buy $5,000 houses. Its appraisal standards, borrowed from the HOLC, gave top marks to new, uniform neighborhoods and marked down areas with foreign-born residents or racial minorities. City planners write that FHA appraisal policy discouraged insured loans in such areas, which gave builders a strong financial incentive to restrict new tracts to white buyers.

The planners also suggest Doelger may have added covenants in the late 1930s to lend an air of exclusivity to his cheaper, plainer houses. The Sunset's blue and green ratings, they conclude, directly helped its fast growth and its builders' success. The same system steered loans away from older, mixed neighborhoods elsewhere in the city. The planners say this kind of redlining lasted into the mid-1960s. This is the general national pattern as applied to the Sunset. No Sunset-specific FHA case files were found for this note.

Sources: [SF Planning, Sunset District Residential Builders 1925-1950](https://default.sfplanning.org/Preservation/sunset_survey/Adopted_SunsetHCS.pdf), [SF Planning, Doelger Building landmark report](https://default.sfplanning.org/Preservation/landmarks_designation/Approved_Doelger_Final_LM_Report.pdf).

## Who lived in the Sunset in 1940: the census sample, Irish and Italian families, and the parishes

In the 1940 census, the new Sunset tracts were all white. City planners studied six builder tracts (12 to 44 houses each) in the central Sunset and found every resident listed as "White." About 89 percent were U.S. citizens, most born in California. The immigrants came mainly from western Europe, many from Ireland and Italy. Only 3 percent of houses were rented. Men worked as salesmen, clerks, policemen and firemen, earning around $2,000 a year, and nearly 40 percent of women worked outside the home. On the Rousseau blocks (34th to 36th Avenues), the 1940 census also shows only white residents.

Catholic parishes grew with the houses. St. Gabriel, at 2550 41st Avenue, was founded in 1941 with a first Mass in a hardware store on Taraval Street. Its school opened in 1948 and had about 1,200 pupils in the 1950s, which the school calls the largest Catholic elementary enrollment west of the Mississippi. Block-level 1950 census figures for the Sunset were not found for this note.

Sources: [SF Planning, Sunset District Residential Builders 1925-1950](https://default.sfplanning.org/Preservation/sunset_survey/Adopted_SunsetHCS.pdf), [SF Planning, Rousseaus' Boulevard Tract draft report](https://default.sfplanning.org/Preservation/rousseau/rousseau_landmark_district_report_draft.pdf), [St. Gabriel School, Our History](https://stgabrielsf.com/about-us/our-history).

## Neighborhood groups and exclusion: the Parkside District Improvement Club letter of 1943

Exclusion in the Sunset was not only a matter of builders' deeds. The 2008 historic study of the Parkside (the Sunset's southern half) says some residents and community groups tried to keep minorities from buying or even living in the Sunset and Parkside from the 1920s into the 1950s. Its documented example is a letter of May 17, 1943, in which the Parkside District Improvement Club asked the Real Estate Association of San Francisco how it could prevent African Americans and Filipino Americans from buying houses in the district. The letter survives in the club's own correspondence files.

The club was the Parkside's main civic voice. In the same years it lobbied for park improvements, new schools and better streetcar and bus service. The same study notes that Chinese American families began buying in the Parkside in the 1970s and 1980s. The study does not say what the real estate association answered.

Sources: [Parkside District Historic Context Statement, 2008](https://archives.sfplanning.org/documents/4976-parkside-statement%20march%202008.pdf).

## Shelley v. Kraemer, 1948: what changed for Sunset covenants and what did not

On May 3, 1948, the U.S. Supreme Court ruled in Shelley v. Kraemer that courts could not enforce racial covenants. The Court did not erase the clauses or forbid people from following them voluntarily. It held only that a judge enforcing one was state action barred by the Fourteenth Amendment. So the words stayed in Sunset deeds, and a seller, builder or agent could still simply refuse a buyer.

San Francisco planners write that exclusion on the west side continued "unofficially" after 1948. Their example is the Standard Building Company, one of the Sunset's largest builders, which was accused of refusing to show or sell houses to African Americans well after the ruling. Two Sunset markers show how slowly practice followed the law: the first Black family on the Rousseau blocks arrived in 1957, and pickets stood outside Standard's 19th Avenue office in 1961. California's own anti-discrimination laws came later: the Unruh Act in 1959 and the Rumford Fair Housing Act in 1963.

Sources: [Wikipedia, Shelley v. Kraemer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shelley_v._Kraemer), [SF Planning, Sunset District Residential Builders 1925-1950](https://default.sfplanning.org/Preservation/sunset_survey/Adopted_SunsetHCS.pdf), [FoundSF, Battle of Vista Francisco](https://foundsf.org/Battle_of_Vista_Francisco), [BlackPast, The Rumford Act](https://www.blackpast.org/african-american-history/california-fair-housing-act-rumford-act-1963-1968/).

## The first Black family on the Rousseau blocks, 1957

The Rousseau blocks beside Sunset Boulevard (34th to 36th Avenues, Kirkham to Lawton) never had racial covenants in their deeds, according to the city's draft landmark report. Even so, the report says the first African American family did not move in until 1957, when Audrey and Josephine Cole bought the corner house at 36th Avenue and Lawton that Oliver Rousseau had built for his own family. The Coles lived there for 52 years. The report takes this from Lorri Ungaretti's book "Stories in the Sand."

The gap between 1933 and 1957 shows that a missing covenant did not mean an open block. The report gives no further explanation. It also notes a 1938 marriage license that points to a possible earlier non-white resident on 36th Avenue, perhaps living there as household help, but it could not confirm this.

No published account was found naming the first Black, Chinese American or Japanese American homeowners in the Outer Sunset west of Sunset Boulevard.

Sources: [SF Planning, Rousseaus' Boulevard Tract draft report](https://default.sfplanning.org/Preservation/rousseau/rousseau_landmark_district_report_draft.pdf).

## Willie Brown's 1961 sit-in and the pickets at the Sunstream builder's 19th Avenue office

In 1961 one of the Sunset's largest builders was caught refusing Black buyers. Salesmen for the Gellerts' Standard Building Company kept leaving an open house in its Forest Knolls tract (outside the Sunset) whenever Black visitors approached. Willie Brown, then a young lawyer and later mayor, went in with friends and waited, day after day, in what he called the city's first sit-in. Pickets followed at Forest Knolls and at the company's headquarters at 2222 19th Avenue in the Sunset.

A company spokesperson told the Examiner (June 3, 1961) that the firm had "not sold in the past to Negroes" and had not decided about the future. Under pressure from the mayor and civic groups, Standard promised to stop. In 1966 the state Fair Employment Practices Commission accused it of a deliberate whites-only policy at Serramonte in Daly City, and pickets returned. This is the best documented proof that a Sunset builder discriminated after 1948, though the houses at issue were not in the Sunset.

Sources: [Western Neighborhoods Project, The Gellert Brothers and Lakeshore Park](https://www.outsidelands.org/lakeshore.php), [FoundSF, Battle of Vista Francisco](https://foundsf.org/Battle_of_Vista_Francisco), [SF Planning, Sunset District Residential Builders 1925-1950](https://default.sfplanning.org/Preservation/sunset_survey/Adopted_SunsetHCS.pdf).

## Sing Sheng and the Southwood vote, 1952 (South San Francisco, not the Sunset)

The Sing Sheng case is often told alongside Sunset history, but it happened in South San Francisco. In 1952 Sing Sheng, a former Chinese Nationalist army officer who had come to the United States in 1947, and his wife Grace, a Chinese American, tried to move from San Francisco's Chinatown to a house in the all-white Southwood tract. When neighbors objected, Sheng proposed that they vote, saying he was sure everyone believed in democracy. The ballots, counted in a neighborhood garage, went 174 to 28 against the family.

The Shengs accepted the result and did not go to court, though by then covenants could not be enforced. The vote drew national coverage, including Edward R. Murrow's television program and the Congressional Record. Other towns offered the family homes, and they settled in Menlo Park.

The case shows what Chinese American families leaving Chinatown faced around the Bay in the early 1950s. No similar vote is documented in the Sunset.

Sources: [San Mateo Daily Journal, Jim Clifford, December 4, 2017](https://www.smdailyjournal.com/news/local/when-south-city-drew-national-attention-it-didn-t-need/article_adc3fde4-d880-11e7-a73f-9387a53a1bfa.html).

## Willie Mays is refused a house, 1957 (Sherwood Forest, not the Sunset)

The best-known west side housing refusal involved Willie Mays, and it was not in the Sunset. In November 1957, as the Giants prepared to move from New York, Mays and his wife Marghuerite offered $37,500, the asking price, for a new house at 175 Miraloma Drive in Sherwood Forest. The builder accepted, then backed out after neighbors objected that a Black family would lower property values. KPIX filmed the story on November 14, 1957. Mays told reporters he had not expected so much trouble buying a place to live.

Mayor George Christopher pressed for the sale, the story ran nationally, and the builder reversed himself. The Mayses moved in. In June 1959 someone threw a bottle through their front window with a note that mentioned his race. (One source places the house in "Miraloma Park." Most say Sherwood Forest.)

The case matters for the Sunset because it shows the west side's climate nine years after covenants lost legal force, and for a man the whole city wanted.

Sources: [SF State Bay Area Television Archive, KPIX film, November 14, 1957](https://library.sfsu.edu/bata-db/willie-mays-buys-house-sherwood-forest), [CBS News, June 19, 2024](https://www.cbsnews.com/sacramento/news/willie-mays-san-francisco-housing-discrimination), [The Baseball Bloggess, 175 Miraloma Drive](https://thebaseballbloggess.com/2020/04/09/175-miraloma-drive/), [Eichler Network, When Willie Wasn't Welcome](https://www.eichlernetwork.com/article/when-willie-wasnt-welcome).

## The Rumford Fair Housing Act (1963), Proposition 14 (1964) and how the Sunset voted

California's Rumford Fair Housing Act passed on September 20, 1963. It banned racial discrimination in public housing and larger residential buildings, though legislators exempted most single-family houses. The California Real Estate Association answered with Proposition 14 on the November 3, 1964 ballot, which wrote into the state constitution an owner's right to refuse to sell or rent to anyone. It passed with 65.39 percent statewide.

San Francisco voted yes by a narrower margin: 165,155 to 147,151, or 52.88 percent. For the Sunset itself, a 2024 Sunset Beacon history piece by Joe Castrovinci says the vote ran about 2 to 1 in favor, matching the state, and that in 1964 the Sunset was 85.8 percent white against 74 percent citywide. Precinct returns were not checked for this note, so treat the Sunset figure as that writer's report.

The California Supreme Court struck down Proposition 14 in 1966, and the U.S. Supreme Court agreed in Reitman v. Mulkey (1967). Voters formally repealed it in 1974.

Sources: [BlackPast, The Rumford Act](https://www.blackpast.org/african-american-history/california-fair-housing-act-rumford-act-1963-1968/), [Wikipedia, 1964 California Proposition 14](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1964_California_Proposition_14), [Sunset Beacon, From Sand Dunes and Discrimination, February 9, 2024](https://richmondsunsetnews.com/2024/02/09/from-sand-dunes-and-discrimination-to-million-dollar-homes-and-diversity/).

## The Fair Housing Act of 1968, and the racial covenants still sitting in Sunset deeds

The federal Fair Housing Act, part of the Civil Rights Act of 1968, banned racial discrimination in housing sales and rentals nationwide. With the Rumford Act restored by the courts, a Sunset builder, agent or owner could no longer legally turn a buyer away because of race. The law did not undo what came before. Families shut out from the 1930s through the 1950s missed the years when a Sunset house cost $5,000 to $12,000 with a small down payment.

Old covenant wording can still sit in the recorded documents for a Sunset house, especially one built from the late 1930s on. It has had no force in court since 1948. San Francisco's Office of the Assessor-Recorder lists a service to "remove discriminatory racial covenants," which strikes unlawful language from recorded documents. Ask that office how to look up the deed or the tract's "declaration of restrictions" for your lot. Early 1930s houses, including the Rousseau blocks and Doelger's 31st Avenue tract, were found to have none.

Sources: [BlackPast, The Rumford Act](https://www.blackpast.org/african-american-history/california-fair-housing-act-rumford-act-1963-1968/), [SF Office of the Assessor-Recorder](https://www.sf.gov/departments--assessor-recorder), [SF Planning, Sunset District Residential Builders 1925-1950](https://default.sfplanning.org/Preservation/sunset_survey/Adopted_SunsetHCS.pdf), [Western Neighborhoods Project, The Gellert Brothers and Lakeshore Park](https://www.outsidelands.org/lakeshore.php).

## How the Sunset opened after the 1960s: Chinese American families and today's neighborhood

The Sunset's population began to change in the late 1960s, after the fair housing laws and the 1965 immigration act. Chinese American families bought into the Parkside in the 1970s and 1980s, and by the 2000 census the Parkside was 54 percent Asian, 41 percent white, 4 percent Hispanic and 1 percent African American. Wikipedia's summary says about half of Sunset residents are now Asian American, mostly of Chinese descent, with shopping streets on Irving (19th to 26th Avenues), Noriega and Taraval. The 2008 Parkside study noted that since district elections returned in 2000, the only supervisors of Asian descent had come from District 4 (Sunset and Parkside).

In November 2017 the Chinese Historical Society of America opened an exhibit, "Chinese in the Sunset," at the Sunset Recreation Center. It covered the restrictions that once kept Chinese families out and how they ended.

The record for Black families is thinner. The Parkside was still 1 percent African American in 2000.

Sources: [Parkside District Historic Context Statement, 2008](https://archives.sfplanning.org/documents/4976-parkside-statement%20march%202008.pdf), [Wikipedia, History of Chinese Americans in San Francisco](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_Chinese_Americans_in_San_Francisco), [Wikipedia, Sunset District](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sunset_District,_San_Francisco), [Sunset Beacon, Chinese Legacy in the Sunset, January 7, 2018](https://richmondsunsetnews.com/2018/01/07/chinese-legacy-in-the-sunset-2/).
