This note covers the animals a neighbor meets on the sand and in the surf zone of Ocean Beach, from the Cliff House to Fort Funston: plovers, shorebirds, gulls, sand crabs, the things that wash up, and the rules (dogs, fires) that shape how people and wildlife share the beach. It says who eats whom and which months each neighbor is here.

## Western snowy plovers at Ocean Beach: when they are here, where they rest, why they do not nest

The western snowy plover (Charadrius alexandrinus nivosus, the name the Park Service uses) is a six-inch, sand-colored shorebird that rests on Ocean Beach about ten months of the year. The U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service listed it as threatened in 1993. Birds start arriving in July and leave by May; June is often the only month with none. In 2020 the National Park Service counted the last wintering birds on April 29 and the first returning birds on July 7. The Snowy Plover Protection Area runs from Stairwell 21 south to Sloat Boulevard. Plovers sit on the dry upper beach, often tucked into footprints out of the wind, and feed on beach flies, sand fleas and other small invertebrates picked from sand and seaweed. They do not nest here. Bay Nature reported that Ocean Beach is too busy with people for eggs laid on open sand to survive, so the birds go to quieter beaches in Monterey and Santa Cruz counties and at Point Reyes to breed.

Sources: [NPS, Ocean Beach](https://www.nps.gov/goga/planyourvisit/oceanbeach.htm), [NPS, Another Strong Year for Snowy Plovers (2020)](https://www.nps.gov/articles/000/strong-2019-2020-golden-gate-plover-season.htm), [NPS, Western Snowy Plover Monitoring](https://www.nps.gov/articles/western-snowy-plover-monitoring-goga.htm), [Bay Nature, Welcome Back, Snowy Plovers](https://baynature.org/?p=70303).

## Snowy plover numbers counted at Ocean Beach, year by year

National Park Service biologists have counted wintering snowy plovers at Ocean Beach since 1994. The long-run average is about 30 birds per winter survey. Around 2010, Bay Nature reported 20 to 30 birds each season. In January 2016 counters found as many as 104 plovers in one day, the record. The winter of 2016 to 2017 averaged about 60 per survey, and the next winter about 50. In 2019 to 2020 the average was about 49, with a high count of 96, the second highest on record and the sixth winter in a row above average. Crissy Field holds far fewer: a record 12 in 2019 to 2020. Many Ocean Beach birds come from around Monterey; some come from Oregon or from as far south as Santa Barbara. Leg bands let biologists follow individuals. One plover banded during the 2007 Cosco Busan oil spill was still returning to Golden Gate beaches in 2018, at least ten years old. Counts for winters after 2020 were not found for this note.

Sources: [NPS, Another Strong Year for Snowy Plovers (2020)](https://www.nps.gov/articles/000/strong-2019-2020-golden-gate-plover-season.htm), [NPS, Plover Numbers Strong on Golden Gate Beaches (2018)](https://www.nps.gov/articles/plover-numbers-strong-on-golden-gate-beaches.htm), [Golden Gate Bird Alliance, Record number of Snowy Plovers on Ocean Beach](https://goldengatebirdalliance.org/blog-posts/record-number-of-snowy-plovers-on-ocean-beach-4-6/), [Bay Nature, Welcome Back, Snowy Plovers](https://baynature.org/?p=70303).

## Dogs on Ocean Beach, the leash rule, and what flushes snowy plovers

Dogs must be on a leash no longer than six feet on Ocean Beach from Stairwell 21 south to Sloat Boulevard, from July 1 through May 15. That is the Snowy Plover Protection Area, set by a 2008 federal rule (36 CFR 7.97(d)). The reason is energy. A plover that runs or flies from a dog, jogger or kite burns fat it needs for winter and for the next breeding season. Park Service surveys in 2019 to 2020 found more than 70 percent of pets on this stretch were off leash. A Golden Gate Audubon report from 2009 to 2010 found about 35 percent compliance. Park stewards also ask people to pack out food scraps, because trash draws ravens and crows. Ravens are a main predator where plovers nest: at Point Reyes in 2025, nests fenced with small exclosures hatched 62 percent of the time, against 27 percent for unprotected nests. Walking on the wet sand near the water keeps people clear of resting birds.

Sources: [NPS, Ocean Beach](https://www.nps.gov/goga/planyourvisit/oceanbeach.htm), [San Francisco Standard, July 1, 2022](https://sfstandard.com/2022/07/01/as-snowy-plovers-return-to-ocean-beach-park-service-reminds-dog-owners-to-keep-canines-on-a-leash/), [NPS, 2025 Snowy Plover Breeding Season at Point Reyes](https://www.nps.gov/articles/000/sfanblog_2025-snowy-plover-breeding-season-has-a-happy-ending.htm), [Sunset Dunes, Visit](https://sunsetdunes.org/visit).

## Sanderlings, willets, marbled godwits and long-billed curlews on Ocean Beach, and what each eats

The small pale birds that chase each wave down the sand at Ocean Beach and run back up are sanderlings (Calidris alba). Audubon says they chase waves mainly to catch sand crabs, which are easiest to spot just after a wave pulls back; they also take amphipods, worms and small mollusks. Sanderlings nest in the far north, mostly above the Arctic Circle. Willets (Tringa semipalmata), gray in winter with a bold white wing stripe in flight, probe for crabs, other crustaceans and small mollusks, and nest inland around freshwater marshes. Marbled godwits (Limosa fedoa) have a long bill that is pink at the base and dark at the tip; they feed by touch on worms, mollusks and crustaceans and nest on the northern Great Plains. The long-billed curlew (Numenius americanus), the largest North American shorebird, reaches crabs and worms too deep for the others. Bay Nature notes that curlews stalk mole crabs. Whimbrels look like a smaller curlew with a striped head; no diet source was opened for them.

Sources: [Audubon, Sanderling](https://www.audubon.org/field-guide/bird/sanderling), [Audubon, Willet](https://www.audubon.org/field-guide/bird/willet), [Audubon, Marbled Godwit](https://www.audubon.org/field-guide/bird/marbled-godwit), [Audubon, Long-billed Curlew](https://www.audubon.org/field-guide/bird/long-billed-curlew), [Golden Gate Bird Alliance, Wintering Shorebirds](https://goldengatebirdalliance.org/blog-posts/wintering-shorebirds/), [Bay Nature, Hustle and Flow](https://baynature.org/article/hustle-and-flow/).

## Gulls and brown pelicans at Ocean Beach: western, California and Heermann's gulls by season

Three gulls are worth learning on Ocean Beach. The western gull (Larus occidentalis) is here all year. It breeds along the Pacific coast, is almost never found inland, and eats fish, crabs, clams, other birds' eggs and scraps. The California gull (Larus californicus) nests at inland lakes and comes to the coast mostly for winter, though young birds may stay through summer. Heermann's gull (Larus heermanni), dark gray with a white head and red bill, nests on islands off western Mexico and is common on this coast from late May until early February. It often snatches fish from a brown pelican's pouch as the pelican surfaces. Brown pelicans (Pelecanus occidentalis) plunge from as high as 60 feet for anchovies and other small fish. They were listed as endangered in 1970 after pesticide poisoning, recovered after the 1972 DDT ban, and were delisted in 2009. In early summer 2022, during an anchovy boom, eight-inch fish fell on roofs in the Outer Richmond, most likely dropped by overfull seabirds.

Sources: [Audubon, Western Gull](https://www.audubon.org/field-guide/bird/western-gull), [Audubon, California Gull](https://www.audubon.org/field-guide/bird/california-gull), [Audubon, Heermann's Gull](https://www.audubon.org/birds/heermanns-gull), [NPS Presidio, Heermann's Gull](https://www.nps.gov/prsf/learn/nature/hermanns-gull.htm), [Audubon, Brown Pelican](https://www.audubon.org/field-guide/bird/brown-pelican), [SFGate via WMTW, anchovies falling from the sky](https://www.wmtw.com/article/anchovies-reportedly-raining-from-the-sky-san-francisco/40485037).

## Dead and starving seabirds on Ocean Beach: common murres, shearwaters and die-off years

When many dead or weak seabirds turn up on Ocean Beach, the usual cause is hunger offshore. Common murres (Uria aalge) dive more than 150 feet after fish. From August through December 2015, far more murres than normal washed up from Sonoma to San Luis Obispo counties, and rescue centers took in more than 1,000 live birds. Necropsies found most were emaciated; starvation was the likely cause, with a large warm-water anomaly and a harmful algal bloom as contributing factors. A University of Washington study of 29 years of beach surveys found that mass die-offs follow marine heat waves, at one to six months and again at 10 to 16 months, and hit murres, puffins, auklets and shearwaters hardest. In early March 2026 the Beach Watch program warned that another die-off might be starting, affecting murres, Brandt's cormorants and brown pelicans; state tests ruled out bird flu and pointed to starvation. Sooty shearwaters, which nest in the Southern Hemisphere, peak off California in late summer.

Sources: [USGS, common murre mortality event in California in 2015](https://www.usgs.gov/data/data-release-investigation-a-largescale-common-murre-uria-aalge-mortality-event-california), [NPS, Heatwaves Cause Seabird Die-offs](https://www.nps.gov/articles/000/heatwaves-cause-seabird-dieoffs.htm), [COASST, spring 2026 update](https://coasst.org/news-views/blog/more-pouchbills-than-normal-spring-2026/), [Audubon, Common Murre](https://www.audubon.org/field-guide/bird/common-murre), [Audubon, Sooty Shearwater](https://www.audubon.org/field-guide/bird/sooty-shearwater).

## Bank swallows nesting in the Fort Funston bluffs: a threatened colony and its counts

The small brown swallows over the bluffs south of Sloat in spring are bank swallows (Riparia riparia), listed as threatened by California in 1989. Fort Funston and Año Nuevo may be the only coastal colonies left in the state. The birds winter in South America, arrive in late March and April, dig burrows one to three feet into the sandstone, feed on flying insects (Lake Merced is a quarter mile away), fledge chicks by July and leave by mid-August. Counts have fallen. A 1987 survey found 417 burrows, roughly 250 pairs. From 1993 to 2006 the count averaged 362 burrows a year (range 140 to 924). In 2012 a Golden Gate Bird Alliance observer counted about 110. In 2023 the Park Service recorded 18 active burrows; it had drilled 50 starter holes and one was used. Since 2020 the birds have nested at the south end, at Phillip Burton Memorial Beach. Ravens and kestrels take chicks, and people walking the bluff edge can collapse burrows.

Sources: [NPS, Biologists Work to Help Fort Funston's Nesting Bank Swallows (2023)](https://nps.gov/articles/000/sfanblog_with-monitoring-and-research-biologists-work-to-help-fort-funstons-nesting-bank-swallows.htm), [Golden Gate National Parks Conservancy, Bank Swallow](https://www.parksconservancy.org/node/136), [Bay Nature, Beachfront Property](https://baynature.org/?p=68391), [Golden Gate Bird Alliance, Fort Funston Bank Swallows (2012)](https://goldengatebirdalliance.org/?p=22586).

## Pacific mole crabs (sand crabs) at Ocean Beach: who they are and who eats them

The egg-shaped crabs that bury themselves in the wet sand at Ocean Beach are Pacific mole crabs, also called sand crabs (Emerita analoga). They live in the swash zone, bury themselves backward in one to seven seconds, and comb plankton from the retreating water with feathery antennae. Females reach about 1.6 inches, twice the size of males, and carry bright orange eggs from spring to early fall. Larvae drift at sea for about four months. Adults ride out winter in offshore sandbars and come back with the sand in spring. Almost everything eats them: sanderlings, willets, curlews, surf scoters that dredge the shallows, and nearshore fish. Barred surfperch may get as much as 90 percent of their diet from sand crabs. Students in the LiMPETS program have counted them here since 2002. Ocean Beach fell from 100 to 300 crabs per square meter in 2002 to 6 in 2007; Bay Nature reported that nobody knew why.

Sources: [Bay Nature, Hustle and Flow (2024)](https://baynature.org/article/hustle-and-flow/), [Bay Nature, Mole Crab Decline (2008)](https://baynature.org/?p=68546), [Wikipedia, Barred surfperch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barred_surfperch).

## By-the-wind sailors (Velella) on Ocean Beach: the blue things in spring and why they strand

The blue, cellophane-like ovals that sometimes carpet Ocean Beach in spring are by-the-wind sailors (Velella velella). Each is a hydrozoan, a relative of jellyfish, with a gas-filled float, tentacles that catch plankton, and a clear sail on top. They live on the open ocean surface and go where the wind pushes them, so a run of onshore wind strands them by the thousands. In Northern California this usually happens in spring or early summer, when upwelling has fed the plankton they eat, and lasts days to weeks. KQED reported strandings at Ocean Beach, Baker Beach and Crissy Field in April 2025 and again, in especially large numbers, in spring 2026. Scientists link big Velella years to warm water, and the Pacific was in a marine heat wave in 2026, but they caution that spring strandings are normal. Ocean sunfish eat them. Their sting is mild to people, but a dog or child that mouths a fresh one could be hurt.

Sources: [KQED, Velella velella on Bay Area beaches (April 30, 2026)](https://www.kqed.org/science/2000924/velella-velella-san-francisco-bay-area-beaches-2026-where-to-see-by-the-wind-sailors-blue-jellyfish-like-creatures).

## Kelp, driftwood and sand dollars on Ocean Beach: why the wrack line matters

The line of kelp, wood and shells left by the last high tide on Ocean Beach is called wrack, and it is food and shelter. Snowy plovers pick flies and sand fleas from sand and seaweed, and the Park Service asks visitors not to remove kelp or driftwood from the beach. Wrack also builds land. The San Francisco Estuary Institute's 2023 dune report, summarized by Surfrider, recommends leaving wood and kelp in place and even setting large logs on purpose, because they trap blowing sand, shelter seedlings and start small new dunes (embryo foredunes) at the foot of the old ones. Sand dollars (Dendraster excentricus) are another common find; they often cover the sand. Low sand also uncovers older things: the wreck of the King Philip, a clipper ship that went aground on Ocean Beach on January 25, 1878, showed itself in November 2010.

Sources: [San Francisco Standard, July 1, 2022](https://sfstandard.com/2022/07/01/as-snowy-plovers-return-to-ocean-beach-park-service-reminds-dog-owners-to-keep-canines-on-a-leash/), [Bay Nature, Welcome Back, Snowy Plovers](https://baynature.org/?p=70303), [Surfrider San Francisco, Climate Action](https://sf.surfrider.org/san-francisco-climate-action), [Wikipedia, Ocean Beach, San Francisco](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ocean_Beach,_San_Francisco).

## Surfperch and white sharks in the surf zone off Ocean Beach

The fish most tied to the Ocean Beach surf are surfperch, which bear live young instead of laying eggs. Barred surfperch (Amphistichus argenteus) live in water less than 16 feet deep off long sandy beaches and eat mostly sand crabs. Redtail surfperch (Amphistichus rhodoterus) share the surf zone year-round and eat more small fish. Bigger hunters pass through too. On September 14, 2016, a surf camera caught a young white shark, judged seven to eight feet long by the Shark Research Committee, leaping clear of the water in front of surfers. In October 2018 Coast Guard helicopter crews counted about 20 white sharks roughly 100 yards off the beach between Pacifica and Ocean Beach. Shark Stewards said young white sharks stay close to shore while large adults gather near seal and sea lion colonies such as the Farallones and Año Nuevo, and that warm water that year likely drew more juveniles. The group called attacks on people on this coast extremely rare.

Sources: [Wikipedia, Barred surfperch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barred_surfperch), [Wikipedia, Redtail surfperch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Redtail_surfperch), [KTVU, white shark leaps at Ocean Beach (2016)](https://www.ktvu.com/news/video-great-white-shark-leaps-out-of-water-at-ocean-beach), [Shark Stewards, white sharks off Pacifica and Ocean Beach (2018)](https://sharkstewards.org/shiver-of-great-white-sharks-spotted-off-pacifica-ocean-beach/).

## Dead whales and sick sea lions on Ocean Beach: what happens and whom to call

When a whale or a sick sea lion comes ashore at Ocean Beach, The Marine Mammal Center and the California Academy of Sciences respond. On June 20, 2021, a 45-foot adult female gray whale washed up. Scientists did a necropsy on the sand the next day. They found broken vertebrae without bruising, which suggested a ship hit her after death, and very little food in her stomach; they could not name the cause. Officials planned to bury her on the beach. A sea lion that has seizures or acts disoriented may be poisoned by domoic acid, a toxin from algal blooms that builds up in anchovies and other small fish. In summer 2026 the Center took more than 500 hotline calls on its busiest days during an outbreak centered on Monterey. The advice is the same for any marine mammal on the sand: stay at least 150 feet back, keep dogs away, and call 415-289-SEAL (7325).

Sources: [NBC Bay Area, June 2021 Ocean Beach whale](https://www.nbcbayarea.com/news/local/scientists-unable-to-confirm-cause-of-death-for-whale-that-washed-up-in-san-francisco/2577120/), [The Marine Mammal Center, update on domoic acid poisoning (August 28, 2026)](https://www.marinemammalcenter.org/news/an-update-on-domoic-acid-poisoning-on-the-california-coast).

## Beach fires at Ocean Beach: the fire rings, the season and the 9:30 curfew

Fires on Ocean Beach are allowed only in the 16 metal fire rings the National Park Service sets out between Stairwells 15 and 20, and only from March 1 through October 31. November through February is a no-burn season that matches the Bay Area's Winter Spare the Air period, and fires are also banned on summer Spare the Air days. All fires must be out by 9:30 p.m. and must be put out with water, because sand only insulates hot coals. Groups of 25 or more need a permit. Homemade rings of rocks or logs are not allowed. Beach fires here are older than the park: they predate the 1975 transfer of the beach to the Golden Gate National Recreation Area. The Park Service ran pilot programs in 2007 and 2014, and the current rules took effect April 4, 2016, after 508 public comments, to address air quality, public safety and the load on park staff.

Sources: [NPS, Ocean Beach Fire Program](https://www.nps.gov/articles/ocean-beach-fire-program.htm), [NPS, Ocean Beach](https://www.nps.gov/goga/planyourvisit/oceanbeach.htm).

## How Ocean Beach changes between winter and summer

Ocean Beach is not the same beach in March as in September. Winter storm waves pull sand off the beach; erosion peaks in winter. City surveys south of Sloat show the dry beach is narrowest in March and April and widest in September and October. The animals move with the sand. Mole crabs spend winter in offshore bars and return in spring. Snowy plovers are here from July to May; bank swallows are at Fort Funston from April to August; Heermann's gulls arrive in late May; by-the-wind sailors strand in spring. The weather runs opposite to what visitors expect. Fog covers the beach much of late spring and summer, with temperatures in the 50s, and the sunniest beach days come in September and October. Fire season is March through October. The dog leash rule for the plover area runs July 1 to May 15.
