This note covers the nearshore Pacific off Ocean Beach and the Outer Sunset: why the water is cold and the summers foggy, which whales, dolphins, seals, sharks and fish pass by and when, the hazards of the surf, and how warm-water years and toxic blooms change things. It ends with a month-by-month list of what a neighbor can see from the sand.

## Why the water off Ocean Beach is cold and Outer Sunset summers are foggy

The ocean off Ocean Beach stays around 52 to 58 degrees Fahrenheit all year, and that cold water is the reason for the Outer Sunset's summer fog. Two things chill it. The California Current brings cool water down from the North Pacific. Then, in spring and summer, northwest winds blowing along the coast push the surface water offshore, and deeper water rises to replace it. This is called upwelling. The upwelled water is colder and full of nutrients, which feed plankton, which feed anchovies, seabirds and whales; NOAA notes the best fishing grounds are found where upwelling is common. Fog forms when moist ocean air passes over that cold water and cools to its dew point. Summer heat in the Central Valley pulls the fog bank inland through the Golden Gate, the biggest gap in the Coast Ranges. The Park Service says fog covers Ocean Beach much of late spring and summer, and the sunniest days come in September and October.

## Gray whales passing Ocean Beach: migration months, the 2019 to 2023 die-off and the count since

Gray whales (Eschrichtius robustus) pass San Francisco twice a year on a round trip of 10,000 to 12,000 miles between Arctic feeding grounds and the lagoons of Baja California. They head south past California in December and January and north from March through May; calves born in January and February travel north with their mothers. From December 17, 2018, to November 9, 2023, NOAA tracked an "unusual mortality event": 690 gray whales stranded, 347 of them in the United States and 95 in California. Many were starving. NOAA tied it to changes in sea ice and prey in the northern Bering and Chukchi seas. The population fell from about 27,000 around 2016 to 14,526 in 2023 and about 12,890 in 2025, among the lowest counts since the 1960s, and only 85 calves were counted in 2025, a record low. In July 2026 NOAA reported a higher estimate of about 17,750, and said the jump is too big to be explained by births and is not yet understood.

Sources: [The Marine Mammal Center, Gray Whale](https://www.marinemammalcenter.org/animal-care/learn-about-marine-mammals/cetaceans/gray-whale), [NOAA Fisheries, 2019-2023 Gray Whale Unusual Mortality Event](https://www.fisheries.noaa.gov/national/marine-life-distress/2019-2023-gray-whale-unusual-mortality-event-along-west-coast-and), [NOAA Fisheries, West Coast gray whale estimate shows increase (July 2026)](https://www.fisheries.noaa.gov/feature-story/west-coast-gray-whale-estimate-shows-increase-new-count-determines).

## Gray whales turning in at the Golden Gate: San Francisco Bay sightings and deaths, 2018 to 2026

Since 2018 some migrating gray whales have turned into San Francisco Bay instead of passing Ocean Beach, and many have died there. A study published in April 2026 by The Marine Mammal Center, the California Academy of Sciences and partners identified 114 individual gray whales in the Bay from 2018 to 2025 by their skin markings. Yearly counts swung from 6 in 2018 and 2024 to 23 in 2019 and 36 in 2025. Whales stayed about 24 days on average in 2025; one stayed 75 days in 2023. At least 18 percent of the catalogued whales were later found dead. Of 70 carcasses examined, 30 had been killed by vessels. In 2025, 21 gray whales were found dead in and around the Bay. NOAA says the whales may be looking for food. The study's authors call for ship speed limits. Carcasses sometimes reach Ocean Beach: a 45-foot female washed up on June 20, 2021.

Sources: [Frontiers in Marine Science, Gray whales in San Francisco Bay experience high mortality (April 2026)](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/marine-science/articles/10.3389/fmars.2026.1775666/full), [ABC7 News, May 9, 2026](https://abc7news.com/post/concerns-grow-more-gray-whale-deaths-bay-area-climate-changes-global-contribution-epidemic/19007871/), [NOAA Fisheries, West Coast gray whale estimate shows increase (July 2026)](https://www.fisheries.noaa.gov/feature-story/west-coast-gray-whale-estimate-shows-increase-new-count-determines), [NBC Bay Area, June 2021 Ocean Beach whale](https://www.nbcbayarea.com/news/local/scientists-unable-to-confirm-cause-of-death-for-whale-that-washed-up-in-san-francisco/2577120/).

## Humpback whales feeding close to shore and inside the Golden Gate since 2016

Humpback whales (Megaptera novaeangliae) feed off California in summer and winter off Central America. About 2,900 use the coasts of California, Oregon and Washington. Before 2016 a humpback inside the Golden Gate was treated as a stray, like "Humphrey" in 1985 and 1990. In late April 2016 Bill Keener of Golden Gate Cetacean Research began seeing humpbacks come in through the Gate to feed, which local scientists had not recorded before. The draw is anchovies. In summer 2024 the San Francisco Chronicle reported huge anchovy schools along the coast and in the Bay, with humpbacks feeding near Pacifica, under the Golden Gate Bridge and in the central Bay, in the path of cargo ships. A whale-watch naturalist compared it to eating dinner in the middle of a freeway. Ship strikes and tangling in fishing gear are the main dangers.

Sources: [KQED, Humpback Whales Exploring the Bay in Unprecedented Numbers (2016)](http://www.kqed.org/science/731114/humpback-whales-exploring-the-bay-in-unprecedented-numbers), [The Marine Mammal Center, repost of San Francisco Chronicle, August 20, 2024](https://www.marinemammalcenter.org/news/san-francisco-chronicle-san-francisco-bay-is-awash-with-endangered-whales-is-it-good-or-bad), [The Marine Mammal Center, Humpback Whale](https://www.marinemammalcenter.org/animal-care/learn-about-marine-mammals/cetaceans/humpback-whale).

## Bottlenose dolphins at Ocean Beach and harbor porpoises back at the Golden Gate

The dolphins seen off Ocean Beach are newcomers. Coastal bottlenose dolphins (Tursiops truncatus) lived only in Southern California until the strong El Niño of 1982 to 1983, when they spread north to Monterey Bay. Beach Watch volunteers recorded the first group at Ocean Beach in November 2006. Researchers photographed 84 individuals around San Francisco from 2007 to 2018 and matched 92 percent to animals known farther south; some now range to Sonoma County. They travel in changing groups of five to ten. Harbor porpoises (Phocoena phocoena) are smaller, about five feet, with a low triangular fin and no beak. They lived in San Francisco Bay for centuries, vanished around World War II, when a seven-mile submarine net crossed the Bay's mouth and sewage fouled the water, and were seen again in 2008. By 2011 researchers had catalogued 250. They eat herring and anchovies. The best place to see them is from the Golden Gate Bridge walkway an hour or two before high tide.

## Sea lions, harbor seals and elephant seals off Ocean Beach: telling them apart and when pups appear

Three kinds of seal turn up in the surf or on the sand at Ocean Beach. California sea lions (Zalophus californianus) are the most numerous. Males reach 850 pounds and seven feet. They breed mostly on the Channel Islands and in Mexico, with pups born in early June, and eat squid, anchovies, herring and sardines; the population is about 257,000 and growing. Pacific harbor seals (Phoca vitulina) are smaller and wary of people, five to six feet long. Their pups are born from February to April, and The Marine Mammal Center says many are orphaned when people or dogs come too close and scare the mother off. Northern elephant seals (Mirounga angustirostris) breed in winter at Año Nuevo and Point Reyes and molt on beaches between April and August. Hunted down to fewer than 100 animals by 1910, they now number about 150,000. For any of them: stay 150 feet back, leash the dog, and call 415-289-SEAL if the animal looks hurt.

## Toxic algal blooms and domoic acid off the San Francisco coast: 2015 to 2026

Domoic acid is a nerve poison made by a microscopic ocean diatom, Pseudo-nitzschia. It builds up in anchovies and other small fish and in crabs and clams, and it sickens the sea lions and other marine mammals that eat them; it has also been found in dead seabirds. The Marine Mammal Center diagnosed the first marine mammal case in 1998. In spring 2015 a bloom that began in unusually warm water became the largest domoic acid outbreak recorded on the West Coast and shut razor clam, rock crab and Dungeness crab fisheries for months. In summer 2026 the Center reported another large outbreak, centered on the Monterey Peninsula, with hundreds of animals rescued; by late October it was calling the outbreak the largest in its history. In fall 2026 part of the Mendocino coast crab fishery was closed for domoic acid. The 2022 red tide inside San Francisco Bay is not covered here.

Sources: [The Marine Mammal Center, update on domoic acid poisoning (August 28, 2026)](https://www.marinemammalcenter.org/news/an-update-on-domoic-acid-poisoning-on-the-california-coast), [The Marine Mammal Center, Unprecedented Toxic Algal Bloom Linked to Unusual Ocean Conditions](https://www.marinemammalcenter.org/publications/unprecedented-toxic-algal-bloom-linked-to-unusual-ocean-conditions), [KPIX via KION, commercial Dungeness crab season delayed (November 2, 2026)](https://kioncentralcoast.com/news/2026/11/02/california-commercial-dungeness-crab-season-delayed-until-january-2027/).

## White sharks off Ocean Beach and the "Red Triangle"

Ocean Beach sits inside the "Red Triangle," the stretch of ocean from Bodega Bay out to the Farallon Islands and down to Monterey Bay or Big Sur where a large share of recorded white shark attacks on people in the United States (about 38 percent, by one tally) have happened. White sharks are here for the seals, sea lions and elephant seals. Adults gather along this coast in fall, leave for the open Pacific around December, are absent in May and return in August. The Greater Farallones sanctuary calls its white shark population one of the most significant on the planet. Close to Ocean Beach the sharks seen are usually young ones: a seven to eight footer breached among surfers on September 14, 2016, and about 20 were counted from Coast Guard helicopters between Pacifica and Ocean Beach in October 2018. Shark Stewards says attacks here are extremely rare and advises extra caution in fall.

Sources: [Wikipedia, Red Triangle (Pacific Ocean)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Red_Triangle_(Pacific_Ocean)), [NOAA, Greater Farallones National Marine Sanctuary, About](https://farallones.noaa.gov/about/), [KTVU, white shark leaps at Ocean Beach (2016)](https://www.ktvu.com/news/video-great-white-shark-leaps-out-of-water-at-ocean-beach), [Shark Stewards, white sharks off Pacifica and Ocean Beach (2018)](https://sharkstewards.org/shiver-of-great-white-sharks-spotted-off-pacifica-ocean-beach/).

## Anchovies and herring: the small fish that feed almost everything off Ocean Beach

Most of the big animals a neighbor sees off Ocean Beach are there for two small fish. Northern anchovies school along the coast and in the Bay in summer. When upwelling is strong they boom. In June 2022 a South Bay survey netted more than 2,600 in a month, fishermen said they had never seen bait so thick, and fish dropped by overloaded seabirds landed on roofs and sidewalks in the Outer Richmond. Humpback whales, brown pelicans, cormorants, sea lions and harbor porpoises all eat them. Pacific herring come in the cool months. Adults move inshore to spawn from as early as October to as late as April, and San Francisco Bay is one of the two largest spawning grounds in California. They lay eggs on marine plants, rocks and pilings in calm, less salty water. The state sets a yearly catch quota for the Bay herring fishery.

Sources: [SFGate via WMTW, anchovies falling from the sky (2022)](https://www.wmtw.com/article/anchovies-reportedly-raining-from-the-sky-san-francisco/40485037), [The Marine Mammal Center, repost of San Francisco Chronicle, August 20, 2024](https://www.marinemammalcenter.org/news/san-francisco-chronicle-san-francisco-bay-is-awash-with-endangered-whales-is-it-good-or-bad), [California Department of Fish and Wildlife, Pacific Herring](https://wildlife.ca.gov/Fishing/Commercial/Herring), [KQED, After 60 Years, Porpoises Return to San Francisco Bay](https://www.kqed.org/news/48914/after-60-years-porpoises-return-to-san-francisco-bay).

## Dungeness crab season off San Francisco and why whales keep delaying it

Two seasons running, the commercial Dungeness crab season off San Francisco has been pushed from fall to the New Year, because humpback whales are still feeding here in fall and can tangle in crab gear. For the 2025 to 2026 season the state Department of Fish and Wildlife delayed the opener, citing many entanglements that year, large numbers of humpbacks, and leatherback sea turtles; the season was held until early January 2026 (a price dispute added to the delay). On October 30, 2026, the state again delayed the commercial season, to at least January 1, 2027, citing humpback entanglements during the year, with the next review in mid-December. Sport crabbing opened November 7, 2026, but with crab traps restricted; hoop nets are allowed and must be checked every two hours. The pattern is a direct trade: more whales feeding close to shore in fall, later local crab.

Sources: [California Department of Fish and Wildlife, news release of October 24, 2025](https://wildlife.ca.gov/News/Archive/recreational-dungeness-crab-fishing-begins-nov-1-with-limitations-commercial-season-delayed), [KPIX via KION, commercial Dungeness crab season delayed (November 2, 2026)](https://kioncentralcoast.com/news/2026/11/02/california-commercial-dungeness-crab-season-delayed-until-january-2027/).

## Rip currents and sneaker waves: why Ocean Beach is dangerous for swimmers

The Park Service says plainly that Ocean Beach is not a swimming beach. Rip currents are fast channels of water flowing away from shore, and people have been swept out from water three feet deep. Calm-looking water is often where the rip is, and many drownings happen on warm, gentle days. The tides running through the Golden Gate add strong currents, measured at up to 1.5 meters per second (about three miles an hour) at the north end. The water is cold enough to rob a swimmer of strength in minutes. Seven people drowned here in 1998, the worst year on record; beach patrols began after that. Sneaker waves, which run much farther up the beach than the waves before them, are another hazard: on November 28, 2025, the Weather Service warned of 13 to 18 foot sneaker waves on west-facing beaches including Ocean Beach. A surfer died near Lincoln Way on May 28, 2026. If caught in a rip, swim parallel to shore.

Sources: [NPS, Surf Warning and Beach Safety Practices](https://www.nps.gov/goga/learn/news/surf-warnings-and-beach-safety-tips.htm), [Golden Gate Xpress, When in Doubt, Don't Go Out (2023)](https://xpressmagazine.org/23302/all/when-in-doubt-dont-go-out), [Wikipedia, Ocean Beach, San Francisco](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ocean_Beach,_San_Francisco), [Sonoma Index-Tribune, sneaker wave warning (November 28, 2025)](https://www.sonomanews.com/2025/11/28/govt-warns-of-deadly-18-foot-sneaker-waves-along-norcal-coast), [Bay City News, May 28, 2026](https://localnewsmatters.org/2026/05/28/man-dies-after-being-pulled-unconscious-from-surf-at-ocean-beach/).

## The Greater Farallones National Marine Sanctuary and the waters off San Francisco

The ocean north and west of the Golden Gate is a federal marine sanctuary. It was set aside in 1981 as the Gulf of the Farallones National Marine Sanctuary, covering 1,279 square miles. In 2015 it was enlarged to 3,295 square miles and renamed Greater Farallones. NOAA counts 36 kinds of marine mammals there, including blue, gray and humpback whales, more than a quarter million breeding seabirds, at least 25 endangered or threatened species, and a major white shark population. The sanctuary's partner group, the Greater Farallones Association, runs Beach Watch, whose volunteers have walked Ocean Beach and other shores on a regular schedule since 1993, counting live and dead birds and mammals. The exact sanctuary lines off Ocean Beach were not confirmed for this note. The Coastal Commission lists the Monterey Bay National Marine Sanctuary among the agencies with a say over the city's seawall project south of Sloat.

Sources: [NOAA, Greater Farallones National Marine Sanctuary, About](https://farallones.noaa.gov/about/), [Keener and others, Aquatic Mammals (2023)](https://cascadiaresearch.org/files/Keener_etal_2023.pdf), [California Coastal Commission staff report, application 2-21-0912 (November 2024)](https://documents.coastal.ca.gov/reports/2024/11/Th10a/Th10a-11-2024-report.pdf).

## Marine heat waves and El Niño: what warm-water years do to Ocean Beach

Warm-water years change both the beach and the animals. In an El Niño winter, bigger storm waves strip sand. The El Niño storms of 1982 to 1983 washed away about 70 percent of the sand the city had stockpiled at the north end of Ocean Beach. In the 2015 to 2016 El Niño, one of the three strongest on record, the USGS measured winter beach erosion along the West Coast at 76 percent above normal, the most ever recorded. Offshore, warm water means less food. Mass seabird die-offs of a quarter million birds or more used to come about once a decade; five happened between 2014 and 2019, as the northeast Pacific stayed unusually warm. The 2015 heat wave also set off the record toxic algal bloom, and the 1982 to 1983 El Niño is when bottlenose dolphins moved north. In spring 2026 KQED reported another Pacific marine heat wave and a possible strong El Niño forming.

## What a neighbor can see from the sand at Ocean Beach, month by month

December and January: gray whales heading south; big winter surf; no beach fires. February to April: harbor seal pups are born along the coast; the dry beach is at its narrowest in March and April. March to May: gray whales heading north, some with calves; bank swallows return to Fort Funston in April; by-the-wind sailors strand in spring winds. Late May: Heermann's gulls arrive from Mexico; snowy plovers leave, and June is usually plover-free. June to September: fog, upwelling and anchovies; brown pelicans diving; humpback whales feeding, sometimes close in; sooty shearwaters peak in late summer. July: plovers return and the dog leash rule starts on July 1. August: adult white sharks return to this coast. September and October: the sunniest weeks, the widest beach, and the last beach fires on October 31. November: crab season is decided, and winter swells and sneaker wave warnings begin. Bottlenose dolphins, sea lions and western gulls can be seen in any month.
