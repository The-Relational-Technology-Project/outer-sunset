This note covers the mammals a neighbor meets in the Outer Sunset, the west end of Golden Gate Park, Stern Grove, Pine Lake and Lake Merced. Most of it is about coyotes: when they came back, how many there are, what they eat, and what the city does and does not do about them. It also covers raccoons, skunks, opossums, gophers, rats and rat poison, bats, the bison, cats, and rare visitors.

## When coyotes came back to San Francisco, and where they came from

Coyotes (Canis latrans) were shot and poisoned out of San Francisco in the early 1900s. The Presidio Trust says the last city record of that era was in 1925, in Golden Gate Park. They returned on their own in the early 2000s. Sources differ on the year: Bay Nature reported the first animals in 2001 in the Presidio and Bernal Heights, and the Presidio Trust says 2002.

In 2003 the Presidio Trust, UC Davis and USGS researchers collared a Presidio coyote and tested its DNA. It matched coyotes north of the Golden Gate, not those on the Peninsula, and a 2004 video showed a coyote on the Golden Gate Bridge. Researchers concluded the animals most likely walked across. A separate graduate study by Katherine Marquez found a Lake Merced coyote linked to the southern population, so the south side of the city may have had a second source.

Sources: [Presidio Trust, Coyotes in the Presidio](https://presidio.gov/about/sustainability/coyotes-in-the-presidio), [Bay Nature, Urban coyotes in our midst](https://baynature.org/articles/urban-coyotes-in-our-midst/), [KQED, Learning to Live With San Francisco's Coyotes](https://www.kqed.org/news/12042228/learning-to-live-with-san-franciscos-coyotes).

## How many coyotes live in San Francisco, and the family territories near the Sunset

Nobody has an exact count of San Francisco coyotes. In 2012 Animal Care and Control estimated at least 15. By 2025 the agency's working estimate was about 100, and reporting that year said the number had held steady. UC Davis researcher Tali Caspi has said that more sightings and more news coverage do not mean more coyotes.

Coyotes live in family groups, a breeding pair plus pups and sometimes yearlings, and each family holds its own territory. Janet Kessler, a naturalist who tracks the city's coyotes, counted 18 family territories in summer 2025 and has put the average territory at about two square miles. A 2025 New York Times report said Golden Gate Park holds two family groups, with 19th Avenue as the rough dividing line, which puts the west end of the park in one family's range. Bay Nature listed Lake Merced and Golden Gate Park among the coyote areas as early as 2012. Most yearlings leave, and Kessler says many head south out of the city.

Sources: [San Francisco Standard, June 22, 2026](https://sfstandard.com/2026/06/22/san-francisco-coyote-trapped-backyard/), [Potrero View, August 2024](https://www.potreroview.net/?p=8461), [GV Wire reprint of The New York Times, May 2025](https://gvwire.com/2025/05/22/the-coyotes-of-san-francisco-are-the-subject-of-great-debate), [Nature in the City field notes](https://natureinthecity.org/field-notes/san-franciscos-coyotes-are-going-after-an-unexpected-source-of-prey-new-study-shows), [Bay Nature, 2012](https://baynature.org/articles/urban-coyotes-in-our-midst/).

## What San Francisco coyotes eat: the UC Davis scat study

The best evidence on coyote diet in San Francisco is a UC Davis study led by Tali Caspi, published in the journal Ecosphere in January 2025. The team collected 707 scats from more than 100 coyotes across the city between September 2019 and April 2022 and read the DNA in them.

Chicken turned up in 72 percent of samples and Botta's pocket gopher, the main natural prey, in about 57 percent. Human-origin food of some kind was in 78 percent. Birds (mostly pigeons and ducks) were in about 24 percent, raccoons and skunks and similar mammals in about 23 percent, and domestic cat in 4.5 percent. The method cannot detect dog, and it cannot tell whether chicken came from trash, pet food or a coop.

Neighborhood mattered. Coyotes around Coit Tower, Bernal Hill and St. Francis Wood ate more human food. Coyotes in greener places such as Golden Gate Park and the Presidio ate more natural prey. Rats were eaten most where restaurants were densest. One coyote specialized in skunks.

Sources: [University of California news, Dining out with San Francisco's coyotes](https://www.universityofcalifornia.edu/news/dining-out-san-franciscos-coyotes-how-city-reshapes-what-native-predator-eats), [Nature in the City field notes](https://natureinthecity.org/field-notes/san-franciscos-coyotes-are-going-after-an-unexpected-source-of-prey-new-study-shows).

## Coyote pupping season, dens and seasonal trail closures

Coyote pupping season is when most trouble between coyotes and dogs happens. The Presidio Trust says coyotes mate in mid-February and pups are born by mid-April, and it treats March through September as the season. San Francisco Animal Care and Control and Recreation and Parks have described it as April through August. Parents guard the den and pups more closely and hunt more actively.

Agencies respond by closing ground near dens to dogs. In April 2012, after pups were born in Golden Gate Park, the city posted signs and barricades on trails near Middle Lake and North Lake at the park's west end and said the limits could last into summer. The Presidio closes parts of the Park Trail and Bay Area Ridge Trail to dog walking from about April to October each year. A UC Berkeley analysis of nearly 4,000 reports to the city from 2012 to 2022 found conflict reports rise in pup-rearing season (April to June) and again in the dry months.

Sources: [Presidio Trust, Coyotes in the Presidio](https://presidio.gov/about/sustainability/coyotes-in-the-presidio), [KQED, Dog owners warned of coyotes in Golden Gate Park](https://www.kqed.org/news/62054/dog-owners-warned-of-coyotes-in-golden-gate-park), [UC Berkeley Research, Fostering coexistence with San Francisco's urban coyotes](https://vcresearch.berkeley.edu/node/28451).

## What coyote hazing is, and what to do when you meet one

Hazing means scaring a coyote so it keeps its distance from people. The Presidio Trust's advice: if a coyote is within 50 feet and does not leave, keep eye contact, make yourself big and loud, and wave your arms. An umbrella helps, and small objects can be tossed to startle, not to injure. If it keeps coming, do not run or turn your back. Back away slowly while still looking large. The National Park Service adds: pick up small children and small pets and move toward a busier area.

Hazing works less well when you have a dog with you or when pups are near, which is why agencies tell dog walkers in spring and summer to simply leave the area. A short leash matters most. In the UC Berkeley study of city reports from 2012 to 2022, about 78 percent of conflicts involved a dog. Feeding coyotes is prohibited in the national park lands and can bring a $500 fine in the Presidio.

Sources: [Presidio Trust, Coyotes in the Presidio](https://presidio.gov/about/sustainability/coyotes-in-the-presidio), [National Park Service, Coyotes in Golden Gate National Recreation Area](https://www.nps.gov/goga/learn/nature/coyotes.htm), [UC Berkeley Research](https://vcresearch.berkeley.edu/node/28451).

## The 2024 coyote bite in Golden Gate Park and other 2020s incidents

On June 28, 2024, a coyote bit a 5-year-old girl at a summer camp in the San Francisco Botanical Garden in Golden Gate Park. She needed stitches and recovered. Over the next two nights a U.S. Department of Agriculture trapper shot three coyotes from the family denning there. DNA matched a yearling male to the bite, and he tested negative for rabies. ABC7 later reported that a four-month-old pup was also shot, that the city paid the trapper $7,500, and that Animal Care and Control staff and coyote watcher Janet Kessler questioned why a camp was running beside a den used for years. A coyote at the same den had been shot in 2021 after lunging at a child.

On October 6, 2024, the National Park Service and Presidio Trust killed a coyote at Crissy Field that had killed small dogs and acted assertively toward people, after hazing failed. In June 2026 officers freed a young coyote stuck four days in a walled backyard.

Sources: [San Francisco Standard, July 1, 2024](https://sfstandard.com/2024/07/01/san-francisco-coyotes-shot-girl-bitten/), [ABC7 News](https://abc7news.com/post/response-coyote-attack-involving-5-year-old-girl-san-franciscos-botanical-gardens-questioned-was-pup-shot/15486350/), [National Park Service, Coyote Management 2024](https://www.nps.gov/goga/learn/nature/coyote-management-2024.htm), [San Francisco Standard, June 22, 2026](https://sfstandard.com/2026/06/22/san-francisco-coyote-trapped-backyard/).

## Why San Francisco does not trap and relocate coyotes

Neighbors often ask why coyotes are not moved somewhere else. There are two reasons in the sources. First, the law: UC Berkeley researchers working with San Francisco Animal Care and Control report that state law prevents the agency from removing or relocating coyotes. The same rule applies to smaller wildlife. An Animal Care and Control sergeant told KQED in 2016 that a trapped raccoon must be released within a couple of blocks or killed.

Second, it does not work. The Presidio Trust says most relocated coyotes try to get back to their home territory, and most die trying or cause conflict where they were let go. So the city's policy is coexistence, with lethal removal reserved for an animal judged a threat to public safety, as happened in Golden Gate Park and at Crissy Field in 2024. In the Stern Grove and Pine Lake area, where coyotes killed a small off-leash dog in September 2015, the city's response was warning signs.

Sources: [UC Berkeley Research](https://vcresearch.berkeley.edu/node/28451), [Presidio Trust, Coyotes in the Presidio](https://presidio.gov/about/sustainability/coyotes-in-the-presidio), [KQED, What happens to raccoons trapped in S.F.](https://www.kqed.org/news/10939892/what-happens-to-raccoons-trapped-in-s-f), [CBS San Francisco, 2015](https://www.cbsnews.com/sanfrancisco/news/coyotes-attack-kill-small-dog-at-stern-grove-park-in-sf/).

## Raccoons, striped skunks and Virginia opossums around Sunset houses

Raccoons, striped skunks and opossums are the night shift in Sunset backyards. The National Park Service calls raccoons common and widespread in the Presidio. They breed January through March, with young born in April and May, and den in hollow trees, logs and burrows. Striped skunks breed in February and March, have young in early May, and den under buildings and woodpiles. Both eat almost anything.

The Virginia opossum is not native. The Park Service says it was introduced to California in 1910 from the southeastern United States and has spread across the state west of the Sierra. It eats mostly carrion and insects.

In early 2022 San Francisco Animal Care and Control took more calls than usual about raccoons and skunks stumbling around in daylight. The cause was canine distemper, which does not infect people but can infect unvaccinated dogs. City officers do not remove healthy wildlife. They refer residents to exclusion specialists who seal entry points.

Sources: [National Park Service, Raccoon](https://www.nps.gov/prsf/learn/nature/raccoon.htm), [National Park Service, Striped Skunk](https://www.nps.gov/prsf/learn/nature/striped-skunk.htm), [National Park Service, Virginia Opossum](https://home.nps.gov/prsf/learn/nature/virginia-opossum.htm), [Bay Nature, March 2022](https://baynature.org/2022/03/02/science-nature/wildlife/raccoons-skunks-and-foxes-falling-sick-in-the-bay-area-as-a-respiratory-virus-spreads/), [KQED, 2016](https://www.kqed.org/news/10939892/what-happens-to-raccoons-trapped-in-s-f).

## Botta's pocket gophers and what their mounds do for sandy soil

The fresh dirt mounds that appear on lawns and park meadows are the work of Botta's pocket gopher (Thomomys bottae), which lives throughout California. Gophers live alone in tunnel systems that can run hundreds of feet. Bay Nature reports that a single gopher may bring more than a ton of soil to the surface in a year.

That digging does several things. It mixes plant scraps and droppings into the soil, which adds nutrients. The loose mounds give earthworms and other soil life a porous place to live. The tunnels take in rainwater, which cuts runoff. Empty burrows shelter salamanders, lizards and other small animals. Gardeners know the other side: gophers eat roots and bulbs, and irrigated lawns can support a lot of them.

Gophers are also the base of the local food chain. In the UC Davis study of San Francisco coyote scat, gopher was the top natural food, found in about 57 percent of samples. Bay Nature notes that great blue herons hunt gophers on watered lawns.

Sources: [Bay Nature, The Gopher Underground](https://baynature.org/?p=70174), [University of California news, coyote diet study](https://www.universityofcalifornia.edu/news/dining-out-san-franciscos-coyotes-how-city-reshapes-what-native-predator-eats).

## Rats, mice, rat poison and California's rodenticide laws

Black rats, Norway rats and house mice all live in San Francisco, and all three showed up in the UC Davis coyote diet study. The trouble with poisoning them is that the poison travels. A rodent that eats anticoagulant bait takes days to die, often eats more than one dose, and slows down, which makes it easy prey. The hawk, owl or coyote that eats it takes in the poison too. Golden Gate Bird Alliance cites the state Department of Pesticide Regulation: all rodenticides are toxic to wildlife.

California has tightened the rules twice. Assembly Bill 1788, signed in September 2020 and in force from January 1, 2021, banned most uses of the second-generation anticoagulants brodifacoum, bromadiolone, difenacoum and difethialone. Assembly Bill 2552, signed September 25, 2024 and in force January 1, 2025, extended restrictions to first-generation anticoagulants including chlorophacinone and warfarin, with exceptions for public health and agriculture.

The alternatives: remove food, seal holes bigger than a dime, and use snap traps.

Sources: [UC Agriculture and Natural Resources, California places further restrictions on rodenticides](https://ucanr.edu/blog/pests-urban-landscape/article/california-places-further-restrictions-rodenticides), [Pest Management Professional on AB 2552](https://www.mypmp.net/california-passes-another-rodenticide-ban-2/), [Golden Gate Bird Alliance, Prevent Raptor Poisoning](https://goldengatebirdalliance.org/conservation/make-the-city-safe-for-wildlife/rodenticides-and-birds/), [Nature in the City field notes](https://natureinthecity.org/field-notes/san-franciscos-coyotes-are-going-after-an-unexpected-source-of-prey-new-study-shows).

## Bats in San Francisco parks, and why Pine Lake is the best place for them

Four kinds of bats have been confirmed in San Francisco by a published survey. Jennifer Krauel and Gretchen LeBuhn of San Francisco State University set up acoustic detectors in 22 city parks in 2008 and 2009 and published the results in PLOS ONE in 2016.

The Mexican free-tailed bat (Tadarida brasiliensis) was recorded in every park. The Yuma myotis (Myotis yumanensis) was in about 36 percent of parks. The western red bat (Lasiurus blossevillii) and the little brown bat (Myotis lucifugus) were scarcer. The strongest predictor of how many species a park had was how close it was to water.

For Sunset neighbors the notable result is Pine Lake Park, next to Stern Grove. It had the highest bat species richness in the city and was one of only two parks, with Twin Peaks Reservoir, where all four species were detected. Bats were active all year, most in May and September and least in December.

Sources: [Krauel and LeBuhn, PLOS ONE 2016, via PubMed Central](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5193347/).

## The bison paddock in Golden Gate Park (not wild, but neighbors ask)

The bison in Golden Gate Park are a kept herd, not wild animals. The first, a bull named Ben Harrison, arrived in 1891, followed by a cow named Sarah Bernhardt. In 1899 the animals were moved from their first site near Kezar to the present 11-acre paddock in the park's west end. The herd peaked at 30 in 1918.

Today's animals are not descended from those. The herd was replaced in 1984 with about a dozen females, a gift from Richard Blum to his wife, then-Mayor Dianne Feinstein. Seven more females came in 2011, and five yearling females were added in March 2020 for the park's 150th anniversary. There has been no bull for years, so there are no calves.

San Francisco Zoo keepers care for the bison, and the Recreation and Park Department runs the paddock. Bison live about 25 years, so the herd ages unless new animals are brought in.

Sources: [Bay City News via Local News Matters, The rich history of the bison paddock](https://localnewsmatters.org/2020/09/22/the-rich-history-of-the-bison-paddock-at-golden-gate-park/).

## Gray foxes, river otters and other rare mammal visitors

Gray foxes all but vanished from San Francisco when coyotes returned, because coyotes kill foxes. Two were recorded in the Presidio in 2004, then none anywhere in the city until February 2015, when Presidio Trust biologist Jonathan Young photographed one in a pine above Baker Beach. A sick gray fox was seen in the Presidio during the 2022 distemper outbreak.

River otters are also rare. An otter nicknamed Sutro Sam lived in the Sutro Baths ruins in 2012 and 2013, eating goldfish people had dumped there. The River Otter Ecology Project called him the first river otter in San Francisco in more than fifty years. The sources opened for this note record no otter at Lake Merced or in Golden Gate Park.

One coyote in the UC Davis diet study had eaten sea lion, and another had fed on the fin whale that washed up at Fort Funston in 2021, so marine mammals reach the food web from the beach.

Sources: [Bay Nature, Gray fox spotted in the Presidio](https://baynature.org/articles/gray-fox-spotted-presidio-first-time-decade/), [Bay Nature, March 2022](https://baynature.org/2022/03/02/science-nature/wildlife/raccoons-skunks-and-foxes-falling-sick-in-the-bay-area-as-a-respiratory-virus-spreads/), [National Wildlife Federation, Sutro Sam](https://blog.nwf.org/2013/03/sutro-sam-san-franciscos-celebrity-river-otter/), [Nature in the City field notes](https://natureinthecity.org/field-notes/san-franciscos-coyotes-are-going-after-an-unexpected-source-of-prey-new-study-shows).

## Outdoor cats, feral cats and wild birds

Outdoor and feral cats are the predator neighbors have the most say over. A study by the Smithsonian Conservation Biology Institute and the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service estimated that outdoor cats kill up to 3.7 billion birds a year in the lower 48 states, with unowned cats responsible for about 70 percent. Mike Lynes, then head of Golden Gate Bird Alliance, cited it in 2013 when he asked San Francisco to stop feral cat feeding and trap-neuter-release in its most important natural areas. The San Francisco SPCA runs the trap-neuter-release program, and the two groups have long disagreed.

Coyotes have changed the picture. Bay Nature reports that in the 1990s Presidio staff saw up to 30 cats gather at feeding spots, and that cat numbers there dropped after coyotes returned. Cat DNA was in 4.5 percent of coyote scats in the UC Davis study. An indoor cat is safe from coyotes, and birds are safe from it.

Sources: [Golden Gate Bird Alliance, Number-One Killer of Birds is Outdoor Cats](https://goldengatebirdalliance.org/blog-posts/number-one-killer-of-birds-is-outdoor-cats/), [Bay Nature, The Comeback Quail](https://baynature.org/?p=221258), [University of California news, coyote diet study](https://www.universityofcalifornia.edu/news/dining-out-san-franciscos-coyotes-how-city-reshapes-what-native-predator-eats).
