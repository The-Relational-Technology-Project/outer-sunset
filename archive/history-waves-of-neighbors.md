This note covers who has moved into the Sunset since about 1950, and the reasons behind each wave: Chinese American families from Chinatown, later immigrants from Hong Kong, Taiwan and mainland China, the Irish community, and recent home buyers. It also gives the census figures we could find, and says where figures cover a wider area than the Outer Sunset alone.

## Who lived in the Sunset around 1950, and why almost no Chinese families did

Around 1950 the Sunset was an almost entirely white neighborhood. The city's Chinese American Historic Context Statement counts roughly 15 Chinese people living or working in the Sunset in 1949, and says Chinese Americans were under 5 percent of most Sunset census tracts in 1950. The reason was not choice. Racial covenants written into property deeds, and lending rules that rewarded them, kept Asian American buyers out of western neighborhoods. A 1916 newspaper ad for Sunset homes even used the absence of Black and Asian residents as a selling point. The U.S. Supreme Court ruled covenants unenforceable in 1948 (Shelley v. Kraemer), but the same study says housing discrimination itself stayed legal into the 1960s. The city ordinance that created the Sunset Chinese Cultural District says a few pioneering Chinese families bought homes and opened businesses here starting in 1948, and that the Sunset was still about 85 percent white in the 1970 census.

Sources: [SF Planning, Chinese American Historic Context Statement (draft, June 2021)](https://sfplanning.org/sites/default/files/documents/preserv/ChineseAmericanHCS/ChineseAmericanHCS-draft.pdf), [SF Administrative Code Chapter 107D, Sunset Chinese Cultural District](https://gocodebook.com/library/us/ca/san-francisco-admin/title-4-supplemental-provisions-4/chapter-107d-sunset-chinese-cultural-district).

## Why Chinese American families moved from Chinatown to the Sunset in the 1960s and 1970s

Chinese American families began moving to the Sunset in the late 1960s, and the move sped up in the 1970s. Three things changed at once. First, the law: the city's historic context statement credits California's Rumford Fair Housing Act (1963), the Civil Rights Act (1964) and the federal Fair Housing Act (1968) with breaking down housing segregation. Second, the 1965 Immigration Act let Chinese American citizens and residents send for relatives, so families grew and Chinatown, with few houses for sale, got more crowded. Third, the Sunset offered what Chinatown could not: bigger houses with yards at prices a middle-class family could reach. One longtime resident told the study's authors the change was slow from 1967 to 1970 and boomed after that, and that his relatives bought houses within a stone's throw of one another so they could help each other. The cultural district ordinance says about half of Sunset properties sold in the 1970s went to Chinese American buyers.

Sources: [SF Planning, Chinese American Historic Context Statement (draft, June 2021)](https://sfplanning.org/sites/default/files/documents/preserv/ChineseAmericanHCS/ChineseAmericanHCS-draft.pdf), [SF Administrative Code Chapter 107D, Sunset Chinese Cultural District](https://gocodebook.com/library/us/ca/san-francisco-admin/title-4-supplemental-provisions-4/chapter-107d-sunset-chinese-cultural-district).

## Later immigration to the Sunset from Hong Kong, Taiwan and mainland China, and the languages spoken

Immigration after 1965 changed which Chinese languages are heard in the Sunset. Before 1965, the city's historic context statement says, most Chinese immigrants to San Francisco came from the Pearl River Delta in Guangdong, and the largest group from Taishan (Toisan), so Cantonese and Toisanese were the main languages. After the 1965 Immigration Act, people arrived from all over China and from Taiwan, and Mandarin and Taiwanese became more common. Cantonese stayed dominant: a 1986 figure in the study has almost 70 percent of Chinese households in the San Francisco area speaking Cantonese and 19 percent Mandarin. The Sunset Chinese Cultural District ordinance names two later pushes: China's emigration reform in 1978, and the 1997 handover of Hong Kong, which brought newcomers, many with significant wealth. Today, census estimates for the combined Inner and Outer Sunset (2024) show a Chinese language, Cantonese or Mandarin, as the main home language in about 27 percent of households.

Sources: [SF Planning, Chinese American Historic Context Statement (draft, June 2021)](https://sfplanning.org/sites/default/files/documents/preserv/ChineseAmericanHCS/ChineseAmericanHCS-draft.pdf), [SF Administrative Code Chapter 107D, Sunset Chinese Cultural District](https://gocodebook.com/library/us/ca/san-francisco-admin/title-4-supplemental-provisions-4/chapter-107d-sunset-chinese-cultural-district), [Data USA, Outer Sunset and Inner Sunset census area](https://datausa.io/profile/geo/san-francisco-county-southwest-outer-sunset-inner-sunset-puma-ca).

## Census figures for the Sunset's Asian American share, decade by decade

There is no single tidy table of the Sunset's Asian American share by decade, but published figures give the outline. 1950: Chinese Americans were under 5 percent of most Sunset census tracts. 1970: the Sunset was about 85 percent white. 1975: 22 percent of Sunset public school students were Chinese American. 1980: Chinese Americans were 15 to 24 percent of most Sunset tracts. Around 2019: SF Planning's study lists the Outer Sunset as 59 percent Asian, Parkside 53 percent and the Inner Sunset 31 percent. 2024: census estimates for the combined Outer and Inner Sunset area show about 46,200 Asian residents out of 99,300, or roughly 46 percent, with about 33,300 white residents. These numbers use different boundaries and different definitions (Chinese in some years, all Asian in others), so they show the direction of change, not an exact line. Figures for 1990, 2000 and 2010 were not found in the sources read for this note.

Sources: [SF Planning, Chinese American Historic Context Statement (draft, June 2021)](https://sfplanning.org/sites/default/files/documents/preserv/ChineseAmericanHCS/ChineseAmericanHCS-draft.pdf), [SF Administrative Code Chapter 107D, Sunset Chinese Cultural District](https://gocodebook.com/library/us/ca/san-francisco-admin/title-4-supplemental-provisions-4/chapter-107d-sunset-chinese-cultural-district), [Data USA, Outer Sunset and Inner Sunset census area](https://datausa.io/profile/geo/san-francisco-county-southwest-outer-sunset-inner-sunset-puma-ca).

## How Irving, Noriega and Taraval became Chinese shopping streets, and when

Irving Street, Noriega Street and Taraval Street became Chinese commercial streets gradually, mostly in the 1980s and 1990s, a generation after families arrived. A resident who moved to the Sunset in 1967 told city historians there was then one Chinese restaurant that would deliver, from Chinatown, and it took two and a half hours. Families kept making weekly shopping trips to Chinatown into the 1980s. Great Wall Hardware opened at 1821 Taraval in 1983. A couple who have lived here since 1977 recalled seeing many Chinese produce vendors on Irving by the early 1990s. In oral histories, easier parking and more choice were the reasons people gave for shopping in the Sunset instead of Chinatown. By 2018 the study counted roughly 2,500 Chinese businesses in the Sunset, along Irving, Lawton, Noriega, Taraval and Vicente, which is why the Sunset is often called the city's third Chinatown, after Chinatown and the Richmond.

Sources: [SF Planning, Chinese American Historic Context Statement (draft, June 2021)](https://sfplanning.org/sites/default/files/documents/preserv/ChineseAmericanHCS/ChineseAmericanHCS-draft.pdf), [SF Administrative Code Chapter 107D, Sunset Chinese Cultural District](https://gocodebook.com/library/us/ca/san-francisco-admin/title-4-supplemental-provisions-4/chapter-107d-sunset-chinese-cultural-district).

## Chinese American institutions in the Sunset: Wah Mei School, Self-Help for the Elderly, temples and churches

Wah Mei School, founded in 1974 at 1400 Judah Street, was the first Chinese American bilingual, bicultural preschool program in San Francisco. A founder, Lillian Sing, said public schools then had no bilingual education and she feared children would lose their language. It teaches Cantonese and Mandarin as well as English. Self-Help for the Elderly, founded in Chinatown in 1966, has run the senior center in the South Sunset Playground clubhouse (2601 40th Avenue) since 1992. About 200 seniors a day used it before a $5.3 million renovation broke ground in November 2024; most are low-income and speak only Cantonese. Reopening was expected in early 2026, which this note could not confirm. The city's historic study also lists the Tien Tao Temple at 2548 24th Avenue, and free or low-cost tai chi, qi gong and Chinese folk dance at the Sunset Recreation Center, 2201 Lawton. St. Anne of the Sunset, the Catholic church on Judah Street in the Inner Sunset, holds Mass in Cantonese as well as English.

Sources: [SF Planning, Chinese American Historic Context Statement (draft, June 2021)](https://sfplanning.org/sites/default/files/documents/preserv/ChineseAmericanHCS/ChineseAmericanHCS-draft.pdf), [Richmond Review/Sunset Beacon, South Sunset Clubhouse groundbreaking](https://richmondsunsetnews.com/2024/11/14/press-release-groundbreaking-ceremony-for-south-sunset-clubhouse-renovation/), [SF Administrative Code Chapter 107D](https://gocodebook.com/library/us/ca/san-francisco-admin/title-4-supplemental-provisions-4/chapter-107d-sunset-chinese-cultural-district), [Wikipedia, St. Anne of the Sunset Church](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/St._Anne_of_the_Sunset_Church_in_San_Francisco).

## The Sunset Chinese Cultural District, created in 2021

The Sunset Chinese Cultural District is an official city designation covering the Sunset. Supervisor Gordon Mar introduced it in late May 2021 and the Board of Supervisors passed it unanimously on July 20, 2021. The ordinance took effect September 4, 2021. It was the city's ninth cultural district and the only one dedicated to the Chinese community. The stated purposes were to strengthen the Asian American community in the Sunset, recognize its history, and respond to the rise in anti-Asian violence and racism since 2020. The ordinance mentions a March for AAPI Solidarity and Safety held on the Great Highway on April 18, 2021. At the time it passed, no money had yet been set aside, an aide to Mar told the Sunset Beacon. The district's director, Lily Wong, later became the lead organizer of the Sunset Night Market.

Sources: [Richmond Review/Sunset Beacon, Board approves Chinese Cultural District](https://richmondsunsetnews.com/2021/08/06/board-approves-mars-legislation-creating-chinese-cultural-district/), [SF Administrative Code Chapter 107D](https://gocodebook.com/library/us/ca/san-francisco-admin/title-4-supplemental-provisions-4/chapter-107d-sunset-chinese-cultural-district), [SF Standard, Sunset Night Market return](https://sfstandard.com/2026/01/08/sunset-night-market-return-2026/).

## The Sunset Night Market on Irving Street, 2023 to 2026, and what became of it

The Sunset Night Market started in 2023 on Irving Street, paid for by the city and pushed by then-Supervisor Joel Engardio. In 2024 it grew from three blocks to seven, with markets in late August and late September, and drew tens of thousands of people over its first two years. In July 2025 the market was canceled for the year. Organizers cited late payments from the city and an event grown too big to manage; the SF Standard also reported it had become tangled in the fight over the Great Highway and the Engardio recall. A barbecue owner, Daniel Ramirez, organized a one-time, privately funded replacement called Sunset After Dark. In January 2026 organizers announced the market's return with a $100,000 city grant: four smaller markets tied to Chinese holidays, starting with a five-block Lunar New Year market on February 27 and a Dragon Boat Festival market on June 12. This note found the plans, but no report confirming how those 2026 markets went.

Sources: [SF Standard, Night market canceled (July 2025)](https://sfstandard.com/2025/07/10/sunset-irving-street-night-market-cancelled-recall/), [SF Standard, Sunset Night Market return (January 2026)](https://sfstandard.com/2026/01/08/sunset-night-market-return-2026/).

## The Irish community in the Sunset: the United Irish Cultural Center and a planned Irish Cultural District

Irish families were one of the Sunset's founding communities. Large numbers moved to the Sunset in the 1920s and 1930s, many from South of Market and later over Twin Peaks from Noe and Eureka valleys, according to the Sunset Beacon. In 1971 a group of former members of the Knights of the Red Branch hall, South of Market, bought land at 45th Avenue and Wawona Street for $130,000. The United Irish Cultural Center, 2700 45th Avenue, has stood there since and marked its 50th anniversary in 2025. It houses the Patrick J. Dowling Library of Irish history and literature, and today also hosts boxing, Chinese American ballroom dancing, bar mitzvahs and quinceañeras. On St. Patrick's Day, March 17, 2026, the Board of Supervisors voted unanimously to state its intent to create a Sunset Irish Cultural District, backed by Supervisor Alan Wong. A steering committee is to set goals and boundaries before a final vote. This note found no later report on that final vote.

Sources: [Richmond Review/Sunset Beacon, Steps taken to establish Irish Cultural District (April 2026)](https://richmondsunsetnews.com/2026/04/12/steps-taken-for-sf-to-establish-irish-cultural-district-in-sunset/).

## Other languages and communities in the Sunset today

The Sunset is not only Chinese American and Irish American. Census estimates for the combined Outer and Inner Sunset (2024) show about 35 percent of residents were born outside the United States, and 47 percent of households mainly speak a language other than English at home: a Chinese language in about 27 percent of households, Spanish in about 6 percent, and Tagalog in about 2 percent. About 10 percent of residents are Hispanic or Latino. When neighbors were asked what they thought of the art at Sunset Dunes in 2025, in a process led by Rec and Park, comments were taken online in English and Chinese, and in person in Russian and Tagalog as well. The sources read for this note did not include a published history of Russian-speaking families in the Sunset; the Western Neighborhoods Project is the place to ask.

Sources: [Data USA, Outer Sunset and Inner Sunset census area](https://datausa.io/profile/geo/san-francisco-county-southwest-outer-sunset-inner-sunset-puma-ca), [Sunset Dunes Temporary Public Art Update (April 2026)](https://media.api.sf.gov/documents/Sunset_Dunes_Art_Update_2026.pdf).

## Homeowners, renters and in-law units in Sunset garages

A little over half of households in the Sunset own their home: 57.1 percent in 2024 census estimates for the combined Outer and Inner Sunset. Many of the rest rent, and a good number rent rooms downstairs. Sunset houses built between 1920 and 1960 usually have a garage and basement level, and over the years many of those spaces became homes for grandparents, grown children, students or tenants, often without permits. Supervisor Alan Wong wrote in July 2026 that thousands of these rooms have ceilings between 7 feet and 7 feet 5 inches, just under the city's 7-foot-6 minimum, and that digging out a floor to legalize one can cost tens of thousands of dollars. On June 2, 2026 he introduced an ordinance to lower the city's minimum to the state's 7 feet, with five co-sponsors and support from the Department of Building Inspection. Citywide, KQED has reported that tens of thousands of people live in in-law units. This note did not find whether the ordinance has passed.

Sources: [Sunset Beacon, City Hall column by Alan Wong (July 2026)](https://richmondsunsetnews.com/2026/07/13/city-hall-alan-wong-7/), [KQED, Meet your new in-laws](https://www.kqed.org/news/10517326/meet-your-new-in-laws), [Data USA, Outer Sunset and Inner Sunset census area](https://datausa.io/profile/geo/san-francisco-county-southwest-outer-sunset-inner-sunset-puma-ca).

## Sunset home prices over time and the tech-era buyers of the 2010s

Sunset home prices climbed through the whole decade of the 2010s. A local real estate columnist, John M. Lee, wrote in the Sunset Beacon that the market bottomed in 2009 and that 2020 was the ninth year in a row of rising prices, with the median single-family house in the Sunset District selling for $1,510,500 that year. He put the demand down to booming tech and biotech jobs, buyers who could work from home, low mortgage rates (about 3 percent by the end of 2020), stock gains, and very few houses for sale: only about 376 to 379 sold each year from 2018 to 2020. In spring 2020, when the pandemic began, sales slowed sharply and prices dipped, then recovered by fall. Census estimates put the median property value in the combined Outer and Inner Sunset at $1.52 million in 2024. Rising prices matter to the neighborhood's makeup because a family that bought in the 1970s could not afford the same house today on a similar income.

Sources: [Richmond Review/Sunset Beacon, Real Estate by John M. Lee (January 2021)](https://richmondsunsetnews.com/2021/01/10/real-estate-john-m-lee-sunset-district/), [Data USA, Outer Sunset and Inner Sunset census area](https://datausa.io/profile/geo/san-francisco-county-southwest-outer-sunset-inner-sunset-puma-ca).

## The pandemic years in the Sunset: schools, shops and safety, 2020 to 2026

The pandemic changed daily life in the Sunset in ways still visible. The Upper Great Highway closed to cars in April 2020 so neighbors could exercise at a distance. New shops opened anyway: the SF Standard listed Palm City Wines (4055 Irving), Mr. Dragon (3914 Judah) and the California Kahve coffee trailer at La Playa and Lincoln among businesses that started in 2020 and 2021. Gus's Community Market at 3701 Noriega, run by a family on that street since 1981, stayed open as an essential business. Public schools lost students: the school district reported a 3.2 percent drop in 2020-21, kindergarten enrollment fell nearly 10 percent, and the Sunset was among the neighborhoods with the biggest drop in applications. Anti-Asian violence rose citywide, one reason the Sunset Chinese Cultural District was created in 2021. Recovery has been uneven. Mission Local reported in October 2026 that sales tax data show business west of 19th Avenue, in the Outer Sunset and Parkside, still below pre-pandemic levels.

Sources: [SF Standard, 7 Outer Sunset businesses that powered through the pandemic](https://sfstandard.com/2021/08/02/7-outer-sunset-businesses-that-powered-through-the-pandemic), [SF Standard, SFUSD enrollment drops (June 2021)](https://sfstandard.com/2021/06/02/sfusd-enrollment-drops-exacerbating-budget-woes/), [SF Administrative Code Chapter 107D](https://gocodebook.com/library/us/ca/san-francisco-admin/title-4-supplemental-provisions-4/chapter-107d-sunset-chinese-cultural-district), [Mission Local, Sunset business recovery (October 2026)](https://missionlocal.org/?p=874791).

## Who lives in the Sunset now: age, income and politics

The best current figures cover the Outer and Inner Sunset together, a census area of about 99,300 people in 2024. The median age was 43.6. About 46 percent of residents were Asian and about 34 percent white. Median household income was $153,489, and 57.1 percent of households owned their home. About 35 percent of residents were born abroad. Supervisor District 4, which takes in the Sunset, Parkside and Lakeshore, has about 50,000 registered voters. The SF Standard describes the district as a moderate stronghold with many homeowners and a large Asian American population. That mix showed in recent votes: District 4 voted 64 percent against closing the Great Highway in 2024, then recalled its supervisor over it in 2025. A breakdown of household types for the Outer Sunset alone (families with children, people living alone, multigenerational homes) was not found in the sources read for this note.
