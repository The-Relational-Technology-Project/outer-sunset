This note covers the sand itself: where Ocean Beach sand comes from, why the north end grows and the south end near Sloat erodes, the seawalls and the built dune, the plants and insects of the dunes, restoration work, and the sewer system buried under the Great Highway. It names what people's choices have changed.

## Where Ocean Beach sand comes from: the Sierra Nevada, the Bay and the San Francisco Bar

Most of the sand on Ocean Beach started in the Sierra Nevada. U.S. Geological Survey studies published in 2013 traced sand grains by their minerals and found that sand in the San Francisco Bay coastal system comes mainly from the Sierra, carried by the Sacramento and San Joaquin rivers, through the Bay and out the Golden Gate. Along the way it picks up a little chert and other local Franciscan rock. Outside the Gate the sand settles on a huge semicircular shoal called the San Francisco Bar (geologists call it the ebb-tidal delta), then turns south along the peninsula's beaches. Eroding sea cliffs add sand locally. The USGS also found that the Bar has been shrinking in both area and volume over the past century, and tied that to people: dredging channels and mining sand inside the Bay removes sand that would otherwise feed the Bar and the beach.

Sources: [USGS, Heavy mineral analysis for assessing the provenance of sandy sediment (2013)](https://www.usgs.gov/publications/heavy-mineral-analysis-assessing-provenance-sandy-sediment-san-francisco-bay-coastal), [USGS, Sand sources and transport pathways based on X-ray diffraction (2013)](https://www.usgs.gov/publications/sand-sources-and-transport-pathways-san-francisco-bay-coastal-system-based-x-ray), [USGS, Synthesis study of an erosion hot spot, Ocean Beach (2012)](https://www.usgs.gov/publications/synthesis-study-erosion-hot-spot-ocean-beach-california).

## Why the north end of Ocean Beach is wide and the south end near Sloat erodes

Ocean Beach gains sand at its north and middle and loses it south of Sloat Boulevard. Monthly USGS surveys from 2004 to 2020 show the shore south of Sloat retreating about 2.3 feet a year on average, and up to 4.7 feet a year near the sewer outfall, while the middle beach grew about 4.3 feet a year. The USGS explains it this way. The San Francisco Bar shelters part of the coast from storm waves but bends and focuses wave energy onto the central and southern beach. As the Bar has shrunk, the pattern of wave focusing and sand movement along the beach has changed. Scour around an exposed outfall pipe makes a local dip in wave height that changes currents and keeps that spot eroding. Sand also moves away from the Sloat area both north and south. The result a neighbor can see: a broad beach at the O'Shaughnessy seawall near the Cliff House, and rubble, rock and sandbags below the bluff south of Sloat.

Sources: [California Coastal Commission staff report, application 2-21-0912 (November 2024)](https://documents.coastal.ca.gov/reports/2024/11/Th10a/Th10a-11-2024-report.pdf), [USGS, Synthesis study of an erosion hot spot, Ocean Beach (2012)](https://www.usgs.gov/publications/synthesis-study-erosion-hot-spot-ocean-beach-california), [USGS, Recent scientific advances and their implications for sand management near San Francisco (2011)](https://www.usgs.gov/publications/recent-scientific-advances-and-their-implications-sand-management-near-san-francisco).

## Ship channel dredging and moving sand to the south end of Ocean Beach

The Army Corps of Engineers dredges sand from the San Francisco Main Ship Channel, which crosses the Bar outside the Golden Gate, and since 2005 some of that sand has been used to feed Ocean Beach. In May 2005 the Corps placed about 300,000 cubic yards just offshore of the erosion spot south of Sloat, in 30 to 45 feet of water, so waves and currents could carry it toward shore. In September 2021 dredged sand was placed on the beach itself, in berms south of Sloat; the Coastal Commission's report gives about 265,000 cubic yards in one place and 255,000 to 380,000 in others. The city and the Park Service also truck sand from the wide north end to the south end ("backpassing"): 77,000 cubic yards in 2012, 30,000 in 2014, and a high of 95,000 in 2016. A 2023 study found the 2021 placement may have changed the small animals living in the sand, so the Commission now requires yearly monitoring of them.

Sources: [USGS, Coastal monitoring of the May 2005 dredge disposal offshore of Ocean Beach](https://www.usgs.gov/publications/coastal-monitoring-may-2005-dredge-disposal-offshore-ocean-beach-san-francisco-calif), [California Coastal Commission staff report, application 2-21-0912 (November 2024)](https://documents.coastal.ca.gov/reports/2024/11/Th10a/Th10a-11-2024-report.pdf).

## The seawalls of Ocean Beach: O'Shaughnessy, Taraval, Noriega and the rock south of Sloat

Three seawalls and two rock piles hold the line along Ocean Beach. The O'Shaughnessy seawall, designed by City Engineer M.M. O'Shaughnessy, starts at the foot of the Cliff House hill. Work began in 1915, the first 670 feet were done by the end of 1916, and the wall, the Esplanade and the Great Highway opened together in 1929 (the Park Service gives 1928 for the wall itself). SPUR's Ocean Beach Master Plan maps a Taraval seawall dated 1941 and a Noriega to Rivera seawall from the 1980s, and counts about 13,000 of the beach's 20,000 feet of shore as armored. South of Sloat the city dumped 600 feet of rock in 1997 without a coastal permit and 440 more feet in 2010 under an emergency permit. The Coastal Commission refused to approve that rock in 2011 and told the city to find another way. The city had started work with SPUR in 2009 on the Ocean Beach Master Plan, finished in 2012.

Sources: [NPS, Vestiges of Ocean Beach](https://home.nps.gov/goga/learn/historyculture/vestiges-ocean-beach.htm), [NPS, Now and Then: Ocean Beach](https://www.nps.gov/goga/learn/photosmultimedia/now-and-then-ocean-beach.htm), [SPUR, Ocean Beach Master Plan open house boards (2011)](https://www.spur.org/sites/default/files/2013-10/OB_open%20house_ALL%20BOARDS_Part6.pdf), [California Coastal Commission staff report, application 2-21-0912 (November 2024)](https://documents.coastal.ca.gov/reports/2024/11/Th10a/Th10a-11-2024-report.pdf).

## Why sand blows onto the Great Highway, and the dune that people built

The dune ridge between Ocean Beach and the Great Highway is not natural. Before the road, the Coastal Commission's ecologists say, the beach ran straight into low, sparsely planted mounds and then waves of moving dunes that stretched inland for miles; wind carried sand freely. Building the Great Highway (finished 1929) pushed the shoreline about 200 feet seaward on fill. More fill was added in 1984, in the years the road was rebuilt along with the new sewer works, making a ridge about 40 feet high. The wind still wants to move sand east. Where foot traffic kills the plants, the wind cuts troughs called blowouts, worst at Lincoln, Judah and Noriega, and sand pours through onto the road. Bay Nature reported the Upper Great Highway closed for sand up to 65 days a year after 2020. The San Francisco Estuary Institute's 2023 report, Growing Resilience, suggested narrowing the roadway to give the sand room.

Sources: [Bay Nature, Along With Its New Park, Ocean Beach Gets a Dunescaping (March 2025)](https://baynature.org/?p=234076), [California Coastal Commission staff report, application 2-21-0912 (November 2024)](https://documents.coastal.ca.gov/reports/2024/11/Th10a/Th10a-11-2024-report.pdf), [Surfrider San Francisco, Climate Action](https://sf.surfrider.org/san-francisco-climate-action).

## European beachgrass and iceplant on the Ocean Beach dunes

Two plants from other continents cover most of the Ocean Beach dunes, and both were put there to hold sand. European beachgrass, or marram grass (Ammophila arenaria), was planted in the 1980s to pin down the newly built dune. It forms dense, uniform stands that crowd out native grasses. Iceplant (Carpobrotus edulis), from coastal South Africa, has shallow roots and holds little sand, but it shrugs off trampling. Bay Nature reported in 2025 that iceplant has overtaken the beachgrass and now dominates the dunes south of Rivera Street. Iceplant is named as a threat to the endangered San Francisco lessingia elsewhere in the city. The Coastal Commission has pointed to removing Ammophila from Ocean Beach, and iceplant from the Fort Funston dunes, as ways the city can make up for dune habitat lost to its seawall project south of Sloat.

Sources: [Bay Nature, Along With Its New Park, Ocean Beach Gets a Dunescaping (March 2025)](https://baynature.org/?p=234076), [California Coastal Commission staff report, application 2-21-0912 (November 2024)](https://documents.coastal.ca.gov/reports/2024/11/Th10a/Th10a-11-2024-report.pdf), [California Department of Fish and Wildlife, San Francisco Lessingia](https://wildlife.ca.gov/Conservation/Plants/Endangered/Lessingia-germanorum).

## Native dune plants of Ocean Beach: sand verbena, beach bur, beach strawberry and beach wildrye

Patches of the original dune plant community survive on Ocean Beach south of Sloat. The Coastal Commission's 2024 review found yellow sand verbena (Abronia latifolia) and beach bur (Ambrosia chamissonis) growing together as "dune mat," a community the state ranks as vulnerable. With them grow beach sagewort (Artemisia pycnocephala), beach strawberry (Fragaria chiloensis), coast buckwheat (Eriogonum latifolium) and beach evening primrose (Camissoniopsis cheiranthifolia). These plants survive by going deep. Yellow sand verbena has a taproot that can reach eight feet down, which is why the Commission required deeper sand over the city's planned buried wall. The native dune grass, beach wildrye (Leymus mollis, also called Elymus mollis), grows wild near the foot of Irving Street, over a freshwater seep that keeps the sand moist. Friends of Sunset Dunes also lists yarrow, coyote brush and California goldenweed among the natives along the park. Ecologists favor wildrye over European beachgrass because it resists and recovers from blowouts better.

Sources: [California Coastal Commission staff report, application 2-21-0912 (November 2024)](https://documents.coastal.ca.gov/reports/2024/11/Th10a/Th10a-11-2024-report.pdf), [Surfrider San Francisco, Climate Action](https://sf.surfrider.org/san-francisco-climate-action), [Sunset Dunes, Visit](https://sunsetdunes.org/visit).

## Dune restoration at Sunset Dunes park: beach wildrye at Judah, 2025 and 2026

Sunset Dunes, the two-mile park on the former Upper Great Highway from Lincoln Way to Sloat Boulevard, opened April 12, 2025, and dune repair is part of it. In winter 2024 to 2025 about 100 volunteers, led by San Francisco Recreation and Parks, dug up native beach wildrye from a healthy stand and replanted more than 1,500 plants in the blowout at Judah Street over five days, then roped it off. Planner Brian Stokle leads the work, which follows the San Francisco Estuary Institute's 2023 report and is paid for by a $1 million State Coastal Conservancy grant. Yellow bush lupine and Chamisso's lupine come next. Partners include the Park Service, the California Academy of Sciences and Surfrider. A volunteer planting day was set for November 14, 2026. Voters created the park with Proposition K in November 2024 and kept it on November 3, 2026, when about 63 percent voted against Proposition G, which would have returned cars. Dogs must be leashed and kept off the dunes.

Sources: [Bay Nature, Along With Its New Park, Ocean Beach Gets a Dunescaping (March 2025)](https://baynature.org/?p=234076), [Sunset Dunes, Visit](https://sunsetdunes.org/visit), [SF Recreation and Parks, Sunset Dunes](https://sfrecpark.org/GreatHighwayPilot), [Richmond Review/Sunset Beacon, dune replanting press release (February 2025)](https://richmondsunsetnews.com/2025/02/13/press-release-ocean-beach-sand-dune-replanting-project-launches/), [KQED, San Francisco votes to keep cars off the Great Highway (November 3, 2026)](https://www.kqed.org/news/12107229/san-francisco-votes-to-keep-cars-off-the-great-highway-again).

## Rare dune plants of San Francisco: lessingia, spineflower and wallflower, and where they survive

The dunes that once covered about a third of San Francisco, including the whole Sunset, had plants found almost nowhere else. San Francisco lessingia (Lessingia germanorum) is a small yellow-flowered annual, endangered under state and federal law, that needs open, shifting sand. Adelbert von Chamisso collected it at the Presidio in 1816. The state now lists eight sites: seven in the Presidio and one near Hillside Park in Daly City, and suggests Fort Funston as a place to start a new population. At Lobos Creek in the Presidio it rebounded into the millions after dune restoration. San Francisco spineflower (Chorizanthe cuspidata) is a rare plant of sandy coastal ground known mainly from around San Francisco; it is among the dune scrub plants at Fort Funston and has been found very near the bluffs south of Sloat. San Francisco wallflower (Erysimum franciscanum), with cream to yellow four-petaled flowers from late winter to spring, was first described from the Presidio and is grown in the nursery there for replanting.

Sources: [California Department of Fish and Wildlife, San Francisco Lessingia](https://wildlife.ca.gov/Conservation/Plants/Endangered/Lessingia-germanorum), [CNPS Yerba Buena Chapter, Lessingia](https://cnps-yerbabuena.org/lessingia-lessingia-germanorum/), [Bay Nature, Happy Plants on New Presidio Dunes](https://baynature.org/article/happy-plants-on-new-presidio-dunes/), [California Coastal Commission staff report, application 2-21-0912 (November 2024)](https://documents.coastal.ca.gov/reports/2024/11/Th10a/Th10a-11-2024-report.pdf), [Bay Nature, Beachfront Property](https://baynature.org/?p=68391), [Wikipedia, Erysimum franciscanum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Erysimum_franciscanum).

## The Xerces blue butterfly, lost from the Sunset dunes, and the silvery blue brought in to replace it

The Xerces blue (Glaucopsyche xerces) was a small blue butterfly that lived only in San Francisco's coastal dunes, from the Presidio through what is now the Richmond and Sunset to Fort Funston. It was described in 1852. Houses and streets replaced the dunes, and with them went deerweed (Acmispon glaber), the plant its caterpillars ate. Accounts differ on the last sighting: 1941 or 1943, on land now in the Golden Gate National Recreation Area. It is counted as the first American butterfly driven extinct by development. A 2021 study of museum specimens confirmed it was its own species, closest to the silvery blue (Glaucopsyche lygdamus). In April 2024 the Presidio Trust and the California Academy of Sciences released silvery blues from Monterey County into restored Presidio dunes to take over its role as a pollinator, and more were released in 2025. All the releases were in the Presidio.

Sources: [Presidio Trust, Bringing Silvery Blue Butterflies to the Presidio (April 2024)](https://presidio.gov/about/press/bringing-silvery-blue-butterflies-to-the-presidio), [Wikipedia, Xerces blue](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Xerces_blue), [Bay Nature, Along With Its New Park, Ocean Beach Gets a Dunescaping (March 2025)](https://baynature.org/?p=234076).

## Restored dunes near the Outer Sunset: Fort Funston and Lobos Creek in the Presidio

Two places show what the Sunset's dunes looked like. Fort Funston, just south of the zoo, holds the largest dune field left on the San Francisco peninsula. The Army planted iceplant, cypress, acacia and eucalyptus there in the 1930s to hide its guns. The Park Service has been pulling iceplant and planting dune scrub (mock heather, lupine, seaside daisy, coast buckwheat) for decades and runs a native plant nursery by the main lot. A study published in 2009 compared plots and found the actively restored area had more native plants, birds and ground animals than areas that were only fenced off. Keeping people out helped only a little; replanting did the work. At Lobos Creek Valley in the Presidio, restored starting in 1994, a boardwalk crosses dunes where San Francisco lessingia now grows by the millions.

Sources: [NPS, Fort Funston](https://www.nps.gov/goga/planyourvisit/fortfunston.htm), [Bay Nature, Beachfront Property](https://baynature.org/?p=68391), [USGS, Evaluating wildlife response to coastal dune habitat restoration in San Francisco (2009)](https://www.usgs.gov/publications/evaluating-wildlife-response-coastal-dune-habitat-restoration-san-francisco-california), [Golden Gate National Parks Conservancy, Lobos Creek](https://www.parksconservancy.org/projects/lobos-creek), [Bay Nature, Happy Plants on New Presidio Dunes](https://baynature.org/article/happy-plants-on-new-presidio-dunes/).

## The Ocean Beach Climate Change Adaptation Project south of Sloat, and sea level rise

The city's plan for the eroding shore south of Sloat is the Ocean Beach Climate Change Adaptation Project, run by the San Francisco Public Utilities Commission. It would close the Great Highway between Sloat and Skyline for good, remove the rock and rubble, build a buried seawall about 3,200 feet long to protect the Lake Merced Tunnel, cover it with sand and native plants, add a trail of more than a mile, and keep adding sand. The Coastal Commission approved it on November 14, 2024, with an estimated cost of about $175 million. As of late 2026 the SFPUC expected street changes at Sloat in 2027 and main construction from 2029, lasting four years. The state guidance the Commission used projects 1.4 to 2.2 feet of sea level rise at San Francisco by 2070 and 2.4 to 3.8 feet by 2090, with as much as 6.5 feet by 2100 in the high case. A one-foot rise moves the shoreline roughly 40 feet inland on a typical sandy beach.

Sources: [SFPUC, Ocean Beach Climate Change Adaptation Project](https://www.sfpuc.gov/construction-contracts/construction-projects/oceanbeach), [California Coastal Commission staff report, application 2-21-0912 (November 2024)](https://documents.coastal.ca.gov/reports/2024/11/Th10a/Th10a-11-2024-report.pdf).

## The sewer under the Great Highway: the Westside box, the Lake Merced Tunnel and the Oceanside plant

Under the Great Highway from Lincoln to Sloat lies the Westside Transport/Storage Structure, a concrete box that holds 49.3 million gallons of mixed sewage and storm water. San Francisco has a combined sewer, so rain and sewage share pipes. The box catches storm surges and feeds them to the Westside Pump Station at Sloat. South of Sloat the Lake Merced Tunnel, 18 feet across and finished in 1993, adds 10 million gallons of storage. The Oceanside Water Pollution Control Plant, built in 1993 beside the zoo and about 70 percent underground, replaced a 1935 plant in Golden Gate Park. It gives full secondary treatment to 43 million gallons a day and partial treatment to 22 million more in storms. Treated water leaves through the Southwest Ocean Outfall, a 4.5-mile pipe that ends about 3.8 miles offshore in 78 feet of water. The whole system was built from the late 1970s to the early 1990s under the federal Clean Water Act to stop frequent sewage overflows onto the beach.

Sources: [Regional Water Board and EPA, Oceanside permit, Order R2-2019-0028](https://www.waterboards.ca.gov/sanfranciscobay/board_decisions/adopted_orders/2019/R2-2019-0028.pdf), [SPUR, Invisible Infrastructure: the Oceanside Water Pollution Control Plant (2014)](https://spur.org/news/2014-04-11/invisible-infrastructure-ocean-beach-oceanside-water-pollution-control-plant), [California Coastal Commission staff report, application 2-21-0912 (November 2024)](https://documents.coastal.ca.gov/reports/2024/11/Th10a/Th10a-11-2024-report.pdf).

## Combined sewer discharges at Ocean Beach and how the water is tested

In the biggest storms the sewer under the Great Highway fills, and the overflow, settled and skimmed but not fully treated, goes into the surf at Ocean Beach. There are three discharge points on Ocean Beach: at Lincoln Way, at Vicente Street, and at Fort Funston (called the Lake Merced discharge). The system was designed for a long-term average of eight discharges a year. In the very wet year of 1997 to 1998 Lincoln and Vicente each discharged 13 times; in dry 2000 to 2001, not at all. After a discharge the city must post "No Swimming" signs within four hours and keep them up until tests pass. The SFPUC samples the surf every week for enterococcus and coliform bacteria at the foot of Balboa Street, Lincoln Way and Sloat Boulevard, and daily after a discharge, for up to seven days. City data for 2012 to 2017 show between 7 and 35 discharges a year, the most in the wet winter of 2016 to 2017.

Sources: [Regional Water Board and EPA, Oceanside permit, Order R2-2019-0028](https://www.waterboards.ca.gov/sanfranciscobay/board_decisions/adopted_orders/2019/R2-2019-0028.pdf), [California Coastal Commission staff report, application 2-21-0912 (November 2024)](https://documents.coastal.ca.gov/reports/2024/11/Th10a/Th10a-11-2024-report.pdf).
