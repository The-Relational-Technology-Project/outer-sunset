This note covers how trains, streetcars and two tunnels tied the Outer Sunset to the rest of San Francisco, and what the beach end of the neighborhood was like from the 1880s to about 1930. It gives the date each thing opened and what it replaced, and it flags the places where sources give different dates.

## The Park and Ocean Railroad on H Street (Lincoln Way): steam trains from 1883, streetcars from 1898

The first public transit to the Sunset was the Park and Ocean Railroad, a steam train that opened on Saturday, December 1, 1883. It was run by the Southern Pacific interests that owned the Haight Street cable line, and it replaced a costly carriage trip: the 1863 toll road to the Cliff House charged $1 round trip. The train left Haight and Stanyan, cut across the southeast corner of Golden Gate Park, and ran west along H Street, now Lincoln Way. Between today's 47th and 48th Avenues it turned north through the park and ended at Balboa and La Playa. The fare was 20 cents round trip, and a reported 10,000 people rode the first day. The Park Commission had refused the route through the park, and the railroad got it only after its allies took over the commission. The line was electrified in 1898 and became the 7 Haight streetcar. Streetcars stopped running through the park in 1947. The old roadbed is the Railroad Trail behind the Beach Chalet.

Sources: [A railroad to Ocean Beach (Gary Kamiya, SF Chronicle)](https://www.sfgate.com/bayarea/article/A-railroad-to-Ocean-Beach-How-the-Octopus-11177030.php), [Park and Ocean Railroad (FoundSF)](https://foundsf.org/Park_and_Ocean_Railroad), [Streetcars in the Sunset (Market Street Railway)](https://www.streetcar.org/streetcars_in_the_sunset_troll-2/).

## The 20th Avenue streetcar, the Parkside "Dinky" and the 12 line on Sloat Boulevard

Before the tunnels, two slow private streetcar routes served the south and middle of the Sunset. The Parkside Transit Company, backed by the developers of the Parkside, won its franchise in October 1907 and laid a single track in June 1908. It ran down 20th Avenue from Lincoln Way to Taraval, and a local car nicknamed the "Dinky" continued west on Taraval to 33rd Avenue and south to Sloat until 1927. In 1916 United Railroads turned the 20th Avenue track into the 17 line to downtown, which lasted until 1945. On Sloat Boulevard, the 12 line ran from the Ferry Building out Mission Street and Ocean Avenue to the beach, the longest line in the city. Two planning histories date the Sloat service from 1909 to 1945. The Oceanside context statement says the 12 began around 1902. The wide median on Sloat was built to carry its tracks. Both routes wound around Twin Peaks, so the ride downtown was long.

Sources: [San Francisco's Parkside District 1905-1957, Historic Context Statement (Western Neighborhoods Project, SF Planning)](https://archives.sfplanning.org/documents/4976-parkside-statement%20march%202008.pdf), [Streetcars in the Sunset (Market Street Railway)](https://www.streetcar.org/streetcars_in_the_sunset_troll-2/), [Sunset District Residential Builders 1925-1950, Historic Context Statement (SF Planning)](https://default.sfplanning.org/Preservation/sunset_survey/Adopted_SunsetHCS.pdf), [Historic Context Statement of the Oceanside (SPEAK, SF Planning)](https://default.sfplanning.org/Preservation/oceanside_survey/2010_Oceanside_Historic_Context.pdf).

## 1909: the Sunset's lettered streets become Irving, Judah, Kirkham and the rest

Until 1909 the Sunset's east-west streets had letters, from H Street next to the park down to X Street. The Bayview used a similar scheme, and mail went astray. In 1909 the Board of Supervisors' street naming commission assigned names that kept the alphabetical order. H Street became Lincoln Way, in the centennial year of Abraham Lincoln's birth. Then came Irving (for writer Washington Irving), Judah (for railroad engineer Theodore Judah), Kirkham and Lawton (military officers), Moraga, Noriega, Ortega and Pacheco. South of Pacheco, the Parkside Realty Company had already chosen Quintara, Rivera, Santiago, Taraval, Ulloa, Vicente and Wawona. The commission first wanted Spanish names for the numbered avenues too. Residents of the Richmond and Sunset fought that, in part from anti-Spanish feeling after the Spanish-American War, and the avenues kept their numbers. Only 1st Avenue (Arguello) and 49th Avenue (La Playa) changed. Accounts differ on X Street: KQED says it was skipped as unpronounceable, and other histories say it became Yorba.

Sources: [From Anza to Yorba: The Messy History Behind the Richmond's and Sunset's Street Names (KQED)](https://www.kqed.org/news/12079532/from-anza-to-yorba-the-messy-history-behind-the-richmond-and-sunsets-street-names), [Historic Context Statement of the Oceanside (SPEAK, SF Planning)](https://default.sfplanning.org/Preservation/oceanside_survey/2010_Oceanside_Historic_Context.pdf), [The Sunset District: From Dunes to Cityscape (Lorri Ungaretti, FoundSF)](https://foundsf.org/Changing_Physical_Landscape_of_the_Sunset).

## The Twin Peaks Tunnel (1918) and the L Taraval reaching the beach (1923)

The Twin Peaks Tunnel opened on February 3, 1918, with Mayor James Rolph driving Muni car 117 through it. It runs about 12,000 feet from Castro and Market to West Portal, cost about $4 million, and was paid for by assessments on the property owners who stood to gain. City Engineer M.M. O'Shaughnessy built it. It replaced the long way around the hills by the 12 and 17 lines. The L Taraval followed in stages. Muni laid track to 20th Avenue and Taraval in September 1918, and the first L cars ran on April 12, 1919, as a shuttle from West Portal to 33rd Avenue, sharing the private Parkside line's track. The rails crossed the dunes to the beach by early 1923 (sources say 46th or 48th Avenue), and by the end of 1923 L cars ran through the tunnel to the Ferry. In 1937 a WPA-funded extension took the L from 46th and Taraval south to Wawona, near the zoo.

Sources: [Tunnel Vision (Market Street Railway)](https://www.streetcar.org/tunnel-vision/), [One "L" of a Streetcar Line (Market Street Railway)](https://www.streetcar.org/?p=12271), [San Francisco's Parkside District 1905-1957, Historic Context Statement (Western Neighborhoods Project, SF Planning)](https://archives.sfplanning.org/documents/4976-parkside-statement%20march%202008.pdf).

## The Sunset Tunnel and the N Judah, opened October 21, 1928

The N Judah opened on Sunday, October 21, 1928, when Mayor James Rolph drove the first car through the new Sunset Tunnel to the end of the line at Judah and La Playa. Thousands turned out along the route. The tunnel, built between 1926 and 1928, runs about a mile and a quarter under Buena Vista Park, from Duboce Park to Carl and Cole Streets. The N gave the north side of the Sunset a direct ride downtown. Before it, people at the beach took the 7 line along Lincoln Way, which made every stop on Haight Street. The line ended in a loop at the Great Highway, about where car-houses of Carville had stood at 48th and Judah. The Market Street Railway's history notes that the building at Judah and La Playa has held a restaurant for as long as the N has run. In the mid-1970s part of the N's trackway was rebuilt as a raised right-of-way, but residents' objections kept that from reaching the beach.

Sources: [The N Judah Turns 87 (SFMTA)](https://www.sfmta.com/en/node/13351), [Streetcars in the Sunset (Market Street Railway)](https://www.streetcar.org/streetcars_in_the_sunset_troll-2/), [Sunset District Residential Builders 1925-1950, Historic Context Statement (SF Planning)](https://default.sfplanning.org/Preservation/sunset_survey/Adopted_SunsetHCS.pdf).

## What the tunnels and streetcars did to land sales and house building in the Sunset

Fast streetcars are the reason most Outer Sunset blocks were built when they were. Before the tunnels, buyers scoffed at the sand, and the Sunset had only three settled pockets by 1910: the Inner Sunset, Oceanside at the beach, and the Parkside. The planning department's history says the Twin Peaks Tunnel stirred growth slowly, mostly near West Portal, while the Sunset Tunnel had a more direct and immediate effect. The L and N lines run parallel about ten blocks apart, and building followed the tracks first. A 1930 photograph shows empty blocks at 31st Avenue and Lawton, and a 1938 aerial shows them built. Rowhouse builders such as Henry Doelger, the Gellert brothers and Oscar Heyman filled in the Oceanside from the mid-1920s, helped in the 1930s by low-interest federal home loans. After the N reached the beach, stucco houses, apartments and motels replaced the last 30 or so Carville cars. Even so, blocks near Sunset Boulevard were still dunes in 1937.

Sources: [Sunset District Residential Builders 1925-1950, Historic Context Statement (SF Planning)](https://default.sfplanning.org/Preservation/sunset_survey/Adopted_SunsetHCS.pdf), [Streetcars in the Sunset (Market Street Railway)](https://www.streetcar.org/streetcars_in_the_sunset_troll-2/), [Historic Context Statement of the Oceanside (SPEAK, SF Planning)](https://default.sfplanning.org/Preservation/oceanside_survey/2010_Oceanside_Historic_Context.pdf), [CARVILLE: Suburban Bohemia in Fin de Siecle San Francisco (Natalie Jahraus Cowan, FoundSF)](https://foundsf.org/CARVILLE:_Suburban_Bohemia_in_Fin_de_Siecle_San_Francisco).

## The 1906 earthquake, refugees at the beach and earthquake shacks moved to the Sunset

The 1906 earthquake and fire left more than half of San Francisco homeless and sent people west. Some who lost homes moved into the car-houses of Carville, and by 1908 the beach neighborhood had about 2,000 residents. There was no official refugee camp in the Sunset. The relief effort built 5,610 small redwood and fir cottages, painted park bench green, in parks elsewhere, including a large camp in the Richmond. As the camps closed in 1906 and 1907, residents got their rent back if they hauled their cottage to a private lot, and dozens came to the Sunset, where lots were cheap. Each was only 10 by 14 or 14 by 18 feet, so owners joined two to four into a house. Four shacks on outer Kirkham Street survived until 2005. Western Neighborhoods Project volunteers saved them, and one was shown downtown for the 2006 centennial. KQED reported in January 2026 that a saved cottage stands at the San Francisco Zoo.

Sources: [Historic Context Statement of the Oceanside (SPEAK, SF Planning)](https://default.sfplanning.org/Preservation/oceanside_survey/2010_Oceanside_Historic_Context.pdf), [San Francisco's Historic Relief Cottages (KQED)](https://www.kqed.org/news/12068602/san-franciscos-historic-relief-cottages-built-after-the-1906-earthquake-are-hidden-in-plain-sight), [The Sunset District: From Dunes to Cityscape (Lorri Ungaretti, FoundSF)](https://foundsf.org/Changing_Physical_Landscape_of_the_Sunset).

## The Great Highway, the Esplanade and the O'Shaughnessy seawall, 1910 to 1929

The Great Highway appears on the 1868 map that laid out the Outside Lands, but for decades it was only a beach road from H Street to Sloat. In 1910 the Oceanside Improvement Club lobbied the city to build a real road from the Beach Chalet south to Sloat Boulevard, hoping to lift property values. Crews graded away the dunes between the road and the beach. Wikipedia's account says that 1910 grading pushed enough sand onto the beach to bury the King Philip shipwreck. Starting in 1915, City Engineer M.M. O'Shaughnessy built the concrete Esplanade and seawall at the north end. The first 670 feet, from the foot of the Cliff House hill to the park, was finished by the end of 1916. Voters passed a boulevard bond in 1927, and the finished highway officially opened in 1929 (the National Park Service dates the seawall's completion to 1928). Since April 12, 2025, the two miles from Lincoln Way to Sloat have been Sunset Dunes park.

Sources: [Vestiges of Ocean Beach (National Park Service)](https://home.nps.gov/goga/learn/historyculture/vestiges-ocean-beach.htm), [Historic Context Statement of the Oceanside (SPEAK, SF Planning)](https://default.sfplanning.org/Preservation/oceanside_survey/2010_Oceanside_Historic_Context.pdf), [Now and Then: Ocean Beach (National Park Service)](https://www.nps.gov/goga/learn/photosmultimedia/now-and-then-ocean-beach.htm), [Great Highway (Wikipedia)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Highway), [Sunset Dunes (SF Recreation and Parks)](https://sfrecpark.org/1555/Great-Highway-Project).

## Fleishhacker Pool (1925), the zoo (1929), and Playland and the Lurline pier to the north

Fleishhacker Pool opened on April 23, 1925, by the San Francisco Zoo's account, at the south end of Ocean Beach near Sloat Boulevard. Park Commission president Herbert Fleishhacker led the project on 30 acres bought from the Spring Valley Water Company in 1922. The pool was 1,000 feet long and 150 feet wide, held six million gallons of seawater pumped in at high tide, and lifeguards patrolled it in rowboats. The Mother's Building opened beside it in 1925. The zoo was established there on June 12, 1929, with animals moved from Golden Gate Park. The pool closed in 1971 and became zoo parking in 2002. The other amusements were at the Richmond end of the beach. Chutes at the Beach began in 1913, added the Big Dipper roller coaster in 1922, and was renamed Playland in 1926. The Lurline pier, just south of Balboa Street, carried a pipe that drew seawater for downtown pools from the early 1890s. It was removed in 1967.

Sources: [Historic Sites: Fleishhacker Pool (San Francisco Zoo)](https://www.sfzoo.org/historic-sites-fleishhacker-pool/), [Vestiges of Ocean Beach (National Park Service)](https://home.nps.gov/goga/learn/historyculture/vestiges-ocean-beach.htm), [Ocean Beach Pier (Woody LaBounty, San Francisco Story)](https://www.sanfranciscostory.com/ocean-beach-pier/).

## Shipwrecks near Noriega Street and the Ocean Beach life-saving station: the King Philip (1878) and the Reporter (1902)

The shipwreck neighbors sometimes see at low tide near the foot of Noriega Street is the King Philip, a three-masted clipper built in Maine in 1856. On January 25, 1878, she was leaving the Golden Gate under tow when the tug let go to help another ship. Her anchors did not hold, and she drifted onto Ocean Beach. John Molloy bought the hulk and blasted off the upper hull for salvage. The rest sank into the sand. On March 13, 1902, the lumber schooner Reporter mistook a Cliff House light for the Point Bonita lighthouse and wrecked on the same spot. Her crew was saved by surfmen from the life-saving station at the northwest corner of Golden Gate Park, which opened in 1878 as California's first and worked until November 1951. Erosion uncovered the King Philip in the early 1980s, and it showed again in May 2007, November 2010 and spring 2011. It has been on the National Register since 1986. We found no confirmed sighting after 2011.

Sources: [Parks for the People: San Francisco County Stories (National Park Service)](https://www.nps.gov/goga/learn/historyculture/parks-for-the-people-san-francisco-story-panel.htm), [Vestiges of Shipwrecks (National Park Service)](https://home.nps.gov/goga/learn/historyculture/vestiges-shipwrecks.htm), [Vestiges of Ocean Beach (National Park Service)](https://home.nps.gov/goga/learn/historyculture/vestiges-ocean-beach.htm), [King Philip (clipper) (Wikipedia)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/King_Philip_(clipper)).

## Early schools and churches in the Outer Sunset, 1903 to 1923

The first school at the beach was the Oceanside Primary School, which started in 1903 in a room beneath a grocery store on 48th Avenue. In 1908 the city built a two-story shingled school on 43rd Avenue between Irving and Judah. It was torn down and rebuilt in 1913 and later renamed Francis Scott Key School. A wooden, half-timbered annex went up on 42nd Avenue around 1920 and still stood in 2010. Farther east, the first Parkside School opened in 1909 with four grades on Taraval near 31st Avenue, on land given by the Parkside Realty Company. Before that, the closest school for Parkside children was at Junipero Serra Boulevard. For churches, Carville had St. Andrew's by-the-Sea, an Episcopal church made of streetcars on 47th Avenue near Irving. St. Paul's Presbyterian, on Kirkham between 46th and 47th Avenues, was dedicated on September 9, 1906, and moved in 1923 to a new building at 43rd Avenue and Judah.

Sources: [Historic Context Statement of the Oceanside (SPEAK, SF Planning)](https://default.sfplanning.org/Preservation/oceanside_survey/2010_Oceanside_Historic_Context.pdf), [San Francisco's Parkside District 1905-1957, Historic Context Statement (Western Neighborhoods Project, SF Planning)](https://archives.sfplanning.org/documents/4976-parkside-statement%20march%202008.pdf).

## The first shops, roadhouses and restaurants near Judah, Taraval and the Great Highway

The earliest shops in the Outer Sunset clustered at 48th Avenue and Judah, long before the N line. The Oceanside and Park News operated there in 1905. The Oceanside Pharmacy, owned by Matilda Burns, opened on the northwest corner in 1906 or 1907. Harry D. Rupp's butcher shop, the Oceanside Market, was just south on 48th Avenue, and a bakery and a fuel and hay dealer followed by 1909. The Oceanside Electric Light Company built a plant on 47th Avenue near Kirkham in 1905. The beach also had roadhouses: three liquor sellers in 1901 and seven by 1914. "Shorty" Roberts built a large place at 2200 Great Highway about 1909. The 1866 Ocean Side House, at the Great Highway between Ulloa and Vicente, became Tait's-at-the-Beach from 1919 to 1931 and burned in 1940. On Taraval, Eugene Williams ran the Parkside's first store, and shops filled in from 17th to 24th Avenues in the 1920s. We found no record of the first shop on outer Irving.

Sources: [Historic Context Statement of the Oceanside (SPEAK, SF Planning)](https://default.sfplanning.org/Preservation/oceanside_survey/2010_Oceanside_Historic_Context.pdf), [San Francisco's Parkside District 1905-1957, Historic Context Statement (Western Neighborhoods Project, SF Planning)](https://archives.sfplanning.org/documents/4976-parkside-statement%20march%202008.pdf).

## The Sunset's early improvement clubs: Sunset District, Oceanside and Parkside

Improvement clubs were how early Sunset residents got streets, sewers and streetcars. The Sunset District Improvement Club formed in 1895 in the Inner Sunset, and by one account the neighborhood's name comes from it. At the beach, a first club formed in 1903. In 1904 Oceanside residents petitioned for H Street (Lincoln Way) to be graded through to the ocean, and it was finally paved between 1911 and 1913. The beach clubs kept changing names: the Oceanside Improvement Club (1909), Promotion Association (1910 to 1911), Improvement Association (1911 to 1913), and Oceanside Community Council in the 1920s. They met at Oceanside Hall on 48th Avenue and later at a hall on Kirkham Street. They won streetlights and sewers, planted 1,400 palms and shrubs in April 1910 to hold the sand, and fought new saloons. Because the men's club barred women, the Oceanside Women's Club formed in 1913. The Parkside District Improvement Club first met in autumn 1908 at 26th Avenue and Taraval.

Sources: [Historic Context Statement of the Oceanside (SPEAK, SF Planning)](https://default.sfplanning.org/Preservation/oceanside_survey/2010_Oceanside_Historic_Context.pdf), [San Francisco's Parkside District 1905-1957, Historic Context Statement (Western Neighborhoods Project, SF Planning)](https://archives.sfplanning.org/documents/4976-parkside-statement%20march%202008.pdf), [The Sunset District: From Dunes to Cityscape (Lorri Ungaretti, FoundSF)](https://foundsf.org/Changing_Physical_Landscape_of_the_Sunset).

## Lost lakes, creeks, wells and dunes under the Outer Sunset

Before streets, the Outer Sunset was sand dunes with ponds between them. Creeks running down from Golden Gate Heights were blocked by the dunes and pooled into seasonal ponds and lagoons. The largest were Lake Merced and Laguna Puerca, now Pine Lake in Stern Grove. Golden Gate Park's site alone had fourteen native lakes, and the Chain of Lakes and Elk Glen Lake are altered remnants of that system. Old maps labeled the district the "Great Sand Waste." Sand won some early fights: J Street (Judah) was graded to the beach in the 1890s, and after a couple of storms the dunes swallowed it. Fresh water lay close under the sand, so Carville and Oceanside lived on wells until Spring Valley Water Company laid mains in 1904, connecting 84 lots that year and 91 the next. A few well houses and a water tower on 47th Avenue survived into the 2000s. Streetcars also hauled street sweepings, mostly horse manure, to fill sandy gullies in the western park.
