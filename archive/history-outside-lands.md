This note follows the land that is now the Outer Sunset from 1776 to about 1900. It covers the ranchos next door, the "Outside Lands" title fight of the 1860s, the making of Golden Gate Park on sand, the first roadhouse, railroad, farms and explosives works, and the competing stories of how the Sunset got its name.

## Was the Sunset part of a Spanish or Mexican rancho? No, but its neighbors were

The Sunset dunes were never part of a rancho. The city's Parkside history says the Spanish and Mexicans largely ignored the area. It finds no record of grazing or crops here, though one source says Presidio cattle grazed at Lake Merced in 1798. Early maps mark the Sunset as "pueblo" lands, meaning land belonging to the town, as distinct from mission or military land.

Two Mexican land grants bordered it. To the south lay Rancho Laguna de la Merced, about 2,000 acres around Lake Merced, granted to José Antonio Galindo in 1835 and sold two years later to Francisco de Haro. His sons ran about 400 cattle there. To the east lay the 4,443-acre Rancho San Miguel, granted to José Noé in 1845. Adolph Sutro bought the remaining 1,200 acres west of Twin Peaks in 1880.

That pueblo status is why the later fight over the Sunset was between the city and the federal government, not rancho heirs.

Sources: [SF Planning, Parkside District Historic Context Statement, 2008](https://archives.sfplanning.org/documents/4976-parkside-statement%20march%202008.pdf), [SF Planning, Sunset District Residential Builders 1925 to 1950, Historic Context Statement](https://default.sfplanning.org/Preservation/sunset_survey/Adopted_SunsetHCS.pdf).

## Why the west side was called the Outside Lands and the Great Sand Waste

"Outside Lands" was a legal term before it was a nickname. San Francisco's 1851 charter drew the city limit near Divisadero Street. The U.S. Supreme Court later explained that land beyond that charter line was called "outside lands" in city and state law. It took in today's Golden Gate Park, the Richmond and the Sunset. Gary Kamiya, in the Chronicle, puts it at 17,776 acres, most of it sand. The Museum of the City of San Francisco says about 17,000.

The mapmakers were blunter. An 1853 map labels the west side the "Great Sand Bank," KQED reports. Other maps called the Sunset the "Great Sand Waste" and marked it "unfit for cultivation." An 1861 map by V. Wackenrueder labels a large part of the Sunset "Seal Rock Rancho," probably after the rocks near the Cliff House.

The name lives on in the Outside Lands music festival in Golden Gate Park and in the Western Neighborhoods Project's website, outsidelands.org.

## The fight over who owned the Outside Lands, 1851 to 1866

For fifteen years nobody knew who owned the Sunset dunes. San Francisco claimed four square leagues of land as successor to the Mexican pueblo. The federal government said the western land lay outside the pueblo and belonged to the United States.

The city filed its claim with the federal land commission, which in December 1854 confirmed only part of it. Appeals sat in court for nearly ten years. On May 18, 1865, the U.S. Circuit Court confirmed the city's title to the full four leagues, bounded on the west by the Pacific.

Meanwhile squatters, some wealthy and well connected, had fenced claims in the dunes, betting on a federal win and free homesteads. After the ruling, Kamiya writes, powerful squatters apparently persuaded Congress to act. The act of March 8, 1866 gave up the federal claim, but required the city to convey the land to people in actual possession, except parcels the city set aside for public use.

Sources: [Trenouth v. ## The Outside Lands ordinance of 1868: how squatters got title and the city got its parks

The 1866 act left the city needing the squatters' consent to keep any Outside Lands for public use. Supervisor Frank McCoppin, elected mayor in 1867, brokered the deal. Occupants gave up about a tenth of their holdings and paid an assessment. In return they got clear title without more lawsuits.

The compromise was approved by the mayor and Board of Supervisors on January 14, 1868 and ratified by the state legislature on March 27, 1868. The city's Parkside history dates final approval to 1869. The Supreme Court later described the ordinance, No. 800, as a general assessment on all lands deeded to occupants, with the money used to compensate people whose land was taken for parks and other public purposes.

During the talks the city hired George C. Potter and William T. Humphrey to map the Outside Lands. Their plat, finished May 18, 1868, drew the grid of avenues and lettered streets the Sunset still has. Most of those streets existed only on paper for decades.

## Squatters and early claimants in the Sunset dunes: the Greenes, Reynolds and Brooks

The first known residents of the Sunset were the Greene family, who arrived during the Mexican-American War of 1846 to 1848 and claimed land from today's Stern Grove west toward the beach. They grew barley and potatoes, raised cattle and hogs, and planted bunch grass and eucalyptus to hold the sand. A lawsuit in 1887 cut their holding down to a few blocks between Wawona and Sloat.

Cornelius Reynolds, from Wiltshire, England, homesteaded at what is now 14th Avenue and Lincoln Way in the 1860s and ran a hog ranch. The 1870 census counted 70 swine. His claim was validated in 1870.

Near the ocean, land attorney Benjamin S. Brooks at one point owned 52 blocks of dunes. Historian Woody LaBounty describes overlapping claims on 1860s maps, with fences put up by day and torn down at night. The Oceanside history adds that several dairy ranches started in the Sunset in the 1860s.

## How Golden Gate Park was created in 1870 at the edge of the Sunset

Golden Gate Park, the Outer Sunset's northern border, grew out of the Outside Lands settlement. The public push for a big park began in 1865. Frederick Law Olmsted, co-designer of New York's Central Park, advised against the dunes. He doubted trees would grow there and proposed smaller linked parks to the east.

The western site won anyway, because, as park historian Terence Young put it, a park there solved the Outside Lands problem. In 1870 the state confirmed the deal and let the city sell $225,000 in bonds. William Hammond Hall, a 24-year-old civil engineer, won the survey contract with a low bid of $4,860 and began work in August 1870. He became the first park superintendent on August 14, 1871.

One side story: Mayor McCoppin held stock in a land-grading company and stood to profit if the park's hills were leveled. Hall kept the hills as windbreaks. Hall resigned in 1876 after a political fight with a legislator he had once fired.

Sources: [San Francisco Chronicle, How Golden Gate Park ended up in the Outside Lands](https://www.sfchronicle.com/chronicle_vault/article/How-Golden-Gate-Park-ended-up-in-the-Outside-Lands-15541522.php), [KQED, Golden Gate Park Was Once Miles and Miles of Sand Dunes](https://www.kqed.org/news/11915008/golden-gate-park-was-once-miles-and-miles-of-sand-dunes), [Museum of the City of San Francisco, Golden Gate Park](https://sfmuseum.org/hist2/ggpark.html).

## How the west end of Golden Gate Park was built on sand: barley, lupine and manure

The west end of Golden Gate Park, across Lincoln Way from the Outer Sunset, was open dune. William Hammond Hall's method was to sow fast-sprouting barley together with native lupine. The barley held the sand for a season while the lupine rooted. Trees could follow.

The famous story says a horse's spilled barley feed sprouted in the sand and gave Hall the idea. Nicole Meldahl of the Western Neighborhoods Project told KQED the pieces may be true but the neat version is doubtful. Barley already grew on a farm at the park's east end, and Europeans were already using quick grasses to hold coastal sand.

Hall worked with the dunes, planting the sheltered lee sides and valley floors first. Near the ocean, his hardest spot, he built a fence so sand would pile against it, then planted behind it. Street sweepings, mostly horse manure, were hauled out as fertilizer, later by streetcar along Lincoln Way. John McLaren, hired in 1890, ran the park for about 50 years.

Sources: [KQED, Golden Gate Park Was Once Miles and Miles of Sand Dunes](https://www.kqed.org/news/11915008/golden-gate-park-was-once-miles-and-miles-of-sand-dunes), [SF Planning, Oceanside Historic Context Statement, 2010](https://default.sfplanning.org/Preservation/oceanside_survey/2010_Oceanside_Historic_Context.pdf).

## The Golden Gate Park windmills and the Beach Chalet at the Outer Sunset's corner

The two windmills at the west end of Golden Gate Park were built to water it. In the 1880s the park was paying the Spring Valley Water Company about $1,000 a month, and the company shut off the water more than once. Park officials found groundwater under the park and decided to pump it with the ocean wind.

Work on the Dutch Windmill, at the park's northwest corner, began May 23, 1902 and took a year. By June 1903 it pumped 15,000 to 20,000 gallons an hour. Banker S.G. Murphy gave $20,000 in 1905 for a second, larger mill near Lincoln Way. KQED says the Murphy Windmill was finished in 1908. Many other accounts say 1907. Electric pumps replaced both by 1913. The Dutch Windmill was restored in 1981 and the Murphy in 2011.

The Beach Chalet, between them on the Great Highway, opened in 1925 to a design by Willis Polk. It replaced an 1892 chalet that stood on the ocean side of the road.

Sources: [KQED, Golden Gate Park's Windmills Were Essential, Then Abandoned for Decades](https://www.kqed.org/news/11915313/golden-gate-parks-windmills-were-essential-then-abandoned-for-decades), [Wikipedia, Beach Chalet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Beach_Chalet).

## The Ocean Side House and other early Ocean Beach roadhouses

The Ocean Side House was the first known building in what is now the Outer Sunset, and the Oceanside neighborhood took its name from it. It stood on the Great Highway between today's Ulloa and Vicente streets. Land attorney Benjamin S. Brooks borrowed $40,000 to build it, sure Ocean Beach would become a resort. It was two stories, about 155 by 115 feet. An 1869 map shows it alone in the dunes, almost a mile from any other building.

Accounts differ on its start. The Oceanside history says it was built in 1866 and appears in city directories from 1867. The Parkside history says advertisements show it as early as 1857. Brooks lost it to foreclosure before he died in 1879. It later became a private home, then Tait's-at-the-Beach from 1919 to 1931, and burned in 1940.

To the north, the Seal Rock House opened in 1858 and the Cliff House in 1863. Inland, near Lake Merced, Cornelius Staggs ran the Ocean House, with a racetrack from 1865 to 1873.

Sources: [SF Planning, Oceanside Historic Context Statement, 2010](https://default.sfplanning.org/Preservation/oceanside_survey/2010_Oceanside_Historic_Context.pdf), [SF Planning, Parkside District Historic Context Statement, 2008](https://archives.sfplanning.org/documents/4976-parkside-statement%20march%202008.pdf), [National Park Service, Vestiges of Ocean Beach](https://home.nps.gov/goga/learn/historyculture/vestiges-ocean-beach.htm).

## Early roads through the Sunset dunes and the Park and Ocean Railroad of 1883

Before 1883 only dirt tracks crossed the Sunset. The Central Ocean Road ran from about today's Parnassus Avenue west through the Inner Sunset, south along 20th Avenue and around Pine Lake. An 1885 guidebook said drifting sand covered part of it. The Ocean Road followed today's Ocean Avenue to the beach. The Great Highway ran along Ocean Beach from H Street (now Lincoln Way) to Sloat.

The Park and Ocean Railroad changed that. Grading started October 5, 1883 and steam trains began running December 1, 1883, from Haight and Stanyan out H Street to the beach, then north across the park's west end. It was controlled by the Central Pacific's owners, who FoundSF says got it through the park after allies joined the park commission. Thousands rode the first Sunday.

Electric streetcars replaced steam in 1898. Even so, Oceanside residents had no graded street to town until Lincoln Way was paved in 1911 to 1913. Judah Street, graded in the 1890s, was buried by sand after a couple of storms.

## Dynamite factories in the Sunset dunes in the 1870s

The empty Sunset dunes were used for making explosives, because few neighbors were close enough to be hurt. Two companies had plants here, both east of today's Sunset Boulevard, and both blew up.

The Giant Powder Company, the first American maker of dynamite under Alfred Nobel's license, moved to the dunes after its first factory in Glen Canyon exploded in November 1869. Sources place the Sunset plant differently. The city's Parkside history says five acres between 19th and 23rd avenues and K and N streets (Kirkham and Noriega). A company history quoted by the Glen Park Neighborhoods History Project describes about 100 acres from Kirkham to Ortega and 20th to 32nd avenues. They agree it exploded in 1879, in January by the second account, and that the company then left for the Berkeley shore.

The California Powder Works, also called the Hercules Powder Works, ran a plant near Fourth Avenue in the Inner Sunset. Explosions in 1870, 1872 and 1877 closed it.

Sources: [SF Planning, Parkside District Historic Context Statement, 2008](https://archives.sfplanning.org/documents/4976-parkside-statement%20march%202008.pdf), [Glen Park Neighborhoods History Project, Giant Powder Company landmark file](https://www.glenparkhistory.org/_files/ugd/7d681f_fc58de7abe1b4f6093ba44596505aec7.pdf), [Wikipedia, Giant Powder Company](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Giant_Powder_Company).

## How the Sunset got its name: Aurelius Buckingham, the Midwinter Fair, or an improvement club

Nobody is sure who named the Sunset District. There are at least three stories.

The oldest credits Aurelius E. Buckingham, a real estate speculator who owned property here. Historian Lorri Ungaretti reports the claim that he coined the name in 1886. The city's Oceanside history says only that some historians assume he named it in the 1880s.

A second story ties the name to the California Midwinter Fair of 1894 in Golden Gate Park, which was nicknamed "The Sunset City."

A third comes from researcher Angus Macfarlane. He found that residents meeting at 9th Avenue and H Street (now Lincoln Way) in 1895 formed the Sunset District Improvement Club. The name was suggested by a member named Gus Fox, by one telling after the group watched a fine sunset. Macfarlane found the word "Sunset" used for the district in a newspaper for the first time on October 1, 1895.

Before that, sellers tried "South Side," "Parkside" and "South of the Park," writes Woody LaBounty. Developer Sol Getz is sometimes credited.

Sources: [FoundSF, The Sunset District: From Dunes to Cityscape, by Lorri Ungaretti](https://www.foundsf.org/index.php?title=Changing_Physical_Landscape_of_the_Sunset), [SF Planning, Oceanside Historic Context Statement, 2010](https://default.sfplanning.org/Preservation/oceanside_survey/2010_Oceanside_Historic_Context.pdf), [San Francisco Story, Scrubland Living](https://www.sanfranciscostory.com/scrubland-living/).

## The 1894 Midwinter Fair and the first houses near Ocean Beach

The California Midwinter Fair of 1894, in Golden Gate Park, brought crowds to the Sunset for the first time. Its main entrance was at Ninth Avenue and H Street (now Lincoln Way), and the groundbreaking alone drew 60,000 people. Sewer and water lines, street grading and shops followed. By 1900 about 100 houses stood in the Inner Sunset.

The fair did little for the dunes farther west. Two miles of sand still separated the Inner Sunset from the beach. Out there, after the Ocean Side House, the next recorded dwelling belonged to Colonel Charles E. Dailey, a Civil War veteran in poor health. In 1893 Adolph Sutro let him live on Sutro's land at Lincoln Way and the Great Highway, in an old real estate office by one report, in a driftwood house by another.

By 1895 retired horsecars, sold for about $20 each, were hauled to Sutro's block bounded by Lincoln, Irving, 48th Avenue and La Playa. That cluster became Carville, then Oceanside.

## Farms, dairies and chicken ranches in the Sunset before 1900

Farming in the Sunset before 1900 was scattered, because most of the land was loose sand that maps marked "unfit for cultivation." What there was sat in the few sheltered or fertile spots.

Several dairy ranches started in the 1860s, and Cornelius Reynolds kept hogs at 14th Avenue and Lincoln Way. In 1888 or 1889 Carl Larsen, a Danish immigrant who owned the Tivoli Café downtown, began buying dune land and started a chicken ranch to supply his restaurant with eggs. Sources place it around 16th to 18th avenues, either between Moraga and Noriega or between Moraga and Ortega. A wagon took eggs downtown each morning. Larsen later gave the city the land for Larsen Park, at 19th Avenue and Ulloa.

South of Sloat Boulevard and west to today's Sunset Boulevard, Italian vegetable growers leased land from the late 1880s, about 350 acres of it from the Spring Valley Water Company. By 1900 their farms supplied much of the city's fresh produce, sold at the Colombo Market downtown.
