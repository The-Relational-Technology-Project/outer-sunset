This note follows the water at an Outer Sunset tap from start to finish: the Tuolumne River in Yosemite, the pipes and tunnels across the state, the Sunset Reservoir on the hill, the wells under the playgrounds, the tap, the drain and the ocean. It also covers what is in the water, what it costs, how to save it, what to store for an earthquake, and the history behind the system.

## Where does Outer Sunset tap water come from? The short answer

Almost all of it is mountain and Bay Area surface water, delivered by the San Francisco Public Utilities Commission (SFPUC). The SFPUC's 2025 water quality report says water from Hetch Hetchy Reservoir in Yosemite National Park makes up as much as 85 percent of what it delivers in a year. Most of the rest comes from reservoirs in Alameda County and San Mateo County, which is treated at the Sunol Valley and Harry Tracy plants.

A small part is local. In 2025 about 0.42 percent of the water served in San Francisco was groundwater pumped from the Westside Basin, the aquifer under the Sunset. Another 0.38 percent of the regional supply, mainly in April and May 2025, was treated water bought from Valley Water in Santa Clara County.

The mix changes through the year with maintenance, drought and demand, so no single percentage holds for every day.

Sources: [SFPUC, 2025 Annual Water Quality Report, City of San Francisco](https://www.sfpuc.gov/sites/default/files/accounts-and-services/water-quality/water-quality-en-SF-2025.pdf), [SFPUC, Annual and Triennial Water Quality Reports](https://www.sfpuc.gov/accounts-services/water-quality/annual-water-quality-reports).

## How Hetch Hetchy water gets from Yosemite to the Bay Area

The trip starts on the Tuolumne River, which rises in the peaks above Tuolumne Meadows. O'Shaughnessy Dam, 430 feet tall, holds the river back in Hetch Hetchy Reservoir, which can store 360,360 acre-feet.

From the dam the water runs through hydroelectric powerhouses, then the San Joaquin Pipelines across the Central Valley, then the Tesla treatment facility, where it is disinfected with ultraviolet light and chlorine. It passes under the hills in the Coast Range Tunnel, crosses the East Bay through the Irvington Tunnels, and goes under the bay in the Bay Tunnel to the Peninsula. Some is stored in Crystal Springs Reservoir.

The SFPUC's own history says the system was engineered to deliver the water entirely by gravity across more than 160 miles. The first Hetch Hetchy water reached the Pulgas Water Temple on the Peninsula at 10:12 a.m. on October 24, 1934. Hetch Hetchy water is clean enough that state and federal rules do not require it to be filtered.

Sources: [SFPUC, Surface Water: Water from Rain and Snowmelt](https://www.sfpuc.gov/about-us/our-systems/surface-water-water-rain-and-snowmelt), [National Park Service, Hetch Hetchy](https://home.nps.gov/yose/planyourvisit/hh.htm), [SFPUC, San Francisco Water and Power: A History (2005)](https://www.sfpuc.gov/sites/default/files/about-us/HH_historybook-2005.pdf), [SFPUC, 2025 Annual Water Quality Report](https://www.sfpuc.gov/sites/default/files/accounts-and-services/water-quality/water-quality-en-SF-2025.pdf).

## From the Peninsula to Sunset Reservoir and down the avenues

Water enters San Francisco from the Peninsula through large mains. The SFPUC's 2005 history names four: San Andreas No. 2, Crystal Springs No. 2, the Sunset Supply Line and Baden-Merced. It says the Harry Tracy treatment plant in San Mateo County feeds the San Andreas pipelines, which fill the Sunset and Merced Manor reservoirs.

Sunset Reservoir, under the lawn from 24th to 28th Avenue between Ortega and Quintara, is the hilltop tank for this side of town. Because it sits high, water runs downhill to homes without pumping. The Sunset Beacon reported in 2017 that water from the Sunset and Sutro reservoirs flows by gravity to the Richmond, the Sunset and about 60 percent of the city's area.

Citywide, the SFPUC runs more than 1,250 miles of distribution pipe, 12 reservoirs and eight tanks holding about 413 million gallons. The sources opened for this note did not include a street-by-street pressure zone map, so ask the SFPUC if you need to know exactly which reservoir feeds your block.

Sources: [SFPUC, San Francisco Water and Power: A History (2005)](https://www.sfpuc.gov/sites/default/files/about-us/HH_historybook-2005.pdf), [SFPUC, Surface Water: Water from Rain and Snowmelt](https://www.sfpuc.gov/about-us/our-systems/surface-water-water-rain-and-snowmelt), [Richmond Review / Sunset Beacon, Groundwater from aquifer starts flowing (May 2017)](https://richmondsunsetnews.com/2017/05/03/groundwater-from-aquifer-starts-flowing/).

## The drinking water wells at West Sunset, South Sunset, Lake Merced and Golden Gate Park

Since April 2017 the SFPUC has pumped drinking water from the ground under the west side. The San Francisco Groundwater Supply Project has six wells, drawing from 120 to 460 feet down in the Westside Basin.

The city's environmental review lists them. Four were new: at the Lake Merced Pump Station, West Sunset Playground, South Sunset Playground and the Central Pump Station in Golden Gate Park. Two more, called South Windmill Replacement and North Lake, are older Golden Gate Park irrigation wells to be converted to drinking water once the park switches to recycled water.

The well water is blended in small amounts with the regional supply in the Sunset and Sutro reservoirs before it reaches any tap. The long-term goal is an average of 4 million gallons a day. The reason is resilience. A local source keeps some water flowing if drought or an earthquake cuts the long pipeline from the Sierra.

Sources: [SFPUC, San Francisco Groundwater Supply Project overview (July 2023)](https://sfpuc.org/sites/default/files/programs/GroundwaterOverview_July2023.pdf), [SF Planning, Groundwater Supply Project Draft EIR, Appendix H4](https://sfmea.sfplanning.org/2008.1396E_DEIR_ApxH4.pdf), [SFPUC, Groundwater](https://www.sfpuc.gov/programs/water-supply/groundwater).

## How much groundwater is in a Sunset tap, and does it change the taste?

Very little right now. In 2025 groundwater was about 0.42 percent of San Francisco's water, and only one of the six wells, the Lake Merced Well, ran, and only now and then. The share has been higher. In late September 2018 the SFPUC said up to 8 percent of the water served to customers in the blend area was groundwater.

That October, Sunset neighbors complained that their tap water tasted different. The SFPUC tested water in the Sunset and nearby, said it had found no quality problem, and promised a community meeting.

The raw well water does carry more minerals. In 2025 it held 8.1 to 9.1 parts per billion of chromium-6, under the state limit of 10, and 3.8 to 4.8 parts per million of nitrate, also under a limit of 10. The state accepts blending in Sunset Reservoir as the treatment. After blending, water at the reservoir measured 0.1 to 0.2 parts per billion of chromium-6. The SFPUC emails its latest groundwater blend report on request.

Sources: [SFPUC, 2025 Annual Water Quality Report](https://www.sfpuc.gov/sites/default/files/accounts-and-services/water-quality/water-quality-en-SF-2025.pdf), [SFBay / Bay City News, Sunset tap water being tested after residents raise concerns (October 2018)](https://sfbayca.com/2018/10/09/sunset-tap-water-being-tested-after-residents-raise-concerns/), [SFPUC, Groundwater](https://www.sfpuc.gov/programs/water-supply/groundwater).

## Is the tap water safe? Fluoride, chloramine, hardness and PFAS

The SFPUC reports that in 2025 the water met every federal and state standard, based on more than 95,350 tests.

Fluoride is added because California law requires it. Treated water averaged 0.7 parts per million, the level the CDC recommends. No PFAS, the "forever chemicals", were detected in testing from 2019 through 2024.

The water is soft. Hardness averaged 62 parts per million in 2025 and ranged from 8 to 112, higher when Bay Area reservoirs supply more of the mix. An earthy or musty taste in warm months usually means algae in a local reservoir, which the SFPUC says is unpleasant but not a health risk.

The disinfectant that stays in the pipes is chloramine, not plain chlorine. It does not evaporate if you let water sit out. The CDC says fish, turtles and frogs must not be kept in chloraminated water unless it is treated first, and pet stores sell products for that. Dialysis centers remove it, and home dialysis users should check with the machine's maker.

Sources: [SFPUC, 2025 Annual Water Quality Report](https://www.sfpuc.gov/sites/default/files/accounts-and-services/water-quality/water-quality-en-SF-2025.pdf), [SFPUC, Taste and Odor in Drinking Water](https://www.sfpuc.gov/accounts-services/water-quality/understanding-your-tap-water/taste-and-odor-drinking-water), [CDC, About Water Disinfection with Chlorine and Chloramine](https://www.cdc.gov/drinking-water/about/about-water-disinfection-with-chlorine-and-chloramine.html), [SFPUC, Understanding Your Tap Water](https://www.sfpuc.gov/tapwater).

## Lead in the pipes: what the city has replaced and how to get a test

The source water has no lead. Lead gets in from old plumbing, mostly inside buildings. The SFPUC says it removed all known lead service lines in the 1980s and has never found a lead line on the customer side.

What remained were galvanized steel lines, some joined to the main by a short lead connector. The SFPUC confirmed 1,495 of these on its side and had replaced 1,433 with copper by January 2026. On the customer side it counts 699 galvanized lines and 109 of unknown material. An online map shows whether your address has one.

In the last round of sampling, at 50 homes in 2024, the 90th percentile result was 4.9 parts per billion. The action level is 15.

A lead test costs $25 per tap. It is free for families in the WIC program and for customers with a galvanized line. To cut exposure, run the cold tap for a few minutes after water has sat in the pipes, and use only cold water for drinking, cooking and baby formula.

Sources: [SFPUC, 2025 Annual Water Quality Report](https://www.sfpuc.gov/sites/default/files/accounts-and-services/water-quality/water-quality-en-SF-2025.pdf), [SFPUC, Lead in Drinking Water and how to order a test](https://www.sfpuc.gov/leadtest).

## Recycled water for Golden Gate Park and what waters Sunset Boulevard

Golden Gate Park has long been watered from wells. A 1993 USGS study said the main use of groundwater in the city was irrigating the park, Stern Grove, the zoo and golf courses.

That is changing. The Westside Enhanced Water Recycling Project takes treated wastewater at the Oceanside plant by the zoo and cleans it further with membrane filters, reverse osmosis and ultraviolet light. Nearly 8 miles of pipe carry it north to Golden Gate Park, the Panhandle and later Lincoln Park Golf Course. The $213 million project is meant to supply 1.6 million gallons a day. Its project page gives the end of 2026 for completion. Another SFPUC page says park irrigation starts in 2027. Harding Park and Fleming golf courses have used recycled water from Daly City since 2012.

Sunset Boulevard's 60-acre greenbelt still drinks tap water. On September 30, 2026 city and state officials marked $1 million in state money toward connecting it to the recycled water line, which they said could save about 40 million gallons a year. No construction dates were given.

Sources: [SFPUC, Westside Enhanced Water Recycling Project](https://www.sfpuc.gov/construction-contracts/construction-projects/westside-enhanced-water-recycling-project), [SFPUC, Westside Enhanced Water Recycling Project FAQ (August 2024)](https://www.sfpuc.gov/sites/default/files/documents/Westside-Enhanced-Water-Recycling-Project-FAQ-Aug-2024.pdf), [SFPUC, Recycled Water](https://sfpuc.gov/programs/water-supply/recycled-water), [USGS, Geohydrology of San Francisco (1993)](https://pubs.usgs.gov/wri/1993/4019/report.pdf), [Local News Matters / Bay City News, Sunset Boulevard recycled water project (October 2026)](https://localnewsmatters.org/2026/10/01/sunset-boulevard-recycled-water-irrigation-project-advances-with-1m-in-state-funding/).

## What a household pays for water and sewer, and how much people use

Rates went up on July 1, 2026. A single-family home with the usual 5/8-inch meter pays a fixed water charge of $19.65 a month. Water itself costs $12.20 per unit for the first four units in a month and $13.55 for each unit after that. One unit is 748 gallons.

The sewer side of the bill is larger. There is a $6.62 monthly charge plus $20.74 per unit, counted on 90 percent of the water a house uses. A separate stormwater charge is based on lot size. The SFPUC estimated that the average single-family bill would rise by about $21 a month this fiscal year, and rates are set to rise again on July 1, 2027.

San Franciscans use little water. The SFPUC's annual report for fiscal year 2024-25 puts home use at 42 gallons per person per day. You can see your own hourly use by signing up for My Account, and the SFPUC has a bill calculator and bill relief programs.

Sources: [SFPUC, Rate Schedules and Fees for Water and Sewer Service, effective July 1, 2026](https://www.sfpuc.gov/sites/default/files/accounts-and-services/Rates_Schedule_Water_Sewer_2026-7.pdf), [SFPUC, Proposition 218 notice for water and sewer rates](https://www.sfpuc.gov/accounts-services/water-power-sewer-rates/proposed-water-sewer-rates-2026/proposition-218-notice), [SFPUC, Water Resources Annual Report FY 2024-25](https://www.sfpuc.gov/sites/default/files/Water/WR-AR-2025.pdf).

## Free water-saving devices, leak alerts, rain barrels and laundry-to-landscape

The SFPUC gives away faucet aerators, 1.5 gallon per minute showerheads, shower timers and toilet flappers. People in single-family homes or buildings under 10 units can pick them up at 525 Golden Gate Avenue, Monday through Friday, 10 a.m. to 2 p.m., with proof of address. Renters qualify. You can also book a free phone consultation online or by calling 415-551-4730.

Leak alerts are automatic. If a home's meter shows 7.5 gallons an hour or more running nonstop for 48 hours, the SFPUC sends an email, text, call or letter. A running toilet is the usual cause.

For the yard, there is a rebate of up to $100 per rain barrel, for up to two barrels, or up to $350 for one cistern of 205 to 5,000 gallons. The laundry-to-landscape rebate pays up to $100 toward the valve and pipe that send washing machine water to plants. It needs a flat or downhill yard and suits fruit trees and berries better than drought-tolerant plants.

Sources: [SFPUC, Water-Saving Devices](https://www.sfpuc.gov/learning/conserve-water/free-evaluations-and-devices/water-saving-devices), [SFPUC, Leak Alerts](https://www.sfpuc.gov/conserve-water/leak-alerts-find-and-fix-leaks), [SFPUC, Rain Barrel and Cistern Rebate](https://www.sfpuc.gov/learning/how-you-can-help/rain-barrel-and-cistern-rebate), [SFPUC, Graywater Laundry-to-Landscape Rebate](https://www.sfpuc.gov/learning/conserve-water/graywater-laundry-landscape-rebate).

## Where the water goes after the drain

Everything that goes down a Sunset drain, and the rain that runs into the street grates, shares one set of pipes. San Francisco has a combined sewer. Citywide it carries more than 80 million gallons on a dry day and up to 500 million gallons a day in rain, to three treatment plants.

West side flows run downhill toward the ocean, into the big storage box under the Great Highway, and south to the Oceanside plant beside the zoo. There the water is treated and sent out a long pipe to the Pacific. The note "Dunes and sand" in this archive gives the sizes, dates and distances for the box, the Lake Merced Tunnel, the plant and the outfall.

One new step is being added. The recycled water facility at the Oceanside plant takes part of the treated flow before it goes to sea and cleans it again for watering Golden Gate Park. So some Sunset water is on its way to a second use a few blocks from where it started.

Sources: [SFPUC, Where Does Wastewater Go?](https://www.sfpuc.gov/about-us/our-systems/sewer-system/where-does-wastewater-go), [SFPUC, Westside Enhanced Water Recycling Project FAQ (August 2024)](https://www.sfpuc.gov/sites/default/files/documents/Westside-Enhanced-Water-Recycling-Project-FAQ-Aug-2024.pdf).

## How much water to store for an earthquake

The SFPUC's advice is one gallon per person per day, for three to five days, and up to a gallon a day for each pet. A family of four with a dog should keep 15 to 25 gallons. After a big earthquake, it says, tap water may be out for 72 hours or more.

Tap water can be stored as it is. Use clean food-grade containers, keep them cool and dark, write the date on them, and swap the water every six months.

If you run out, the water heater and the toilet tank (not the bowl) hold water you can treat. Boil it for three to five minutes, or add 8 drops of plain unscented bleach per gallon and wait 30 minutes.

The SFPUC's own goals after a major earthquake are to resume regional deliveries to most connection points within 24 hours and to pressurize the mains serving the major trauma hospitals within 24 hours. Homes come later, which is why the stored water matters.

Sources: [SFPUC, Storing and Treating Water](https://www.sfpuc.gov/learning/emergency-preparedness/storing-and-treating-water), [SFPUC, Water Enterprise Level of Service Goals](https://www.sfpuc.gov/about-us/our-systems/surface-water-water-rain-and-snowmelt/water-enterprise-level-service-goals).

## Fire hydrants, cisterns and the brick circles in the street

The white hydrants on every Sunset block run off the drinking water system. The city's separate high-pressure firefighting system, built after 1906, mostly serves the east and center of town. The Sunset Beacon reports that it reaches west only to 19th Avenue here, where its hydrants have black tops.

West of that, the SFPUC says, the outer neighborhoods rely on the regular hydrants and on cisterns. A cistern is a buried tank, typically 75,000 gallons, that fire engines can pump from if the mains break. There are roughly 200 in the city, usually marked by a ring or square of bricks in an intersection. Thirty were added with 2010 bond money, 15 of them in west side neighborhoods. The sources opened for this note did not list which Sunset corners have one.

Along the park, hydrants on Fulton Street from 22nd to 48th Avenue are fed by Blue Heron Lake, formerly Stow Lake, which holds 15.3 million gallons for firefighting.

Sources: [SFPUC, Emergency Firefighting Water System fact sheet (February 2026)](https://www.sfpuc.gov/sites/default/files/documents/Firefighting-Water-System-Fact-Sheet-02.24.2026.pdf), [SFPUC, Westside Emergency Firefighting Water System](https://www.sfpuc.gov/construction-contracts/construction-projects/westside-fire), [Richmond Review / Sunset Beacon, Emergency Firefighting Bond Measure Goes to Voters (February 2026)](https://richmondsunsetnews.com/2026/02/03/emergency-firefighting-bond-measure-goes-to-voters/), [99% Invisible, Decoding Rings](https://99percentinvisible.org/article/decoding-rings-beneath-mysterious-brick-circles-san-francisco-streets/).

## The plan to bring high-pressure firefighting water to the west side

The SFPUC proposes a Westside Emergency Firefighting Water System: about 16 miles of earthquake-resistant pipe, 2 to 3.5 feet wide, from Lake Merced north to Lincoln Park and the Presidio and west to the Great Highway. It would carry drinking water on normal days and switch to high pressure for fires. It could also draw from Lake Merced. New pump stations are planned, including one at Sunset Reservoir. The project page lists the first phases as starting in 2027.

Money has been the problem. The Sunset Beacon reported that a 2020 bond fell short, and that by 2023 the Outer Sunset pipeline north of Lawton Street was shown as unfunded. In June 2026 voters passed Proposition A, a $535 million bond, with about 77 percent in favor. About $130 million of it is for the firefighting system.

Not everyone agrees with the design. The Sunset-Parkside Education and Action Committee (SPEAK) and others argued for a separate system that could pump unlimited seawater, not one that depends on drinking water reservoirs.

Sources: [SFPUC, Westside Emergency Firefighting Water System fact sheet (February 2026)](https://www.sfpuc.gov/sites/default/files/documents/About-Westside-Emergency-Fire-Fighting_2.24.2026_0.pdf), [SFPUC, Westside Emergency Firefighting Water System](https://www.sfpuc.gov/construction-contracts/construction-projects/westside-fire), [Richmond Review / Sunset Beacon, Emergency Firefighting Bond Measure Goes to Voters (February 2026)](https://richmondsunsetnews.com/2026/02/03/emergency-firefighting-bond-measure-goes-to-voters/), [Local News Matters / Bay City News, SF Prop A earthquake safety bond leading (June 2026)](https://localnewsmatters.org/2026/06/03/sf-prop-a-earthquake-safety-bond-leading/), [El Reportero, San Francisco approves infrastructure bond (June 2026)](https://elreporterosf.com/san-francisco-approves-infrastructure-bond-term-limits-rejects-tax-measures/).

## Before the pipes: ponds, wells and the Spring Valley Water Company

Before any main reached the dunes, water here meant ponds between the sand hills and shallow wells. The note on streetcars, tunnels and the beach in this archive covers those lost lakes and the well houses of Carville and Oceanside.

The city's early supply was private. Gold Rush San Francisco drew on Mountain Lake and Lobos Creek in the Presidio. In 1858 George Ensign got a state charter and formed the Spring Valley Water Works, named for a spring near Mason and Washington streets. The company went south for more. It finished the main Pilarcitos Dam in 1867, started San Andreas Dam in 1868, and acquired rights to Lake Merced in August 1868.

For decades the company and the city fought over rates and service. When Spring Valley's mains broke in the 1906 earthquake, the push for a public supply from the Sierra grew stronger. San Francisco bought the whole Spring Valley system in 1930 for $39,962,606.51. Its Peninsula reservoirs are still part of what comes out of the tap.

Sources: [SFPUC, San Francisco Water and Power: A History (2005)](https://www.sfpuc.gov/sites/default/files/about-us/HH_historybook-2005.pdf), [San Francisco Fire Department, Water Supply Systems](https://sf-fire.org/node/283).

## The Hetch Hetchy fight, and whose valley it was

Hetch Hetchy was a valley before it was a reservoir. The National Park Service says people have lived there for more than 6,000 years and that the name comes from a Miwok word, hatchhatchie, for edible grasses. Restore Hetch Hetchy, the group that wants the reservoir drained, describes it as a homeland of Paiute and Sierra Miwok people, called Iyaydzi in Paiute, and says most Native people were forced out of the Yosemite area around the Mariposa War of 1850 and 1851.

San Francisco began eyeing the valley in 1882. John Muir led the opposition, arguing a reservoir could go outside the national park. President Woodrow Wilson signed the Raker Act, which allowed the dam, on December 19, 1913. O'Shaughnessy Dam was dedicated on July 7, 1923.

The argument is not over. Restore Hetch Hetchy says the city's water use has fallen enough that the valley could be restored and the water stored elsewhere on the Tuolumne. The SFPUC treats the reservoir as the heart of its supply.

Sources: [National Park Service, Hetch Hetchy](https://home.nps.gov/yose/planyourvisit/hh.htm), [Restore Hetch Hetchy, History of the valley](https://hetchhetchy.org/history-of-the-valley/), [Restore Hetch Hetchy](https://hetchhetchy.org/), [SFPUC, San Francisco Water and Power: A History (2005)](https://www.sfpuc.gov/sites/default/files/about-us/HH_historybook-2005.pdf).

## Does the fog count as water?

A little, and mostly in an indirect way. The best local measurement found for this note comes from a watershed on the San Francisco Peninsula, studied by UC Berkeley engineers with SFPUC staff and published in 2017. Fog that caught on leaves and dripped to the ground added about 10 to 30 millimeters of water a year, roughly half an inch to a bit over an inch. That is small next to rain.

The bigger effect was shade and damp air. Fog cut the water that plants and soil lost to evaporation by about 125 millimeters a year, around 5 inches. Together the two effects shrank the dry-season water shortfall in that watershed by 25 percent.

No source opened for this note measured fog drip in the Sunset itself, so treat these as numbers from a nearby, more forested place. The plain version: fog does not fill the reservoir, but it lets a summer garden here get by on less watering than the same garden inland.

Sources: [Chung, Dufour, Pluche and Thompson, How much does dry-season fog matter? Hydrological Processes (2017)](https://www.ce.berkeley.edu/sites/default/files/assets/users/thompson/Chung_et_al-2017-Hydrological_Processes.pdf).
